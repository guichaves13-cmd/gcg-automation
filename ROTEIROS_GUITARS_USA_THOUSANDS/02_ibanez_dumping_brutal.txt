[REASON #7 — THE NECK GOT TOO THIN FOR GROWN-UP HANDS]

Number seven. The neck got too thin for grown-up hands.

The Wizard neck is legendary. Thin, flat, fast. When you're sixteen and you want to play every note of every solo, it feels like a cheat code.

But here's what happens. That sixteen-year-old is now thirty-eight. He's got a desk job. He's been typing all day. And when he wraps his hand around a super-thin neck for two hours, his thumb and wrist start talking to him.

I hear this all the time now. "My hand cramps." "My thumb hurts at the base." "I can't do long sessions anymore."

A slightly chunkier neck, a C or a soft V, actually fills the hand and lets the muscles relax. Lots of players who grew up on thin necks are discovering this in their late thirties and forties, and it feels like a revelation.

So they sell the shred guitar and buy something with a fatter neck. That's the first reason, and it's purely physical.

[REASON #6 — THE FLOATING TREM TAX]

Number six. The floating trem tax.

I love a good double-locking tremolo. When it's set up right, it's a marvel of engineering. You can dive-bomb, flutter, and come back in tune.

But let me tell you what it costs in real life.

Want to change string gauge? That's a re-balance. Want to drop to D for one song? You can't, not without a workaround, because the whole bridge shifts. Break a string on stage? The other five go out of tune. Changing strings takes three times as long as on a fixed bridge. And when a beginner tries to do it themselves, I usually end up seeing that guitar on my bench with a bridge pulled forward and the springs a mess.

I call it the floating trem tax. Every time you want to do something simple, you pay it.

And in 2026, a lot of rock players have realized they don't actually dive-bomb anymore. They play riffs. They play rhythm. They play in alternate tunings. So they're dumping the floating trem guitar and grabbing a hardtail. One player told me, "I spent more time tuning that guitar than playing it." That's not a guitar problem. That's a mismatch.

[REASON #5 — THE SOUND OF ROCK MOVED BACKWARD]

Number five. The sound of rock moved backward.

Look at the guitar sound that's popular right now in rock. It's not the scooped, high-gain, super-saturated shred tone of the eighties and early two-thousands. It's chunkier. Warmer. More mid-range. More "band in a room." Think of all the retro-leaning rock bands filling theaters right now. The guitars they play are mostly single-cut and offset shapes with lower output pickups.

High-output humbuckers, which are great for metal, can feel too hot for that kind of music. They compress, they lose dynamics, and clean-up when you roll the volume back isn't always as sweet.

So players who want that vintage rock thing are trading a guitar built for speed for a guitar built for vibe. Same players, different sound goals.

And here's a fun detail. Ibanez actually makes excellent guitars for exactly that sound. Their AZ series, their semi-hollow models, their more vintage-styled lines. But the reputation is still "shred guitar," so a lot of players never even look. That's a branding problem more than a building problem.

[REASON #4 — THE MODELER EFFECT]

Number four. The modeler effect.

This year, compact amp modelers got shockingly cheap and shockingly good. There are little floor units now for a couple hundred dollars that would have blown everybody's mind ten years ago. Boss, Line 6, Neural, Nux, Mooer, Zoom, everybody is fighting for the bedroom player.

Why does that matter for guitars? Because when your amp can be anything, your guitar becomes the only thing that defines your personal sound. Players start thinking about their instrument differently. They stop needing a guitar that "does metal" and start wanting a guitar that feels and sounds unique when everything else is digital.

And a lot of them come to the conclusion that the most versatile thing they can own is something classic and simple. A two-pickup guitar with a fixed bridge and a comfortable neck. The modeler does the heavy lifting. The guitar just needs to be honest.

So the shred guitar, with its specialized pickups and its specialized bridge, starts to feel too specialized. And it ends up on the trade-in wall.

[REASON #3 — THE SEVEN-STRING HANGOVER]

Number three. The seven-string hangover.

For about fifteen years, extended range guitars ruled modern heavy music. Seven strings, eight strings, multi-scale, headless. Ibanez was one of the brands that made that possible for regular players, and a lot of people bought into it hard.

Now, I'm not saying seven-strings are dead. They're not. But I am seeing a hangover. Players who bought an eight-string because a band they loved used one, and then realized they mostly play on the top six strings. Players whose wrists hurt from the wide necks. Players who just want to jam with friends in standard tuning and feel silly bringing a plank the size of a surfboard.

So when they clean house, the extended-range guitars go first. And because a lot of those happened to be Ibanez, the brand shows up on the wall more often than anybody else's.

This is a big one that people miss. It's not really a brand trend. It's a category trend wearing a brand's logo.

[REASON #2 — THE RESALE MATH]

Number two. The resale math.

Here's where it gets cold.

Mid-level imported guitars with lots of features, locking trems, specialized pickups, flashy finishes, tend to lose value faster than simple guitars with classic designs. That's true across the industry. The more specific a guitar is, the smaller the group of buyers who want it.

And a lot of players are doing the math right now. Prices on everything went up. Rent went up. Groceries went up. And that guitar that's been hanging on the wall for three years suddenly looks like money. Not a lot of money, but money.

So they sell it before it drops further. And the more people do that, the more the price drops. You've seen this before. It's the same cycle we talked about in the Taylor video.

If you own one and you love it, this doesn't matter. Keep it. But if it's just hanging there, the market is quietly telling you something.

Before number one, comment one word for me: Speed or Vibe. Which one matters more to your playing right now?

[REASON #1 — THE BRUTAL REASON]

Number one. The brutal reason.

I asked one guy, maybe forty years old, why he was selling a really nice Japanese RG. Beautiful guitar. Perfect frets. I would have been proud to set it up.

He looked at it for a while and said, "Because I'm never going to play like the guys I bought it for."

That's it. That's the brutal reason.

A lot of these guitars were bought as a dream. The dream of being the fastest player in the room. Of nailing that solo. Of being one of the heroes on the poster. And for most of us, that dream quietly faded. Not because we failed. Because life happened. Because we found other things we love. Because we realized we actually like playing simple riffs with friends more than practicing sweep arpeggios with a metronome.

And when you finally admit that to yourself, the guitar built for that dream starts to feel like a judgment. Every time you look at it, it reminds you of the player you didn't become.

So you sell it. And honestly? A lot of these guys tell me they feel lighter afterward.

[THE TWIST — WHAT I TELL THEM]

But here's what I tell every one of them.

You didn't fail the guitar. And the guitar didn't fail you. You just changed. The kid who wanted to shred grew up into a musician who wants to groove. That's not a loss. That's a new chapter.

And sometimes, I talk them into one more thing before they sell. I say, "Let me do a real setup. Let me block the trem so it acts like a hardtail. Let me put on a slightly heavier set of strings. Let me lower the pickups a bit so they breathe."

You would not believe how often a guitar that felt like a dream they'd given up on suddenly feels like a brand-new rock guitar. Same wood, same neck, totally different personality.

I had one guy cancel his listing on the spot. He played it for twenty minutes in my shop, through a little modeler set to a vintage crunch, and said, "Why didn't anybody tell me it could do this?"

So before you dump yours, give it one honest setup that matches who you are now, not who you were at sixteen.

[WHAT THEY'RE BUYING INSTEAD]

For the ones who do sell, here's where they're going.

Most go simpler. Fixed bridge, two humbuckers or a couple of single-coils, a medium neck. Classic shapes. A lot of single-cut and offset designs right now.

Some go to the semi-hollow world. Thinlines and three-hundred-series style guitars. Warm, woody, great at low volume, perfect for playing at home in an apartment.

And a surprising number of them are not leaving the brand at all. They sell the RG and buy one of Ibanez's newer, more classic-voiced models. That tells you the "dumping Ibanez" story is really a "dumping the old version of myself" story. The logo is just where it shows up.

[BENCH TECH TIPS — BEFORE YOU SELL A SHRED GUITAR]

Practical advice, because that's my job.

One. If it has a floating trem, make sure it's balanced and parallel to the body before you list it. A bridge tilted forward screams "problem" to a buyer, even when it's just a spring adjustment.

Two. Clean and condition the fretboard, and polish the frets. Jumbo frets show every bit of grime, and shiny frets photograph like a much more expensive guitar.

Three. Include the trem arm, the tools, and the string locks. Missing parts on a locking bridge knock real money off the price. Buyers know how annoying they are to replace.

Four. If you modified the pickups, keep the originals and include them. Collectors care about that.

And if you're buying one of these guitars that are flooding the market right now, you might be getting one of the best values in the used world. A good Japanese-made instrument with excellent fretwork, at a price that's dropped because of a trend. Trends end. Good necks don't.
