Thousands of electric players are quietly walking away from Fender in 2026. Not with angry posts. Not with smashed Strats on social media. They're just not buying the next one. They walk into a store, they pick up an American Professional Two, they look at the tag, they put it back on the hook, and they walk out with something else. Or they go home and they buy a used one from 2009 for half the price. Or they buy nothing at all.

I'm a bench tech. I set up guitars for a living. And over the last year, the mix of guitars coming across my bench has changed. I still see plenty of Fenders. Fender is not going anywhere, and I want to be clear about that from the start. But the new ones I see are fewer, the used ones are older, and the customers who own them are asking me a question I didn't hear much five years ago: is the new one actually worth it?

So today we're going to do this the honest way. No sponsors. Nobody sent me a free guitar. I'm going to count down six real reasons players are drifting away from Fender this year. Then I'm going to give Fender the fair side, because there is a fair side, and it's a strong one. And at the end I'll give you a straight verdict: keep it, sell it, or buy one.

Let's start with number six.

Reason number six: the tariff squeeze is real, and Fender is right in the middle of it.

Fender is an American company with a famous factory in Corona, California. But a huge part of what Fender sells is not built in Corona. The Player series and Player Two series come out of the factory in Ensenada, Mexico. A lot of Squier production comes out of Asia, including China and Indonesia. And even the American guitars depend on parts, hardware, electronics and tonewood that cross borders before they ever become a guitar.

When the United States put new tariffs on imports from Mexico and China in 2025, the guitar world felt it almost immediately. Moody's, the credit rating agency, downgraded Fender's credit rating and estimated that the tariffs on Mexico and China production could add somewhere around twenty to twenty-five million dollars in operating costs. That's not a forum rumor. That's a credit agency putting a number on it. Moody's also warned that short-term fixes like price increases and vendor concessions might not be enough if the tariffs stay in place.

Now, to be fair, Fender has argued that its Mexico-made products meet the rules of origin under the United States, Mexico, Canada Agreement, the USMCA, and that the Mexico tariffs don't currently apply to them. We'll come back to that in the fair side. But here's the thing a player feels. Even when a specific guitar is technically exempt, the whole system around it gets more expensive. Shipping costs, parts costs, dealer margins, all of it. And the uncertainty itself changes how people shop. When you don't know if the price is going up next quarter, a lot of players decide to wait. And waiting, for a company that depends on steady sales, looks a lot like walking away.

Reason number five: the price tags crossed a psychological line.

According to an S&P Global report covered by Guitar World, Fender raised prices across its entire lineup by around five percent in July of 2025. Five percent doesn't sound like much. But five percent on top of the increases that came during the pandemic years adds up.

Let me put it in bench-tech terms. When the American Professional series launched, a lot of players thought of a US Strat as roughly a fifteen-hundred-dollar guitar. Today, depending on the model, the finish, and the store, an American Professional Two Stratocaster sits well above that, and the American Ultra models push past two thousand. A Player Two from Mexico, which a lot of us remember as a five or six hundred dollar guitar not long ago, now sits up in the eight-hundred-dollar neighborhood. Check the current prices in your area, because they move, but the direction has been clear.

And there's a line in every player's head. For a lot of working guitarists, that line is two thousand dollars for a production electric guitar. Once a mass-produced Strat gets near that line, the comparison changes. You're no longer comparing a Fender to a cheaper Fender. You're comparing it to a boutique builder, to a used Custom Shop, to a Suhr or a Tom Anderson on the used market. And when a player starts making that comparison, a lot of them discover that the Fender name alone is not enough to win it.

Reason number four: the used market is eating the new market alive.

This is the one I see every single week. Fender has made millions of guitars. Literally millions. The Stratocaster has been in production since 1954. The Telecaster, in its Broadcaster and Esquire forms, goes back to 1950 and 1951. That means there is an enormous pool of used Fenders out there, and the good ones from the last twenty years are very, very good.

An American Standard from the late two-thousands, an American Deluxe, an early American Professional. These guitars come across my bench all the time, and once I level the frets if they need it, cut a fresh nut if they need it, and set them up properly, they play every bit as well as a new one off the wall. Sometimes better, because the neck has settled and moved through a few seasons already. And they usually cost a lot less than a new American Professional Two.

Here's the honest truth that Fender's marketing will never say out loud: a Strat is a Strat. The design is from the nineteen-fifties. A bolt-on neck, a contoured ash or alder body, three single coils, a five-way switch, a tremolo. The improvements over the years are real but they're incremental. A rolled fingerboard edge, a better neck profile, a modern truss rod adjustment wheel, a set of updated pickups. Those are nice. But they're not worth hundreds of dollars more to a player who can get ninety-five percent of the experience used.

So the player who used to buy new every few years now buys used. And every used sale is a new sale that Fender didn't make. The guitar stays a Fender. The player stays a Fender player. But the money goes to another guitarist on Reverb, not to Corona.

Reason number three: Squier got so good that it's competing with its own parent.

This is the strangest one, and honestly my favorite one, because it's a compliment and a problem at the same time.

The Squier Classic Vibe series, which Fender sells under its own Squier brand, has gotten genuinely good. On the bench I see Classic Vibe Strats and Teles with decent frets, solid necks, and pickups that sound like the era they're imitating. With a proper setup, many of them are gig-ready guitars. They're not perfect. Sometimes I have to dress a few fret ends, sometimes the nut slots are cut a little high, sometimes the electronics feel cheap. But for the money, they're remarkable.

And here's the problem for Fender. A player who's on a budget used to save up and buy a Mexican Fender, then save up again and buy an American one. That ladder was the whole business model. Squier to Mexico to America to Custom Shop. Now a lot of players stop at the first rung. They buy a Classic Vibe, they put a set of upgraded pickups in it, maybe new tuners, they bring it to someone like me for a fret level and a setup, and they're done. That guitar does the job on stage, at church, at the bar gig, and in the bedroom. They never climb the ladder.

That's not Fender losing the player. Technically the money still goes to the same company. But it's a much smaller amount of money, and it means the expensive guitars at the top of the ladder lose some of the people who used to be pushed toward them.

Reason number two: the competition stopped copying Fender and started beating it on specs.

For decades, everybody else made Fender-style guitars, and the message was always, it's almost as good as a Fender. That's not the message anymore.

Look at what's on the wall today. Brands like Schecter, Ibanez, Charvel, which is actually owned by Fender, and a long list of direct-to-consumer companies are selling S-style and T-style guitars with stainless steel frets, roasted maple necks, locking tuners, compound radius fingerboards, and modern pickups, sometimes for less money than a Fender with older specs. Stainless steel frets especially matter to me as a tech, because they last much longer and they make my job easier for the customer over the life of the guitar. Fender does offer stainless frets on some models, but it is not standard across the line the way it is on many competitors.

Then you have the boutique and semi-boutique builders. Suhr, Tom Anderson, and others who build S-style and T-style guitars at a higher level of consistency. On the used market, some of those guitars sell for prices close to a new American Fender. A player who is going to spend two thousand dollars anyway starts asking why not get the boutique one.

And at the very bottom, there are budget brands that sell full guitars for a couple hundred dollars that, after a setup, play much better than anything at that price used to. They're not Fenders, and they don't hold value the way a Fender does, but for a beginner or for a backup, they're enough.

So Fender is now squeezed from above by boutique builders, from the side by spec-heavy competitors, and from below by budget brands and its own Squier line. That's a lot of pressure on one guitar shape.

And reason number one: players realized the logo on the headstock isn't the tone.

This is the big one, and it's a cultural shift more than a price shift.

For a very long time, the Fender logo meant something specific. It meant you were playing the real thing. The guitar that Hendrix played, that Stevie Ray Vaughan played, that thousands of country pickers played on Telecasters. That meaning is still there, and it's still powerful. But a younger generation of players learned guitar on YouTube, on modelers, on cheap guitars that were set up well, and they learned something older players sometimes forget: most of your sound is in your hands, your amp or modeler, your pickups, and your setup.

When I set up a guitar, I see this every day. A cheap guitar with a proper fret job and a great setup will often feel better to a player than an expensive guitar with high action and buzzing frets. And once a player experiences that, the headstock logo matters less. They start shopping for feel, for specs, for value. Not for a name.

That's why the drift away from Fender is quiet. These players don't hate Fender. Many of them grew up dreaming of one. They just decided that in 2026, with these prices and these alternatives, the name alone doesn't justify the premium. So they buy used. They buy Squier. They buy a competitor. Or they keep the guitar they already have and spend the money on a good setup.

Now let's be fair to Fender. Because if I stopped here, you'd get the wrong idea.

First, the tariff story is more complicated than people think. Fender itself says its Mexico production meets the USMCA rules of origin, so the Mexico tariffs don't apply to those guitars right now. And the Corona factory in California is a real American factory, employing real American workers, building guitars in the United States. Whatever you think about trade policy, Fender has more domestic manufacturing than a lot of its competitors, and that counts for something.

Second, quality control at the American level has been good in my experience. The American Professional Two guitars I've had on the bench have generally come in with decent fretwork, stable necks, and good hardware. They're not perfect, no factory guitar is, but they're consistent. And consistency is underrated. When you buy a new American Fender from a reputable dealer, you're very unlikely to get a dud.

Third, the resale value. This is the strongest point in Fender's favor. A Fender holds value better than almost any other production electric guitar brand. If you buy a used American Fender at a fair price, you can often sell it later for close to what you paid. That makes the real cost of ownership much lower than the price tag suggests. A cheaper brand might cost less up front, but you'll often lose a much bigger chunk of the price when you sell.

Fourth, the parts ecosystem. This is a tech's favorite reason. Fender-style guitars are the easiest guitars in the world to repair, modify, and maintain. Necks are bolt-on. Pickups are standard sizes. Pickguards, bridges, tuners, pots and switches are available everywhere, from dozens of makers. If something breaks on a Fender, I can fix it quickly and cheaply. That's not true of every brand, and over the life of a guitar it saves real money.

And fifth, the innovation hasn't stopped. Fender keeps updating the line. The Player Two series brought rosewood fingerboards back to the Mexican line and added options players asked for. The American Professional Two added a deep C neck profile and the Tim Shaw designed V-Mod Two pickups. The S&P Global report itself expects improving volume trends from new products, even if overall revenue growth stays modest. So Fender is not sitting still. It's adjusting to a hard market like everybody else.

So here's the verdict.

If you already own an American Fender, keep it. Seriously. Don't sell a good guitar because of a headline. Its value is going to hold up, it's easy to maintain, and if it plays well, you already have the thing everyone else is shopping for. Put the money into a fret dress and a professional setup instead, and it'll feel like a new guitar.

If you own a Fender you never play, and it's sitting in a case under your bed, this might be a very good time to sell it. Used prices for American Fenders are strong, partly because new prices are so high. Somebody out there is looking for exactly that guitar at a used price. Let it go to someone who'll play it.

If you're thinking about buying, here's my honest advice: buy used, or buy Squier Classic Vibe and spend the difference on a professional setup. A used American Fender from the last fifteen to twenty years, checked by a tech, is one of the best values in the guitar world right now. And if your budget is tight, a Classic Vibe with a proper setup will take you further than most people think.

Only buy a brand-new American Fender at full price if there's a specific model, finish, or feature you can't find used. And if you do, buy it from a dealer with a good return policy, play it before you commit, and check the frets and the neck before you walk out.

Fender isn't dying. It's going through what every big brand goes through when prices climb faster than paychecks. Players aren't walking away from the guitar. They're walking away from paying full price for the logo.

Now let me go a little deeper on a few of these, because I want you to be able to check what I'm saying and make your own decision.

Let's talk about how to judge a used Fender, since I'm telling you to buy one. The first thing I check is the neck. Sight down it from the headstock to the body. You want a slight relief, a tiny dip in the middle, not a hump and not a big bow. Then turn the truss rod. On a lot of modern Fenders, the adjustment wheel is at the heel of the neck, by the end of the fingerboard, and it's easy to reach. On older vintage-style necks, you might have to loosen the strings and pull the neck or the pickguard to reach the adjustment nut. Either way, the rod should move smoothly. If it's maxed out, or it doesn't turn at all, walk away or ask for a big discount.

Second, the frets. Look at the first five frets especially, because that's where most players spend most of their time. If you see deep grooves under the strings, the guitar needs a fret dress or a partial refret. A dress is affordable. A full refret is a significant job. Factor that into the price.

Third, the electronics. Plug it in and move through every switch position. Turn every knob slowly. Scratchy pots are cheap to fix. Dead pickups are less cheap, but still not a disaster on a Fender because replacements are everywhere. Still, it gives you something to negotiate with.

Fourth, the serial number and the paperwork. Fender serial numbers can help date the guitar. Fender has published guides to its serial number formats. If a seller claims the guitar is American, the serial number and the neck plate and the neck date should all make sense together. If they don't, be careful. There are plenty of fakes and parts guitars out there, and a lot of honest mixed-parts guitars that are sold as original.

Fifth, the price. Look at sold listings, not asking prices. Asking prices on Reverb and eBay can be wishful thinking. Sold prices tell you what people are actually paying. That's your benchmark.

Now let's talk about Squier for a minute, because I said it's a real option. Not every Squier is a Classic Vibe. The Sonic and Affinity lines are cheaper, and the quality varies more. If you're buying Squier as a real guitar to keep and gig, look at the Classic Vibe and the 40th Anniversary lines, and plan on a setup. A good setup on a Squier is often the single best upgrade you can buy. After that, pickups and tuners are the usual upgrades, and they're cheap on a Fender-style guitar because, again, the parts ecosystem is huge.

And let's talk about the competitors for a second, because I don't want to sell you on them blindly either. Stainless steel frets and roasted necks are great, but specs aren't everything. Some spec-heavy guitars come with mediocre fretwork or electronics, and some direct-to-consumer brands don't let you play the guitar before you buy it. Check the return policy. And remember that a lot of these guitars won't hold their value the way a Fender does. If you plan to keep the guitar for a decade, that doesn't matter much. If you flip guitars every couple of years, it matters a lot.

One more thing on tariffs, because people ask me about this all the time. Tariff policy has been changing quickly. Some tariffs have been announced, delayed, changed, or challenged. Exemptions come and go. So if you're reading this or watching this later, don't assume the numbers I mentioned are still current. Check the latest news, and check the current price at a couple of dealers before you decide. What I can tell you with confidence is the direction of the last two years: costs went up, prices went up, and players responded by getting smarter about how they shop.

And honestly, that's a good thing. A player who knows how to check a neck, read a serial number, compare sold prices, and find a good tech is going to get a better guitar for less money, whether it says Fender on the headstock or not.
