It is a bright Saturday morning at an open air market in a small French town. Striped canvas awnings, crates of red tomatoes and green beans, a man in a blue apron weighing peaches on an old brass scale. A woman in her late sixties moves through the crowd in a navy cotton sweater, cream trousers, and white leather sneakers. Across her body, resting just at her hip, is a small cognac leather bag, no bigger than a hardcover book. In one hand, she carries a woven straw basket for the vegetables. Her shoulders are relaxed. She is not hunched, not shifting a heavy strap from one side to the other, not digging through a cavernous tote for her coin purse. She simply reaches into that little bag, pays, and moves on.

I watched a scene like that years ago, and I went home and looked at the enormous black tote bag hanging on the back of my kitchen door. It weighed, I am almost embarrassed to say, as much as a small bag of groceries. And I asked myself a question I had never asked before: why am I carrying all of this?

Today, I want to talk about why so many women over sixty are quietly moving away from big handbags, and why French women seem to have figured this out long before the rest of us. I will tell you exactly what to look for in a smaller bag, what to put inside it, and how to make the change without feeling like you have lost anything.

And stay with me until the end, because I am going to share the one simple exercise that made me finally let go of my giant tote. It involves your kitchen table, a soft cloth, and about fifteen minutes. And it will tell you more about yourself than you might expect.

Let me be honest with you first. I loved my big bags. For years, I carried a tote that could hold a paperback, a water bottle, an umbrella, a cardigan, a makeup pouch, a second makeup pouch, three lipsticks, receipts from months ago, a hairbrush, a snack, and, somewhere at the very bottom, my keys. Always my keys, at the very bottom.

A big bag felt like being prepared. It felt responsible. As women, many of us spent decades being the person who had everything. The tissue, the bandage, the snack for the child, the pen when someone needed to sign something. The big bag was a kind of uniform for that role.

But somewhere in our sixties, many of us start to notice things. The strap digs into the shoulder. We find ourselves always carrying the bag on the same side. We spend long seconds standing at a checkout, rummaging. And when we catch our reflection in a shop window, the bag sometimes looks like it is wearing us, instead of the other way around.

So let us go through the reasons, one by one, that women are making this change. I have five of them, and the fourth is the one nobody talks about, even though I think it matters most.

Number one. The body notices.

I am not a doctor, and I will not tell you anything about your health. But I can tell you what I noticed in my own body and what so many women tell me in their letters and comments. A heavy bag on one shoulder, day after day, does not feel good. You feel it at the end of a long afternoon of errands. You feel it on the drive home. You notice yourself lifting one shoulder slightly to keep the strap from sliding off.

A small bag changes that feeling immediately. Picture a soft leather crossbody, the strap adjusted so the bag sits right at your hip bone, the weight spread across your body instead of hanging from one side. Your hands are free. Your shoulders can drop. You can walk through a museum, a train station, or a busy market without that low, constant awareness of a weight pulling at you.

French women have long favored the crossbody. You will see it everywhere in Paris, a slim bag in black, tan, burgundy, or navy, worn across the body over a trench coat or a striped cotton top. It is practical in a city where people walk a great deal, take the metro, and carry their shopping home in a basket or a tote that is used only for the shopping, not for everything else.

That last detail is important, and it leads us to the second reason.

Number two. One bag does not have to do every job.

Here is something I wish I had understood thirty years ago. A French woman often does not ask one handbag to do everything. She has a small everyday bag for the essentials. And then she has other carriers for other jobs. A woven straw basket for the market. A plain canvas tote for books from the library or a baguette and a bottle of wine. A sturdy shopping bag that folds into a little square in a pocket.

The big bag in my old life was trying to be all of these at once. My purse, my briefcase, my grocery bag, my gym bag, my emergency kit. No wonder it was heavy. No wonder I could never find anything.

When you separate the jobs, your everyday bag can become small, light, and beautiful. You carry the canvas tote only on the days you actually need it. And on the days you do not, your hands are free and your outfit looks cleaner.

Picture it. A camel wool coat, a gray cashmere scarf, and a small black leather bag with a thin strap, worn across the body. Now picture the same coat and scarf with a huge, slouching tote bumping against the hip. The first looks composed. The second looks like you are running late for something. The coat is the same. The scarf is the same. The bag changes the entire story.

And that brings us to the third reason, which is all about the eye.

Number three. A smaller bag is more flattering.

This is a matter of proportion, and stylists talk about it often. A very large bag carried against the body tends to visually widen the area where it sits, usually the hip and the side of the waist. It can also pull the whole silhouette downward, because the eye goes to that big shape and stays there.

A smaller bag, especially one worn high on the body, near the waist or the hip, keeps the line clean. It also lets your clothes do their work. If you have chosen a lovely knit dress in a deep olive or a soft ivory blouse with a little drape at the neckline, you want people to see that, not a large slab of leather or nylon.

There is also something about scale and age that I find interesting. When we are older, a smaller, finely made object carried with ease can look very elegant. It suggests a woman who knows what she needs and does not need to prove anything. Think of a woman in a simple black dress with a small chain strap bag over one shoulder, or a short handled bag in quilted leather carried in the crook of her arm. There is a quiet confidence in that.

Now, small does not mean tiny. I am not talking about the micro bags you see in fashion photographs that can hold one lipstick and nothing else. That is not practical, and practical matters to us. I mean a bag that holds your real essentials, well organized, and nothing extra. We will talk about exactly what that is in a moment.

But first, the reason I promised you, the one almost nobody mentions.

Number four. Less in the bag means less on the mind.

This is the reason that surprised me most. When I moved to a smaller bag, I expected my shoulder to feel better. I expected my outfits to look more polished. What I did not expect was to feel lighter in my head.

A huge bag is a kind of portable worry. It holds the umbrella in case it rains, the cardigan in case it is cold, the snack in case you get hungry, the second pair of glasses in case the first breaks, the tissues, the hand cream, the medicines just in case, the papers you have been meaning to look at. Every item in there is a small what if. And you carry all of those what ifs on your shoulder, all day long.

When I emptied my big tote and chose only what fit in a small bag, I had to make decisions. Do I really need an umbrella today? I can check the sky. Do I really need three lipsticks? I need one, the soft rose one I wear every day. Do I need every receipt from the last four months? Absolutely not.

Those little decisions added up to something that felt like freedom. I stopped preparing for every possible disaster. I started trusting that I could handle the day with what I had, and that if I truly needed something, I could find it, buy it, or do without it.

I think that is something French women understand in their bones. There is a lightness in the way they move through the world. It is not carelessness. It is a kind of trust, in themselves and in the day.

I would love to hear from you right here. Go down to the comments and tell me: what is the one thing you always carry in your handbag, the thing you would never leave the house without? I am curious whether it is something practical, like reading glasses, or something sentimental, like a small photograph or a family keepsake. Tell me. I read every comment.

Now, the fifth reason is about how the world has changed around us, and how that has made small bags easier than ever.

Number five. Our lives need less carrying than they used to.

Think about what used to fill our bags. A checkbook in a leather cover. A thick wallet stuffed with cards and photographs. A paper address book. A calendar. A map of the city. A camera. A paperback for the bus.

For many of us, a lot of that has changed. A phone can now handle some of what used to take up space, whether that is a map, a camera, or a list of appointments. Some of us pay with a card or a phone more often than with cash. I am not saying everyone lives this way, and I know many women prefer paper calendars and real cash, as do I sometimes. But for many of us, the list of truly essential things has gotten shorter, even if our bags have not caught up.

This is one reason I think the quiet shift toward smaller bags is happening. We are simply noticing that we no longer need a suitcase on our shoulder to get through a day.

So, how do you actually make the change? Let me walk you through it, the way I would if we were sitting together at a kitchen table.

First, think about what a good everyday small bag looks like.

Look for a size somewhere around a large paperback book or a little bigger. Big enough for a phone, a slim wallet, keys, reading glasses in a thin case, and a lipstick. That is the core. If it can hold a small folded tissue pack and a little hand cream too, wonderful.

Look for a strap you can adjust. A crossbody strap that can be lengthened or shortened lets you wear the bag across your body on busy days and over one shoulder on quieter ones. Some bags come with two straps, a thin leather one and a wider fabric one, so you can change the look. A wider strap can feel more comfortable on the shoulder because it spreads the weight.

Look at the closure. A zip across the top keeps everything secure, which is helpful in crowded places. A flap with a magnetic snap is quick and easy to open with one hand. A simple turn lock is elegant but can be fiddly if your fingers are stiff on a cold morning, so test it in the store. Open and close it several times. If it frustrates you in the shop, it will frustrate you more at the bus stop.

Look inside. A small interior pocket or two, one with a zip for keys or a card, makes such a difference. Some women like a bright lining, in red or soft yellow, because it makes it easier to see what is inside. A black interior can swallow small black objects like keys and glasses cases.

Look at the material. Smooth leather in a classic color like black, cognac, tan, navy, or a deep burgundy will go with almost everything and will soften beautifully over time. A pebbled leather is more forgiving of small scratches. A sturdy canvas or a woven fabric can be lighter and more casual for summer. Pick up the empty bag and feel the weight. A small bag that is heavy before you have put anything in it defeats the purpose.

And look at the color with your wardrobe in mind. French women often choose one neutral bag that works with their main coat and shoes. If most of your clothes are navy, gray, and cream, a navy or a soft gray bag is very easy. If you wear a lot of camel, brown, and olive, a cognac leather bag is wonderful. Then, if you want, add a second small bag in a color that makes you smile, a deep red or a soft green, for the days you want a little lift.

Second, think about the companion carrier.

Since your small bag will not carry everything, choose one companion for the bigger days. A simple canvas tote in natural or navy that folds flat into a drawer. A woven market basket with leather handles for summer weekends. A light nylon shopping bag that folds into a pouch. This is not your handbag. It is your carrier for the day's groceries or library books, and it goes home and gets emptied.

The magic is in the emptying. When the tote is only for one job, it never becomes that bottomless pit again.

Third, think about how you organize what you carry.

A few small pouches can help, but be careful, because pouches inside a small bag can become their own little clutter. I keep one slim pouch in soft gray fabric with a lipstick, a tiny comb, and a folded tissue. That is all. My glasses go in a thin case. My keys clip onto a small ring inside the bag so I never dig for them again. After decades of searching the bottom of a tote for my keys, I cannot tell you how satisfying that little ring is.

Fourth, think about how the small bag works with your outfits through the seasons.

In spring, picture a beige cotton trench coat, belted loosely, a striped navy and white top underneath, straight leg jeans, and a tan leather crossbody worn over the coat so it sits neatly at the hip. In summer, a loose white linen shirt with the sleeves rolled, wide cream trousers, flat leather sandals, and a small woven bag on a thin strap. In autumn, a charcoal turtleneck, a long plaid wool skirt in gray and rust, low brown ankle boots, and a cognac bag that picks up the warm thread in the plaid. In winter, the camel coat, the soft gray scarf, black trousers, and a black leather bag worn across the body under the open front of the coat, so it stays close and secure.

Notice that in every one of these pictures, the bag is a finishing touch, not the main event. It echoes a color already in the outfit, a brown in the boots, a navy in the stripe, a cream in the trousers. That is a very French habit. Nothing fights for attention. Everything quietly agrees.

And now, let me come back to the kitchen table exercise I promised you. This is the moment that changed everything for me.

Choose a quiet afternoon. Clear your kitchen table and lay down a soft cloth, a dish towel or a pale linen napkin. Take your current everyday bag, the big one, and empty every single thing out onto that cloth. Every pocket, every zipped corner, every crumb at the bottom. Turn the bag upside down and give it a little shake.

Now, look at everything spread out in front of you. Do not judge. Just look.

Then, divide it into three groups. In the first group, put only the things you used in the last week. Your phone, your keys, your wallet, your glasses, the lipstick you actually wear. In the second group, put the things you might need sometimes, but not every day. The umbrella, the spare cardigan, the extra hand cream. In the third group, put everything else. The old receipts, the dried out pen, the loose mints, the three lipsticks you never wear, the papers you meant to deal with.

Here is what happened when I did this. The first group was so small it would have fit in my two cupped hands. The second group belonged in my car, or in my canvas tote for specific days. And the third group, honestly, mostly belonged in the trash or a drawer.

Looking at that first little group, I understood. I had been carrying that enormous, heavy bag every day for the sake of a handful of things that could fit in a bag the size of a book. The rest was just weight. Weight on my shoulder, and if I am honest, a little weight on my spirit too.

That exercise is the bridge. Once you see your true essentials laid out on a cloth in front of you, choosing a smaller bag no longer feels like giving something up. It feels like a relief.

Let me add a few gentle words for the transition, because change is not always easy, even when it is good.

Give yourself a trial week. Put your essentials into a smaller bag you already own, even if it is not perfect. Carry it for seven days. Notice what you miss, if anything. Most women find they miss almost nothing.

Keep a small car kit, if you drive. A little box or pouch in the glove compartment or the trunk with an umbrella, tissues, a spare pair of reading glasses, and anything else that gives you peace of mind. That way you have your just in case items nearby without carrying them on your shoulder all day.

Do not throw away your big bag in a burst of enthusiasm. Keep it for travel, or for the occasional day you truly need it. Use it on purpose, not by default.

Empty your small bag regularly. Once a week, take everything out, wipe the inside with a soft cloth, and put back only what belongs. Little bags fill up quickly too, and this habit keeps them light.

And care for the leather. A soft brush or a dry cloth, and a leather conditioner if the maker recommends one, used now and then, will keep a good leather bag looking lovely for years. Store it stuffed lightly with tissue paper, in a cotton dust bag, out of direct sunlight, so it keeps its shape and its color.

Let me go back, for a moment, to the woman at the market. The striped awnings, the crates of tomatoes, the navy sweater and the cream trousers. The little cognac bag at her hip and the straw basket in her hand. What struck me most was not the bag itself. It was how free she looked. Her hands were available to choose a peach, to greet a neighbor, to touch the arm of a friend. Her shoulders were open. She moved like someone who was not carrying anything she did not need.

I think that is what this quiet change is really about. It is not just a fashion choice. It is a way of moving through our sixties and beyond with a little more lightness, a little more ease, and a little more trust in ourselves. French women may have shown us the way, but there is nothing French about wanting to feel light. That belongs to all of us.

Before you go, I have one more question for you, and I would really love to read your answers. Leave me a comment and tell me: after watching this, if you emptied your bag onto your kitchen table today, what is the one thing you suspect you would find at the bottom that you have not used in months? Be honest. I will tell you mine. It was a single earring I had been looking for since the spring.

Thank you so much for spending this time with me. Next time, we will talk about more of these small, quiet changes that make dressing after sixty feel easier and more elegant. Until then, my dear, empty that bag onto a soft cloth, keep only what you love and need, and walk out into your day with your hands free.
