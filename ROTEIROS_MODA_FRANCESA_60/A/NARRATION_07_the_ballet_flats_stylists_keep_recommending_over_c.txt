So let's start with the first thing stylists look at. The leather.

Hold a ballet flat in your hands. Really hold it. Turn it over. Press your thumb into the side, just behind the toe. Good leather for a flat should feel supple, almost like the inside of a soft leather glove. When you press, it should give a little and then relax back. It shouldn't crackle. It shouldn't feel like cardboard wrapped in plastic.

There are a few kinds of leather you'll see again and again. Smooth calfskin, which has that soft, quiet sheen, perfect with a gray flannel trouser or a black wool skirt. Lambskin, which is even softer and molds to the foot beautifully, though it can scuff more easily. Suede, with its velvety nap, lovely in a deep chocolate brown or a dusty rose with a denim midi skirt in autumn. And patent leather, that glossy mirror finish, very chic with a black cigarette pant, but less forgiving because the coating makes it stiffer and less breathable.

If your feet tend to swell a little by the end of the day, and many of us notice that, a softer leather like lambskin or a supple suede can be kinder because it stretches and shapes itself to you. Patent, on the other hand, holds its shape firmly. It looks crisp, but it may not give you much room.

And look inside the shoe, too. The lining matters as much as the outside. A leather lining, smooth and slightly cool to the touch, lets your foot breathe. A synthetic lining can feel slick and warm, and over a long day of walking, that warmth adds up. When I pick up a flat in a store, I always slide my fingers inside and feel along the edge where the lining meets the top of the shoe. Is it smoothly finished? Is the stitching neat and even? Or is there a rough ridge that's going to rub against the back of my heel by three o'clock?

All right. The sole. And this is the part that, honestly, most people never think about until their feet are aching.

Turn the flat upside down. Look at the bottom. A traditional ballet flat often has a very thin leather sole, sometimes with a tiny stacked heel just a few millimeters high. It looks elegant. It's light. It's the closest thing to walking barefoot in a beautiful shoe. And for a short evening out, sitting at a dinner table with a glass of wine, it can be perfect.

But think about your real life. Think about walking across a parking lot. Standing in line at the pharmacy. Strolling through a farmers' market on a Saturday, looking at peaches and sunflowers. Climbing the steps at church. Walking the grandchildren to the park. A thin leather sole on hard pavement for hours can feel like walking on a cutting board.

What stylists look for instead is a sole that has a bit of substance while still looking slim from the side. A rubber sole, or a leather sole with a rubber layer added at the forefoot and heel, gives you grip on a slick floor and a little bit of shock absorption with each step. Look at the edge of the sole from the side. You want it to look refined, not clunky, but you also want to see that there's something there.

Here's a quick check. Hold the flat by the heel and the toe and gently bend it. Where does it bend? A well-designed flat should bend at the ball of the foot, right where your own foot naturally flexes when you take a step. If it folds in half in the middle of the arch, like a piece of paper, it gives your foot almost no support. If it doesn't bend at all, it'll feel rigid and awkward, and your foot will be fighting the shoe all day.

And now look at the heel. Even a very small heel, just a slight lift, can make a flat feel more comfortable for many women than a completely flat sole. Many of us find that a tiny rise takes a bit of pressure off the back of the leg. You're not looking for a kitten heel here. Just a whisper of height. When you look at the flat from the side, you should see the heel sitting just a little higher than the ball of the foot.

Now, here's where I want to pause, because the next point is the one that changed everything for me. The toe shape. And it's where so many of us have been quietly suffering without realizing there was another option.

Let's talk about the toe. Picture three ballet flats lined up on a wooden shelf in a boutique. The first has a sharp pointed toe, sleek and long, almost like an arrow. The second has a perfectly round toe, soft and sweet, like a classic dancer's slipper. And the third has what's often called an almond toe, gently tapered but rounded at the very tip.

Each has its charm. A pointed toe elongates the leg beautifully, especially with an ankle-length trouser in charcoal wool or a midi skirt in a soft camel. It reads as sophisticated, a little bit Parisian office. But a pointed toe often means less room for your toes, and if you have any bunions or toes that have changed shape over the years, a pointed flat can pinch.

A round toe gives the most room. It's comfortable, it's friendly, and it has a youthful, playful look. With cropped jeans and a striped Breton top, it's pure French weekend. The only thing to watch is that a very round, very wide toe can sometimes look a little heavy, especially on a smaller foot.

The almond toe, and this is the one many stylists love for women over sixty, sits right in between. It gives your toes more room than a point, but it still looks polished and lengthening. It's the shape that works with almost everything. A navy pleated skirt. Straight white jeans. A black jersey dress. A beige linen trouser in summer.

But here's the thing that matters more than the outline you see from above. It's the depth of the toe box. Some flats are cut very low over the toes, so the leather presses down on the tops of your toes. Others have a bit more height, more space. When you try on a flat, wiggle your toes. Really wiggle them. Can you move them? Or are they pressed flat like sardines in a tin?

Also look at the topline, which is the edge of the shoe opening. A very low-cut topline, showing a lot of the top of your foot, is the classic dancer look. It's lovely, but it can mean the shoe doesn't hold on to your foot very well, and you end up gripping with your toes to keep it on. That gripping is exhausting by the end of a day. A slightly higher topline, or one with a small elastic edge hidden inside, can hold the shoe more securely.

Cushioning. Slip your hand inside the flat and press down on the footbed, the part your foot actually rests on. Is it a flat, hard board with a thin piece of leather glued on top? Or is there a little bit of padding, a soft give, especially under the heel and the ball of the foot?

Many of us have noticed that the natural padding under our feet feels thinner than it used to. That's one reason a hard, flat footbed can feel so uncomfortable after a while. A flat with a cushioned insole, ideally covered in soft leather, can make an enormous difference. Some flats have a little arch shaping built in, a gentle rise under the middle of the foot. Press your thumb along the inside of the footbed from heel to toe. Do you feel a slight curve, or is it completely flat?

And here's one of my favorite tips. Check whether the insole is removable. Some well-made flats have a footbed you can lift out. That means if you use your own cushioned insert, the kind you might already have at home, you can slip it in without the shoe becoming too tight. If the insole is glued in, adding your own insert can make the shoe too snug.

I'll say it simply, because I think it's important. A ballet flat should feel like a soft embrace, not like a plank. If you put it on and your first thought is, oh, that's hard, listen to that thought. It won't get softer with time the way leather uppers sometimes do. The footbed is the footbed.

Now let's talk about the heel counter, which sounds technical but is really very simple. The heel counter is the back part of the shoe that cups your heel. Squeeze it gently between your thumb and finger. In a good flat, it should have a bit of structure, firm enough to hold your heel in place, but not so hard that it digs in. And look at the very top edge, where it touches the back of your ankle. Is it padded? Is it soft? This is where blisters are born, my friend. A soft, slightly padded edge is a gift.

And now, the moment I promised you at the very beginning. The one-foot test.

When I try on a pair of ballet flats in a store, after I've walked around a little, after I've looked in the mirror and admired them with my trousers, I do one more thing. I stand near something steady, a chair or the edge of a display table, just to be safe. I stand on one foot in the shoe for a few seconds, then I slowly rise up a little onto the ball of that foot. Then I do the same with the other foot.

What am I feeling for? Three things. First, does my heel stay inside the shoe, or does it slip out the back? If it slips, the shoe is either too big or the heel counter isn't holding me, and I'll be gripping with my toes all day. Second, do I feel a sharp edge anywhere, across the top of my toes, at the side of my little toe, at the back of my heel? Standing on one foot puts all my weight in one place, so any pressure point shows up right away. Third, does the shoe bend with me when I rise up, right at the ball of the foot, or does it fight me?

If a flat passes all three, I know it's worth considering. If it fails even one, I thank it kindly, put it back in its box with the tissue paper, and walk away. No matter how beautiful it is. No matter what name is printed inside.

Let's go back to the store for a moment, because I want to give you a little shopping ritual. Something you can do every time.

First, shop in the afternoon. Many of us find our feet are a little larger later in the day. If you try on shoes in the morning, they might feel perfect then and tight by evening.

Second, bring the socks or tights you'll actually wear. If you'll wear your flats with thin nylon tights in winter, bring them. If you'll wear them barefoot in summer, try them barefoot, or with a thin no-show liner.

Third, try both shoes, not just one. Our two feet are often slightly different sizes. Fit the larger foot.

Fourth, walk on a hard floor, not just the soft carpet in the fitting area. Find a stretch of tile or wood and walk back and forth. Listen to the sound. A good flat shouldn't slap loudly against the floor with each step. That slap often means the shoe isn't staying with your foot.

Fifth, do the one-foot test I described. Steady yourself, stand, rise, feel.

And sixth, look at yourself in a full-length mirror from the side. How does the shoe look with your legs, your trousers, your skirt? Does it look balanced? Does it make you stand a little taller?

And let's talk about caring for your flats, because a good pair, well cared for, can stay with you for a long, long time.

Store them with a little shaping inside, a cedar shoe tree or even some tissue paper, so the toe keeps its form. Keep them in a soft cotton dust bag, away from direct sunlight, which can fade beautiful colors like navy and red. Clean smooth leather gently with a soft cloth and a cream made for leather. For suede, a suede brush, used lightly in one direction, keeps the nap looking fresh. And when the sole or heel tip starts to wear, take them to a shoe repair shop before the damage reaches the shoe itself. A small repair can give a pair you love many more seasons.
