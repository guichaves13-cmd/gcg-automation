Let's go through the most common fibers you'll see, and what each one tends to feel like.

Cotton. Natural, breathable, soft. Think of a crisp white cotton shirt, a striped cotton Breton top, a pair of chino trousers in khaki. Cotton feels fresh in warm weather. It can wrinkle, and some cottons shrink a little if washed in hot water. But it's comfortable and honest.

Linen. Made from flax. Cool, airy, a little crisp, with a beautiful natural texture. A linen shirt in sand or white, a linen trouser in soft olive. Linen wrinkles, and the French have made peace with that. Those soft creases are part of the charm.

Wool. Warm, springy, beautiful. A fine merino sweater in charcoal, a camel wool coat. Good wool feels soft and resilient. When you squeeze it in your hand and let go, it springs back.

Silk. Smooth, fluid, with a soft glow. A silk blouse in ivory, a silk scarf in navy and red. It drapes beautifully but often needs gentle care.

Then there are the man-made and blended fibers. Polyester, nylon, spandex, which you might also see called elastane. And rayon, viscose, or modal, which are made from processed plant material.

Now, I want to be fair. Synthetic fibers are not automatically bad. Many travel knits, the kind that come out of a suitcase without a wrinkle, rely on polyester and spandex. That's useful. If you travel often, a wrinkle-resistant knit dress in a deep navy or black can be a wonderful friend. A little spandex in a pair of trousers can make them move with you, which is a real comfort when you're sitting in a car or on a plane.

But here's what I look for. How does it feel? How does it breathe? A top that's mostly polyester can feel warm and a little clingy on a hot day. Rayon and viscose can drape beautifully, like a soft flowing blouse, but some can be delicate when washed. And look for blends that combine the best of both. A cotton with a touch of spandex. A wool with a little nylon for strength.

So the label isn't telling you good or bad. It's telling you what to expect. And once you know what to expect, you can decide if it fits your life.

Now, this next part is where I think so many of us were never taught what to do. Reading the label is step one. But the real test happens with your hands. And there's a little trick with light that I use every single time, which I'll show you in just a moment.

Let's talk about quality checks. These are simple things you can do in any store, in a few seconds.

Check number one. The seams. Turn the garment inside out, just like our woman did. Look at the seams along the sides and under the arms. Are the stitches small, even, and close together? Or are they long and loose, with threads hanging off? Gently pull the fabric on either side of a seam. Does it hold firmly, or do you see the stitches stretch and gaps appear? A well-made garment has seams that feel secure.

Check number two. The light test. This is my little trick. Hold a single layer of the fabric up to the store light. Can you see right through it? If a knit top is very sheer when you hold it up, it may be thin, and it may lose its shape faster, or show more than you'd like. A denser knit, where the light glows softly through but you can't see the details of your hand behind it, often holds up better. This works beautifully on T-shirts, cardigans, and knit dresses.

Check number three. The stretch and recovery test. Take a small section of a knit fabric, maybe at the hem, and gently stretch it a little. Then let go. Does it snap back to its shape? Or does it stay a bit stretched and wavy? Good recovery means the garment is more likely to keep its shape at the elbows, the knees, and the seat.

Check number four. The pattern match. If the garment has stripes or a print, look at where the pieces meet. At the side seams, at the shoulders, at the center front. Do the stripes line up? In a well-made striped Breton top, the stripes often meet neatly at the sides. When they're badly mismatched, it can be a sign of a rushed cut.

Check number five. The buttons and closures. Look at the buttons. Are they sewn on firmly, or do they wobble? Is there a spare button sewn inside? Look at the zipper. Does it glide smoothly? Is it hidden neatly under a flap of fabric, or does it bulge?

Check number six. The hem. Look at the bottom edge of a skirt, a trouser, a sleeve. Is it finished smoothly and evenly? Does it hang straight when you hold the garment up?

Six checks. Seams. Light. Stretch. Pattern. Closures. Hem. They take less than a minute. And they work under any brand name, under any owner.

And now, my five-minute fitting room ritual, the one I promised you.

Minute one. Before you even put it on, do the quick checks. Read the fabric label. Turn it inside out. Hold it to the light. Gently stretch and release. If it fails here, it doesn't come into the fitting room.

Minute two. Put it on and stand still. Look in the mirror straight on. Does it lie smoothly over your shoulders? Are the shoulder seams sitting at the edge of your shoulders, not halfway down your arm? Are there pulls across the bust or the hips? Does it gape between buttons?

Minute three. Move. Lift your arms as if you're reaching for a high shelf. Sit down on the little bench. Bend as if you're picking up a grandchild's toy. Twist as if you're looking over your shoulder while backing out of a parking space. Does it stay in place? Does it ride up? Does it pull? Real life is movement, and your clothes should move with you.

Minute four. Turn around. Use the side mirrors, or a hand mirror, to look at the back. How does it sit across your back, your seat, the back of your legs? Many of us forget to look at the back, but everyone behind us in line at the post office sees it.

Minute five. The closet question. Ask yourself, honestly, what are three things I already own that I would wear with this? If you can name three right away, that's a very good sign. If you can't name even one, it might be a lovely piece, but it may end up hanging alone in your closet with the tags still on.
