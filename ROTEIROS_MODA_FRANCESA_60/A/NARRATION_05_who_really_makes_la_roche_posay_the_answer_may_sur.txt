It is a quiet evening in my bathroom. The light over the mirror is soft and warm, there is a folded white cotton towel on the edge of the sink, and I am holding a small white tube with that familiar blue lettering. I squeeze a pearl of cream onto the back of my hand. It is thick, a little cool, the color of fresh milk, and it smells of almost nothing at all. I have been using products from this brand on and off for years. And one evening, standing exactly like this, I turned the tube over, read the very small print on the back, and realized I did not actually know who made it.

So today, let us find out together. Who really makes La Roche-Posay? The answer is not a secret, but for many women I talk to, it is a genuine surprise. And more importantly, I want to talk about what that answer actually means for you, standing in the aisle of your drugstore, trying to decide what to put on your face after sixty.

Stay with me to the end, because I am going to share the three questions I now ask myself before I buy any skincare product, French or otherwise. They have changed the way I shop, and they have nothing to do with the price on the shelf.

Let me start with the name itself, because it tells a story.

La Roche-Posay is a real place. It is a small spa town in France, in the west central part of the country, and it is known for its thermal water. For a very long time, people have traveled to towns like this one in France to take the waters, as they used to say. Picture a quiet main street, pale stone buildings with gray slate roofs, wrought iron balconies, and a thermal center where people come for treatments using the local spring water. The skincare brand takes its name from that town, and its thermal spring water is a central part of the brand's identity. You have probably seen it yourself, that slim spray can of thermal water with the blue and white label.

So that is the first part of the answer, and it is the romantic part. The brand is tied to a real French town with a real thermal spring.

Now here is the part that surprises people.

As far as public information shows, La Roche-Posay is owned by L'Oréal. Yes, the same large French beauty group whose name you have seen on hair color boxes and mascara wands for most of your life. Within L'Oréal, La Roche-Posay sits in the division the company calls Dermatological Beauty. That division groups together brands that are positioned around skin health and are often recommended through pharmacies and skincare professionals.

I remember the first time I learned this. I had always pictured La Roche-Posay as a small, independent pharmacy brand, a little family operation tucked away in that spa town, separate from the glossy world of department store beauty counters. And when I found out it was part of one of the largest beauty companies in the world, I felt, honestly, a little bit fooled. Not because anybody lied to me, but because I had told myself a story without checking it.

And that is really what this video is about. Not about whether a brand is good or bad because of who owns it, but about learning to read past the story on the front of the box.

So let us go through what this ownership means for you, step by step. I have five things I want you to understand, and the third one is the one that really changed my own shopping habits.

Number one. Big ownership is not the same as bad.

When some women hear that their favorite pharmacy brand belongs to a large corporation, their first reaction is disappointment. I understand that feeling. But let us be fair about it.

A large company can bring things to a brand that a small one sometimes cannot. Wide distribution, so you can find the same product in your local drugstore in Ohio and in a pharmacy in Lyon. Consistent supply, so the cream you love does not suddenly disappear from shelves. Packaging and quality control systems. These are real things that matter to a shopper.

At the same time, large ownership means that the brand is part of a business portfolio. Decisions about which products to launch, which ones to discontinue, how to market them, and how to price them are made within a much bigger company. That is simply how it works. It does not make the product in your hand better or worse. It just means you should judge the product on what is actually in it and how your skin responds to it, not on an image of a tiny artisanal workshop.

So, neither panic nor blind loyalty. Just clear eyes. And clear eyes lead us straight to the next point, which is something many of us were never taught to do.

Number two. The front of the box is marketing. The back of the box is information.

Pick up any skincare product. Turn it over. On the front, you will see the brand name, a product name, perhaps words like gentle, soothing, for sensitive skin, or developed with dermatologists. Those words are chosen carefully by the company, and some of them are more meaningful than others. Phrases like for sensitive skin are not always defined by strict universal rules, so they tell you how the company wants you to see the product.

Now look at the back. Find the ingredient list. In the United States, cosmetic ingredients are generally listed in descending order of concentration, at least for ingredients present above a certain small amount. So the first few ingredients make up most of what is in the tube. Very often, the very first ingredient is water, sometimes written as aqua. On products from a thermal water brand, you may see the thermal spring water listed, often near the top.

You do not need to become a chemist. But I want you to get comfortable doing three small things with that list. Notice the first five ingredients, because they tell you the base of the product. Notice whether fragrance or parfum appears, if you know your skin does better without added scent. And notice anything you already know your skin does not tolerate well, because you have learned that through experience.

I keep a little card in my wallet, an index card in pale yellow, with three or four ingredient names written on it that I know my own skin does not love. When I am in a store and I am unsure, I pull out the card and compare. It takes thirty seconds. It has saved me from buying many beautiful jars that would have ended up at the back of my cabinet.

And now, the point that changed things for me.

Number three. The same parent company may own several brands on the same shelf.

Here is where it gets interesting. When you walk down the skincare aisle in an American drugstore, you will see rows and rows of boxes in white, blue, green, and pale gold, all promising gentle care for your face. It looks like a competition between many separate companies. And sometimes it is. But sometimes, several of those brands share the same parent.

As far as public information shows, L'Oréal's Dermatological Beauty division includes other well known names alongside La Roche-Posay, such as Vichy, which is also named after a French spa town, and CeraVe, a brand many American women know well. I am not telling you that these products are identical, because I do not know that, and they are positioned and formulated as distinct brands. But I am telling you that the choice between them is not always a choice between rival companies.

Why does this matter to you? Because it frees you from brand loyalty as a reason to buy. If you have been paying for a particular product because you believe in the story of a small independent house, it is worth knowing the bigger picture. And it reminds you to compare products by their ingredient lists, their textures, and how they feel on your skin, rather than by the logo.

The same is true across the beauty industry, by the way. Many familiar names at every price point belong to a handful of large groups. This is not unique to one company. It is simply the landscape we shop in, and once you see it, you see it everywhere.

I would love to pause here and ask you something. Go down to the comments and tell me: is there a skincare product you have used for more than ten years, and do you know which company owns it? Write the product, and if you found out the parent company, tell me whether it surprised you. I read all of your comments, and I learn so much from them.

Now, the fourth point is about texture, and it is where French women have something genuinely useful to teach us.

Number four. Choose by texture and by ritual, not only by promise.

When I watch French women shop in a pharmacy, and I have spent many happy minutes in French pharmacies with their green neon crosses outside and their long glass counters inside, I notice that they ask about texture constantly. Is this a fluid or a cream? Is it rich or light? Does it absorb quickly or leave a little veil on the skin?

For many of us over sixty, skin feels different than it did at forty. It may feel drier, it may feel tighter after cleansing, or it may react to things it never minded before. These are feelings, and I am not going to tell you what causes them for you, because I am not a doctor and every face is different. If you have real concerns about your skin, a dermatologist is the person to see. But I can tell you how to choose by feel.

A light fluid, almost like milk, pours easily into your palm and disappears into the skin within a minute. Some women like it under makeup in the morning. A rich cream, thick enough to hold a peak when you lift your finger out of the jar, sits on the skin a little longer and can feel comforting at night, especially in winter when the heating is on. A balm, waxy and solid at first and then melting with the warmth of your fingers, is often used on very dry patches or as a final layer.

The French approach is to build a small, consistent ritual. A gentle cleanser in the evening, perhaps a milk or a cleansing water on a soft cotton round. A moisturizer whose texture they like. Sunscreen during the day. And very often, a spritz of thermal water, a fine cool mist from that slim spray can, simply because it feels good. They do not chase every new launch. They find what their skin likes, and they keep doing it.

So when you stand in front of that wall of white and blue boxes, ask for a tester if you can. Put a little on the inside of your wrist or the back of your hand. Rub it in. Wait. How does it feel after two minutes? Sticky? Smooth? Tight? That feeling is more useful to you than any word printed on the front of the box.

And the fifth point is the one that brings it all together, because it is about alternatives.

Number five. Knowing the owner gives you choices.

Once you know that a brand is part of a large group, you have more freedom, not less. You can decide what matters to you.

Maybe you are perfectly happy with your La Roche-Posay moisturizer. Your skin likes it, the texture is right, you can find it easily, and you are comfortable with the price. Wonderful. Keep it. Knowing who owns it does not change how it feels on your face.

Maybe you would prefer to support smaller, independently owned companies when you can. That is a values choice, and it is a legitimate one. In that case, you can look for brands that clearly state their ownership, or do a little reading about a brand before you buy. Be patient with this, because ownership of beauty brands changes over time, and information online is not always up to date. Look for recent, reliable sources, and remember that a brand's own website will tell you its story, but not always the full corporate picture.

Maybe you are trying to spend less. Then the lesson is to compare ingredient lists across brands and price points. Sometimes a simpler, less expensive product has a base formula that is quite similar in style, a basic moisturizing cream with familiar ingredients. Sometimes it does not. The ingredient list and your own skin are the judges.

And maybe what you really love is the French pharmacy ritual itself. The calm, the simplicity, the small bottles lined up on a glass shelf, a cool mist on a warm day. That ritual is something you can recreate at home with whatever products suit you. It is a way of caring for yourself, not a logo.

Before the practical tips, I want to linger for a moment on something that does not show up on any label, because I think it matters more for women our age than almost anything else in this conversation. It is the question of trust.

When we were young, many of us trusted the beauty counter completely. A woman in a crisp white coat behind a glass case, a row of frosted glass jars lit from below, a little sample tucked into a pink paper bag. We believed what we were told, because it was told to us with such confidence. Then, over the years, many of us learned to trust nothing. We became cynical about every promise, every new jar, every beautiful advertisement in a glossy magazine.

I would like to suggest a middle path, and I think it is a very French one. Trust your own experience first. Trust the ingredient list second. And trust the marketing story last, as something pleasant to enjoy, like the pretty illustration on a box of chocolates, but not as the reason you buy.

Think about the way a French woman chooses bread. She does not care very much about the sign above the bakery or whether the bakery belongs to a family or a larger business. She cares about the crust, how it crackles when she squeezes the baguette, and how the inside smells when she tears off the end on the walk home. She knows what good bread feels like because she has paid attention for years. Skincare can be the same. Over time, if you pay attention, you will know what a good cream feels like on your own face, and no label will be able to talk you in or out of it.

There is one more thing I want to mention about ownership, and it is simply this. Brands can change hands. A product you love today may be reformulated tomorrow, and that can happen with any brand, large or small. Companies usually do not announce small formula changes with big fanfare. So if a familiar cream suddenly feels different, smells different, or behaves differently on your skin, do not assume you imagined it. Compare the ingredient list on the new tube to the photo of the old one on your phone. That little habit of photographing the back of products you love becomes even more useful here.

And if a favorite does change and no longer suits you, give yourself permission to let it go. Loyalty to a product is lovely, but your skin does not owe anything to a logo.

Let me give you a few very practical tips for your next trip to the store.

Bring your reading glasses. I am serious. Ingredient lists are printed in tiny type on curved tubes, and the lighting in drugstores is often harsh. I keep a pair of inexpensive readers in a soft gray pouch in my handbag just for this.

Take a photo of the back of a product you already love. Then, when you are comparing something new, you can look at the two ingredient lists side by side on your phone.

Look for the smaller details on the packaging. Many products show a little symbol of an open jar with a number and the letter M, which tells you how many months the product is meant to be used after opening. There may be a batch code stamped on the crimp of the tube or the bottom of the box. These are useful to notice, especially if you tend to keep products for a long time.

Patch test new products. Put a small amount somewhere discreet, like behind your ear or on the inside of your arm, for a few days before using it on your whole face. This is simple, sensible advice that many skincare professionals give, and it is especially wise if your skin has become more reactive over the years. And if anything stings, burns, or causes a rash, stop using it and talk to a professional.

Be wary of very dramatic promises. Words like erase, reverse, or transform are marketing language. A good moisturizer can make your skin feel comfortable and look more hydrated. That is a lovely thing on its own. You do not need a miracle.

Store your products well. Keep them away from the sunny windowsill and the steam of a hot shower if the label suggests it. Close the caps tightly. That little white tube on your bathroom counter will treat you better if you treat it gently.

The town is real. The thermal spring is real. The brand's connection to that place is a real part of its identity. And, as far as public information shows, the brand also belongs to one of the largest beauty companies in the world. Both things are true at the same time. A product can have a beautiful origin story and a big corporate parent. What matters for you is whether it is right for your skin, your budget, and your values.

Now, I promised you the three questions I ask before I buy any skincare product. Here they are.

The first question is: what does the back of the box say? Not the front. The back. I read the first few ingredients, I check for anything on my little yellow card, and I look for fragrance if I am trying to avoid it.

The second question is: how does it feel on my skin? I test the texture whenever I can, on my wrist or my hand, and I wait a couple of minutes. I ask myself whether I would actually enjoy putting this on every night for months, because consistency is what makes a ritual.

The third question is: who am I buying from, and does that matter to me today? Some days, I want convenience and familiarity, and I am happy to buy a widely available product from a large company. Other days, I want to support a smaller maker. Either answer is fine, as long as it is a choice I made with my eyes open.

Those three questions have made me a calmer shopper. I buy less. I return less. I waste less. And I enjoy my little evening ritual in front of the mirror more than I ever did when I was chasing every new jar.
