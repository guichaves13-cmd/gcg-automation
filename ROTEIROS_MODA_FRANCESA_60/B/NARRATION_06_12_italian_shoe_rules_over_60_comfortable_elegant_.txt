It's a bright Sunday morning, and I'm walking slowly along a cobblestone street lined with little shops, their shutters just beginning to roll up. Ahead of me, a woman with silver hair is crossing the square in a navy wool skirt, a cream cardigan, and the most beautiful pair of cognac leather loafers I have ever seen. Low heel. Soft, supple leather. Polished, but clearly worn and loved. She isn't wobbling. She isn't wincing. She's simply walking, easily and gracefully, over stones that would defeat most of the shoes in my closet.

I'm in my mid-sixties, and I'll be honest with you. For a long time, I thought I had to choose. Either my feet were comfortable, or I looked elegant. Never both. And so, like so many women, I slowly slid into shoes that were comfortable but shapeless, clunky, and, if I'm truthful, a little bit frumpy. Then I started paying attention to how Italian women dress from the ankle down, and everything changed.

Today, I'm going to share twelve Italian shoe rules for women over sixty. Rules that help you stay comfortable, look elegant, and never, ever look frumpy. For each one, I'll tell you which shoe or which idea it's about, why it matters after sixty, how to style it, and the frumpy mistake that so many of us make without realizing.

And stay with me until the end, because rule number twelve isn't really about a shoe at all. It's about the one thing that can make an inexpensive pair look beautiful, and a beautiful pair look tired. It's the rule I wish someone had told me thirty years ago.

Here's the idea that runs through every rule today. Shoes finish an outfit the way a frame finishes a painting. You can wear a lovely coat and a perfect sweater, but if the shoes are wrong, the whole picture looks unfinished. Get the shoes right, and even the simplest outfit looks considered.

Let's begin.

Rule number one. Own a great leather loafer.

Picture the loafer from that square. A classic leather loafer in cognac, chocolate brown, or black, with a low, stacked heel and a gently rounded or almond-shaped toe. Worn with cropped gray trousers and a navy sweater, or with dark jeans and a camel coat.

Why does it matter after sixty? A loafer gives you a closed, supportive shoe that you can walk in all day, but it has a polished, tailored shape. There are no laces to tie and no straps to fuss with, which matters more as our hands and backs ask for a little more ease. It's the shoe that bridges comfort and elegance better than almost any other.

How do I style it? I love a loafer with ankle-length trousers, so a little of the ankle shows, or with a thin sock in a matching color in the colder months. With a midi skirt and opaque tights, a loafer looks wonderfully academic and chic. And a cognac loafer warms up a gray or navy outfit beautifully.

The frumpy mistake is the wide, heavy, square-toed loafer with a thick rubber sole that looks more orthopedic than elegant. You can have cushioning and support without a bulky shape. Look for a slimmer outline and a sole that sits close to the ground, and try it with the trousers you actually wear.

I'll add one small thing. A loafer in suede, in a deep chocolate or a soft taupe, is a lovely second pair once you have a smooth leather one. Suede has a velvety surface that looks warm and relaxed with wool trousers and chunky knits in the colder months.

And speaking of trousers, rule number five is the one that will change how you think about trouser length and shoes forever. Keep it in mind.

Rule number two. Choose a low block heel, not a high stiletto.

Imagine a pump in soft black or navy leather, with a heel about the height of two fingers, and a heel shaped like a little block rather than a thin spike. Worn with a black wool dress and a long camel coat, or with tailored navy trousers.

Why does it matter after sixty? A low block heel spreads your weight over a wider base, which feels much more stable on your feet. It still lifts you a little, which changes your posture and the way trousers and skirts hang. You get that sense of occasion without the worry of every step.

How do I style it? A block heel pump is perfect for lunches, concerts, weddings, and those moments when flats feel too casual. With a midi skirt, it gives a graceful line. With trousers, it lengthens the leg. I like it in suede in the winter, because suede has a softness that feels very elegant.

The frumpy mistake is going too chunky. A heel that's too wide and too tall, with a platform on the front, starts to look heavy and dated. Look for a block heel that's slender enough to look refined, but solid enough to feel secure. Balance is everything.

And here's a gentle reminder. Wear new heels around the house for an evening or two before you take them out. Walk on the kitchen floor, climb a few stairs, stand at the counter while you make a cup of tea. You'll know very quickly whether they're friends or strangers.

Rule number three. Invest in a sleek ankle boot.

Picture a slim ankle boot in black or dark brown leather, with an almond toe, a low heel, and a side zip. Worn with straight black trousers that fall over the top of the boot, and a charcoal coat.

Why does it matter after sixty? In fall and winter, an ankle boot is the most versatile shoe you can own. It keeps your feet warm, it gives the ankle support, and a side zip makes it easy to get on and off without bending over and wrestling with laces. It works with trousers, with jeans, and with skirts and tights.

How do I style it? With trousers, I like the trouser to cover the top of the boot, so the line from waist to floor is unbroken. With a skirt, I match the tights to the boot, black tights with black boots, brown tights with brown boots, so the leg looks long and continuous.

The frumpy mistake is the wide, slouchy ankle boot with a big gap around the ankle and a heavy tread. Paired with a skirt, it can make the legs look shorter and thicker. A boot that fits closer to the ankle always looks more elegant.

Rule number four. Pick an almond toe over a round or a sharp point.

Now let's look closely at the front of the shoe. Imagine three shoes side by side. One with a very round, bulbous toe. One with a sharp, needle-like point. And one in the middle, gently tapered, softly rounded at the tip. That's the almond toe.

Why does it matter after sixty? An almond toe gives your toes more room than a sharp point, which is kinder to the foot. But it still elongates the line of the leg in a way that a round toe doesn't. It's the shape that quietly flatters almost every foot and almost every outfit.

How do I style it? I look for an almond toe in loafers, ballet flats, ankle boots, and pumps. In a black ballet flat with an almond toe, worn with cropped black trousers and a striped knit, you get a look that feels effortless and polished at once.

The frumpy mistake is a very round, wide toe on a flat shoe. It can make the foot look short and wide and give the whole outfit a childlike or heavy feeling. If you need room for your toes, look for a shoe that's generous inside but still slightly tapered at the tip.

Rule number five. Match your shoe to your trouser length.

Here it is, the rule I promised. Picture three pairs of trousers. A wide-leg trouser that falls to the floor. A straight trouser that ends at the ankle bone. And a cropped trouser that ends a few inches above the ankle. Each one needs a different shoe.

Why does it matter after sixty? So much of what makes an outfit look frumpy isn't the trouser or the shoe. It's the meeting point between them. Get that meeting point right, and you look taller, slimmer, and more pulled together. Get it wrong, and you can look like you're wearing someone else's clothes.

How do I style it? With a long wide-leg trouser, I choose a shoe with a little lift, a low block heel or a slim boot, so the hem just grazes the floor without dragging. With an ankle-length straight trouser, I like a loafer or a ballet flat, which shows a sliver of ankle. With a cropped trouser, a sleek flat or a low ankle boot that meets the hem works best.

The frumpy mistake is the gap. A cropped trouser with a chunky shoe and a slice of white sock in between. Or a long trouser that bunches and pools on top of a flat shoe. Stand in front of a full mirror with every pair of trousers and every pair of shoes you own. It's an afternoon well spent.

Take photos on your phone if it helps. Seeing the outfit from a little distance, the way other people see you walking down the street, makes it so much easier to notice where the line breaks. Then write down which shoes work with which trousers, and you'll never have to guess on a busy morning again.

Rule number six. Learn to love the ballet flat, done right.

Imagine a soft leather ballet flat in black, navy, or a warm taupe, with an almond toe, a thin flexible sole, and a slightly higher back so it stays on your heel. Worn with ankle-length navy trousers and a cream sweater, or with a full midi skirt.

Why does it matter after sixty? A ballet flat is light, easy, and feminine. It's perfect for spring and early fall, for travel, and for days with lots of short walks between shops and cafés. It gives a delicate, graceful finish to an outfit without any height at all.

How do I style it? I love a ballet flat with a cropped trouser, because it shows the ankle and keeps the look light. With a midi skirt, it feels very classic. And a taupe or nude flat close to your skin tone lengthens the leg wonderfully.

The frumpy mistake is the paper-thin flat with no support, the one that leaves your feet aching by noon. Look for a little padding inside and a sole with some structure. If you need extra support, a slim cushioned insole can often be added without changing the outside shape at all.

Rule number seven. Choose a knee-high boot that fits your calf.

Picture a tall leather boot in dark brown or black, smooth and slim, ending just below the knee, with a low heel. Worn under a midi skirt or a knit dress, so the hem of the skirt meets or overlaps the top of the boot. A long coat over the top.

Why does it matter after sixty? A tall boot is warm, it covers the leg completely, and it creates one long elegant line from the knee down. With a skirt or dress, it means you're never worried about bare skin in the cold. And it gives an outfit a sense of polish that's hard to match.

How do I style it? Let the skirt or dress cover the top of the boot, so no gap of leg shows. Match the tights to the boot if a little leg does peek through. And keep the colors earthy and rich, a brown boot with an olive dress and a camel coat, for example.

The frumpy mistake is the boot that doesn't fit the calf. Too tight, and it pinches and wrinkles at the top. Too loose, and it slouches and flaps when you walk. Many boots come in different calf widths, so take the time to find one that fits your leg properly. Fit is what makes a tall boot look elegant.

Also, think about how you'll get them on. A full-length inside zip makes a tall boot so much easier to wear, especially on mornings when bending feels like a chore. Sit on a chair, zip from the ankle up, and you're ready in seconds.

Rule number eight. Yes, you can wear a sneaker. Choose it carefully.

Here's the controversial one. Picture a clean, simple leather sneaker in white or cream, with a slim, low sole and no loud colors or logos. Worn with straight dark jeans, a navy cashmere sweater, and a camel coat. Or with a soft knit midi dress.

Why does it matter after sixty? A sneaker can be the most comfortable shoe you own, and there's no reason to give it up. But Italian women tend to treat sneakers as part of an elegant outfit, not as an excuse to stop thinking. When it's minimal and clean, a sneaker can make a classic outfit look relaxed and modern.

How do I style it? Pair it with your most polished pieces. A good coat, a tailored trouser, a fine knit. The contrast between the casual shoe and the refined clothes is exactly what makes it look chic. Keep it spotless, and keep the laces fresh and white.

The frumpy mistake is the athletic running shoe with bright mesh panels, chunky soles, and loud colors, worn with everyday clothes. That shoe is built for the gym and the trail, and it looks out of place with a wool coat. Keep the athletic shoes for exercise, and choose a sleek leather sneaker for everything else.

Rule number nine. Build around three core colors.

Imagine a small shoe shelf with just a few pairs. Black, a warm brown like cognac or chocolate, and one soft neutral, like taupe, navy, or a pale nude. Every pair on that shelf goes with almost everything in the closet.

Why does it matter after sixty? A smaller, more thoughtful shoe wardrobe means you spend less time deciding and more time enjoying. When your shoes come in a few versatile colors, you can always find the right pair, and you're less tempted to buy impulse shoes that never really work with anything.

How do I style it? Black for gray, black, and charcoal outfits. Cognac or chocolate for camel, cream, olive, and denim. Taupe or nude to lengthen the leg in lighter outfits, especially in spring and summer. Navy can stand in for black with a softer feel.

The frumpy mistake is buying shoes in colors you don't wear. A bright purple shoe might look fun in the store, but if nothing in your closet goes with it, it'll sit in the box. Choose color in your clothes and keep your shoes in a supporting role, unless you truly love and wear a statement shoe.

Rule number ten. Value quality leather over trendy shapes.

Picture a pair of plain, beautifully made leather shoes. Smooth, soft leather that creases gently at the toe, a neat stitched edge, a sole that's been resoled once or twice. Nothing trendy. Nothing flashy. Just good.

Why does it matter after sixty? Real leather tends to soften and mold to your foot over time, which can make a shoe more comfortable the longer you wear it. And a classic shape doesn't date, so you won't feel out of step in a year or two. That's very much the Italian approach. Buy fewer, buy better, and wear them for years.

How do I style it? Honestly, quality leather styles itself. A good leather loafer with dark jeans. A smooth leather pump with a navy dress. A soft leather boot with a camel coat. The shoes simply look right.

The frumpy mistake is chasing trends that don't suit our feet or our lives. Extreme platforms, very pointed toes, very high heels, or heavy chunky soles might be fashionable for a season, but they often look awkward and feel uncomfortable. Choose shapes you'll still love in ten years.

When I'm deciding on a new pair, I ask myself one simple question. Can I picture wearing these with three different outfits I already own? If the answer is yes, they come home with me. If I have to imagine buying new clothes to go with them, I leave them on the shelf, no matter how pretty they are.

Two more rules to go, and number twelve is the one I said I wish I'd learned thirty years ago.

Rule number eleven. Comfort comes from fit, not from bulk.

Picture a woman in a fitting chair at a little shoe shop, trying on a pair of brown leather loafers. She stands, walks across the floor, turns, and walks back. She presses her thumb at the toe. She checks the heel to see if it slips. She takes her time.

Why does it matter after sixty? Our feet can change over the years, and the size we wore decades ago might not be the size we need today. A shoe that fits properly in length, in width, and at the heel will be far more comfortable than a bulky shoe in the wrong size. Comfort is about fit first.

How do I style it? This one is really about shopping. Try shoes on in the afternoon, when feet tend to be a little larger. Bring the socks or tights you'll actually wear with them. Walk on a hard floor if you can, not just carpet. And if one foot is slightly larger, fit the larger one.

The frumpy mistake is assuming that only a big, heavy shoe can be comfortable. That's what leads so many of us into shapeless shoes. A well-fitted, well-made shoe with a supportive inside can be comfortable and elegant at the same time. Don't give up on elegance before you've found the right fit.

Rule number twelve. Take care of your shoes.

Here it is. The rule that matters most. Picture a quiet evening at home, a soft cloth, a tin of cream polish, and a pair of cognac loafers lined up on a folded newspaper. Shoe trees waiting nearby. A small brush for the suede boots.

Why does it matter after sixty? Because nothing ages an outfit like tired shoes. Scuffed toes, worn-down heels, salt stains on boots, and faded leather can make a beautiful coat look neglected. And well-cared-for shoes, even inexpensive ones, look polished and intentional. Care is the quiet secret behind so much Italian elegance.

How do I style it? I polish leather shoes regularly, brush suede gently, and use shoe trees to keep their shape. I take heels and soles to a cobbler when they start to wear, rather than waiting until it's too late. And I let shoes rest between wears, so they're not worn day after day.

The frumpy mistake is wearing shoes until they fall apart without ever caring for them. You notice it most at the heel, where the back edge gets worn down and the shoe starts to tilt. A simple trip to the cobbler can make an old favorite feel new again.

There's something almost meditative about it, too. Ten quiet minutes on a Sunday evening, polishing a pair of brown boots by the lamp, getting them ready for the week ahead. It's a small act of respect, for the shoes and for yourself.

Love the ballet flat, done right. Choose a knee-high boot that fits your calf. Wear a sneaker, but choose it carefully. Build around three core colors. Value quality leather over trendy shapes. Remember that comfort comes from fit, not bulk. And take care of your shoes.

If there's one thing I hope you remember, it's this. You never have to choose between comfort and elegance. The Italian way is to find shoes that fit beautifully, in shapes that flatter, in colors that work with everything, and then to look after them as if they matter, because they do. Your feet carry you through every day. They deserve shoes that make you feel like yourself.
