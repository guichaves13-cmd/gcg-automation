It's just after sunrise on a November morning in the Texas Hill Country, and a hunter is kneeling in the frost beside a white-tailed buck he shot ten minutes ago. In another year, he'd already be thinking about the drag back to the truck, the photo, the cooler. But this year he does something his grandfather would have recognized and his father never had to do. He pulls on a pair of gloves, rolls the deer onto its side, and slowly, carefully, starts checking every wound, every scrape, the navel, the ears, the edges of the antlers. He's looking for something small and white and moving.

Across Texas this season, thousands of hunters are doing the same thing. Quietly. Without much fanfare. Kneeling over deer in the brush country, the Hill Country, the Pineywoods, the plains, and looking closely at flesh. They're doing it because of an insect that Texas spent decades and enormous amounts of money driving out of the United States. And now it's back.

Here's why.

The insect is called the New World screwworm. Its scientific name is Cochliomyia hominivorax, and the second half of that name gives you an idea of its reputation. Roughly translated, it means man-eater. It isn't a worm. It's a fly, a metallic blue-green fly not much bigger than a common housefly. The problem is not the adult. The problem is what it leaves behind.

A female screwworm fly is drawn to open wounds on warm-blooded animals. A tick bite. A barbed-wire cut. A fresh brand. The navel of a newborn calf or fawn. A scrape from a fight between two bucks. She lays her eggs in a mass at the edge of the wound, often a couple hundred at a time. Within a day or so, the larvae hatch. And unlike most fly maggots, which feed on dead tissue, screwworm larvae feed on living flesh.

They burrow in, head down, in a tight cluster, and as they feed, the wound gets bigger. A bigger wound with a strong odor attracts more female flies. More eggs. More larvae. Left untreated, an infestation can kill an adult animal in a matter of days to a couple of weeks. For a newborn, it can be faster.

The larvae get their name from their shape. They're ringed with small spines, which makes them look a bit like a wood screw, and they twist into the tissue as they feed. After several days, the mature larvae drop out of the wound, burrow into the soil, and pupate. A new generation of flies emerges. In warm weather, the whole cycle can take only about three weeks.

That's the creature Texas hunters are looking for this fall.

To understand why its return is such a shock, you have to go back in time.

For most of the twentieth century, the screwworm was simply part of life in the American South and Southwest. Ranchers rode pastures looking for infested animals. They doctored wounds with smelly chemical treatments. They timed branding, castrating, and dehorning to avoid the warm months when flies were active. Livestock losses ran into the tens of millions of dollars a year. And wildlife paid a terrible price too.

Before eradication, in parts of Texas, screwworms were a major killer of white-tailed deer fawns. Wildlife biologists have repeatedly cited historical estimates that in bad years, screwworm infestations killed as many as eighty percent of fawns in some areas. Fawns are born with an open navel. In screwworm country, that navel was a target. Researchers have long noted that deer populations in Texas grew substantially after the screwworm was eliminated, and the parasite's absence is one of the reasons the Texas deer herd became what it is today.

So how did the United States get rid of it?

The answer is one of the great stories in the history of science, and it began with two entomologists working for the U.S. Department of Agriculture, Edward Knipling and Raymond Bushland. In the 1930s and 1940s, Knipling had an idea that sounded almost absurd. Female screwworm flies typically mate only once. So what if you flooded the wild population with males that were sterile? A female that mated with a sterile male would lay eggs that never hatched. Release enough sterile males, generation after generation, and the population would collapse.

Bushland worked out how to raise screwworms in enormous numbers and how to sterilize them using radiation without destroying the males' ability to compete for mates. The method became known as the sterile insect technique. It was tested successfully on the island of Curaçao in the 1950s, and then used on the mainland, first in Florida and then across the Southwest.

The effort was huge. Production plants raised hundreds of millions of sterile flies a week. Airplanes dropped them in small boxes across vast stretches of land. Texas ranchers themselves helped fund the program. Over the following years, the screwworm was pushed out of the United States. Outbreaks returned for a time, and the program pressed on. Eventually, the barrier was pushed south through Mexico and Central America, all the way to the narrow neck of Panama, where a permanent line of sterile fly releases was maintained to keep the pest from moving north out of South America.

Knipling and Bushland were later awarded the World Food Prize for that work. It's one of the very few times humans have deliberately eradicated a major pest from an entire region. For generations of Texans, the screwworm became something from their grandparents' stories.

Then the barrier broke.

In 2023, screwworm cases began appearing north of the Panama barrier. They spread through Central America. In late 2024, cases were confirmed in southern Mexico, and the U.S. suspended imports of live cattle from Mexico at times to protect the American herd. Through 2025, the infestation kept moving north through Mexico, getting closer and closer to the border. American agricultural agencies began preparing. The USDA announced plans for a sterile fly dispersal facility at Moore Air Base near Edinburg, Texas, and for expanded sterile fly production.

And in 2026, the screwworm was confirmed in Texas.

By midsummer, Texas Public Radio was reporting dozens of confirmed infestations in the state, tracked through the Texas Animal Health Commission and the USDA. By late August, the count had reached forty-seven. Agencies released sterile flies, set up response zones, and pleaded with livestock owners to check animals and report suspicious wounds. The USDA clarified that it would not lock down ranches simply because a case was confirmed, but the message was clear. The fly was back, and it was going to take everyone watching to stop it.

Which brings us back to the hunters.

Texas has one of the largest white-tailed deer herds in North America. In its fall forecast this year, Texas Parks and Wildlife estimated the state's white-tailed deer population at about five point six four million animals. Hundreds of thousands of hunters go to the field every season. Most of the deer they shoot will be examined by human eyes in a way no other wild animal in Texas ever is.

That's an extraordinary surveillance network, and wildlife officials know it.

Texas Parks and Wildlife has been asking hunters to look closely at the deer they harvest and at any deer they find, and to report suspicious wounds to their local wildlife biologist. It's a simple ask. Look at the animal. If you see an open wound with maggots in it, especially a cluster of larvae burrowed deep into living tissue, a wound that smells strongly, or larvae that look different from ordinary blowfly maggots, don't ignore it. Take pictures. Write down where you were. Make the call.

Here's why that matters.

So far, the reports out of Texas have centered on livestock and other domestic animals. Earlier this year, officials said there was no indication of confirmed screwworm cases in the state's deer or other wildlife. But wildlife is the hard part of any screwworm fight. A rancher can gather cattle, look at them, treat them, and keep records. No one can gather five and a half million deer. If the screwworm gets established in wild animals, it gets established in a population that no one can doctor, pen, or count with precision.

Wildlife specialists at Texas A&M have pointed out that white-tailed deer, coyotes, and feral hogs can all serve as hosts. Feral hogs alone number in the millions in Texas, and they roam widely, fight often, and get cut up regularly. Some experts have warned they could act as a kind of super-spreader if the screwworm takes hold in them. Deer, especially bucks during the rut, injure each other with antlers. Fawns are born with that open navel. Every one of those wounds is a potential doorway.

That's why the hunter kneeling in the frost is doing something important, even if he doesn't think of it that way. He's one point in a statewide early-warning system. If screwworm shows up in deer, it's likely a hunter, a landowner, or a biologist examining a carcass who will find it first.

And there's history here that makes Texans pay attention.

In 2016, the New World screwworm turned up in the Florida Keys, the first U.S. infestation in decades. It hit the endangered Key deer, a tiny subspecies of white-tailed deer found only in that chain of islands. The outbreak killed around a hundred and thirty-five Key deer before it was stopped through sterile fly releases, treatment of animals, and an all-out response by state and federal agencies. For a population that numbered only in the hundreds to around a thousand, it was a serious blow. And it showed what screwworm can do to deer in the modern era.

Texas is not the Florida Keys. The deer herd is vast. But that's exactly the worry. In a population that large, spread across so much private land, an infestation in wildlife could spread quietly for a long time before anyone notices.

So what are hunters actually doing this season?

They're looking at the deer. All of it. Not just the shot placement. They're checking the head, around the eyes and ears and the base of the antlers. They're checking the neck and shoulders, where bucks get gouged in fights. They're checking the legs, where wire fences cut. They're checking the belly and the navel. They're paying attention to any wound that looks deeper than it should, that has larvae packed into it, or that looks like living flesh has been eaten away.

They're learning to tell the difference between ordinary blowfly maggots and screwworm larvae. That isn't always easy in the field. Blowfly maggots usually feed on dead or decaying tissue and tend to be spread out on the surface. Screwworm larvae feed on living tissue and tend to burrow deep in a tight, packed group. But hunters aren't being asked to make a final diagnosis. They're being asked to notice, document, and report. Laboratory testing does the rest.

Some are carrying gloves and plastic bags in their hunting packs for the first time. Some landowners are adding questions to their camp routines. Did anyone see anything strange on that deer? Some leases are talking about it around the fire at night, which is how a lot of important information spreads in rural Texas.

And some are doing something even more practical. They're paying closer attention to how they care for dogs, horses, and livestock on hunting properties. A hunting dog that gets cut in the brush has an open wound too. Agencies have stressed that pets and working animals can be infested, and that prompt wound care matters.

There's also the matter of moving animals. Wildlife officials have urged caution about the movement of live animals, and anyone who keeps deer in captivity, transports livestock, or moves animals between properties has been asked to check them carefully. A single infested animal moved a long distance can carry the problem with it.

If you step back, there's something fascinating about this moment.

The original screwworm eradication was a triumph of government science, of entomologists, irradiation machines, and airplanes dropping boxes of flies. But it also depended on ranchers. People who rode their pastures looking for infested animals. People who collected larvae and sent them to labs so the program knew where the fly still was. People who helped pay for the work through their own associations.

The new fight looks similar. Sterile flies again. Federal and state agencies again. But the surveillance layer is different now, because so much of Texas is used for hunting. Every deer stand, every feeder, every trail camera, and every hunter cleaning a deer is part of how the state sees what's happening on private land. And the people who spend the most time looking at Texas wildlife up close, outside of biologists, are hunters.

That's why thousands of them are quietly checking every deer this season. Not because they've been forced to. Not because a law tells them to. But because they understand what's at stake. The deer they hunt today exist in these numbers partly because their grandparents' generation beat the screwworm. If it gets back into the deer herd, the next generation might not see the same Texas.

There's another reason, too, and it's less about deer and more about trust.

Hunting in Texas is built on private land, on relationships between landowners and hunters, ranchers and lessees. An outbreak on a property doesn't just threaten wildlife. It can threaten livestock, pets, and the business of the ranch itself. A hunter who finds something and reports it is protecting the place that lets him hunt. That's not a small thing in a state where access to land is everything.

It's worth being clear about what's known and what isn't.

As of the latest public counts, confirmed infestations in Texas this year have been concentrated in domestic animals, and officials had not reported confirmed cases in wild deer. The USDA and Texas agencies are publishing updated numbers through official channels, including the screwworm information pages run by the federal government and the Texas Animal Health Commission. The situation is changing, and anyone following it should check those official sources rather than rumors on social media.

What is known is the biology. The screwworm is a fly that lays eggs in wounds. Its larvae eat living tissue. Warm weather speeds it up. Cold weather slows it down, but it doesn't necessarily stop it in South Texas, where winters are mild. And the most powerful tool against it is still the one Knipling and Bushland invented more than seventy years ago. Sterile males, released in overwhelming numbers, combined with constant watching on the ground.

So the fight comes down to two things. Flies from the sky and eyes on the ground.

It helps to understand how the sterile fly method actually works in the field, because it explains why hunters' reports matter so much.

A sterile fly program doesn't kill flies directly. It changes the odds of reproduction. If a wild female mates with a sterile male, her eggs won't produce larvae. For that to work, sterile males have to vastly outnumber wild males, and they have to be released where the wild flies are. That means the people running the program need to know where infestations are happening. A confirmed case is a pin on a map. A cluster of cases is a target. Without reports from the ground, sterile flies are being dropped half blind.

That's why every report counts, including the ones that turn out to be nothing. A biologist who gets a call about a strange wound on a deer and has it tested learns something either way. If it's screwworm, the response zone shifts. If it isn't, the map gets a little clearer.

Production is the other half of the challenge. In the decades after eradication, the main sterile fly plant serving the barrier was located in Panama, through a partnership between the United States and Panama. When the screwworm moved north, that production capacity became a bottleneck. The response has included building and expanding facilities in Mexico and in Texas, so more sterile flies can be produced and released closer to where they're needed. Building that kind of infrastructure takes time, and in the meantime, surveillance on the ground is the best tool available.

There's also a simple, almost old-fashioned piece of advice that wildlife officials and veterinarians keep repeating. Treat wounds. If you have livestock, horses, or dogs, check them often during warm weather and treat any wound promptly with products approved for that use. Avoid unnecessary wounds, like routine procedures, during peak fly season when possible. Those were the same rules ranchers followed in the 1950s. They're back in the handbook again.

For hunters, the season itself offers a strange kind of advantage. Most of Texas deer season falls in the cooler months, when fly activity slows down. That means hunters are examining deer at a time when infestations might be less common, but it also means they're getting a baseline. If cases show up in wildlife, hunters who have spent a season checking every animal will be much more likely to recognize something wrong.

And there's one more group that deserves mention. Wildlife rehabilitators, veterinarians, game processors, and taxidermists all handle animals up close. A processor who skins dozens of deer a day sees more wounds than almost anyone. Agencies have reached out to those groups too, because the more trained eyes are on wildlife, the sooner an outbreak in deer would be found.

Let's go back to that hunter in the Hill Country.

He finishes checking the buck. The wounds are clean. A bullet hole. A small scrape on one shoulder from a fight earlier in the season, dry and healed. No larvae. Nothing moving. He sits back on his heels for a moment, breath fogging in the cold, and then gets on with the work of the morning.

Most of the time, that's how this ends. Nothing found. Nothing to report. A few extra minutes in the cold.

But that's the point. Surveillance only works if thousands of people are willing to spend those few extra minutes looking for something they hope they'll never find. Every clean deer is a data point. Every careful check is another piece of the map.

The screwworm was beaten once because ordinary people in ranch country refused to stop looking. More than half a century later, their grandchildren are kneeling in the same brush, checking the same kind of deer, looking for the same small, terrible thing.

And if Texas wins this fight again, the history books might talk about sterile fly factories and federal response plans. But a big part of the story will be written by hunters in the frost, rolling a deer onto its side, and taking a closer look.
