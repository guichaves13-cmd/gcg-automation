It's an hour before dawn on a ranch in South Texas, and the prickly pear flats are silver under a sinking moon. Out of the dark comes a sound most visitors never forget the first time they hear it: a sharp clacking, like someone snapping two pieces of wood together, followed by a low, rough grunt. Then the smell arrives, musky and sharp, drifting through the brush. A moment later, a line of small, bristly, dark-gray animals trots out of the thorns, one after another, a white band of hair across their shoulders like a collar. They spread out across the pear flat and begin to eat.

One of them is wearing a real collar. A narrow band with a small box on it, recording every move it makes.

Most people driving through South Texas would call these animals wild pigs. Plenty of people who live there would too. They'd be wrong. These are javelinas, collared peccaries. And for the ranchers who've watched them cross their land for generations, the data coming off those collars and the trail cameras set around them is revealing something many never suspected. Javelinas are not what most Texans think they are, and the way they live on a ranch is far more organized, and far more fragile, than it looks.

Let's start with the biggest misconception, because everything else depends on it.

A javelina is not a pig. It looks like one. It roots like one. It has a snout and a stocky body and short legs. But peccaries and pigs belong to different families that split apart a very long time ago. Pigs, including the feral hogs that cause so much damage across Texas, are Old World animals. Their ancestors came from Europe, Asia, and Africa, and they were brought to the Americas by people. Peccaries are native to the Americas. They've lived here for millions of years.

The differences are real, and you can see them if you know where to look. A javelina has straight, short tusks that point downward and lock against each other, which is part of what makes that clacking sound when the animal is alarmed or annoyed. A feral hog's tusks curve outward and up. A javelina has a scent gland on its back, near the rump, which it uses to mark territory and other members of its group. That's where the strong musky smell comes from. Feral hogs don't have it. Javelinas have a more complex stomach that helps them digest tough plant material, and they typically have small litters, often twins. Feral hogs can have large litters more than once a year.

That last difference matters enormously, and we'll come back to it.

The collared peccary ranges from the American Southwest down through Mexico and Central America and into South America. In the United States, they live in Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. In Texas, they're found mainly in the southern and western parts of the state, across the brush country, the Trans-Pecos, and the Edwards Plateau. Texas Parks and Wildlife manages them as a game animal, with hunting seasons and bag limits. They're not pests in the eyes of the law. They're native wildlife.

And yet many ranchers lump them in with feral hogs, which have very different consequences for the land. That's where the research comes in.

Researchers working through Texas A&M University have been studying javelinas across private ranches in South Texas, combining GPS collar data with camera surveys, helicopter surveys, observations from the public, and historical records. In a podcast released by the Texas A&M Natural Resources Institute this summer, wildlife scientist Dr. Stephen Webb described that work as part of a broader effort to understand big game on Texas private lands. The goal is simple to describe and hard to do. Figure out where javelinas are, how many there are, how they use the land, and whether their numbers are going up or down.

Because here's the thing. For an animal that so many Texans have seen, remarkably little is known about how javelina populations are doing across the state.

Let's talk about what the collars and cameras show.

The first thing is the group. Javelinas live in herds, sometimes called squadrons or sounders, often somewhere between a handful and a dozen or more animals, though group sizes vary. These are stable social units. The animals eat together, travel together, bed down together, and defend a shared home range. When you see one javelina, there are almost always others nearby.

That means a collar on one animal is, in a sense, a collar on the whole group. Where it goes, they go. And the tracks show something striking. The group's home range is relatively small and well defined. Instead of roaming widely across the landscape like a transient coyote or a young feral boar, a javelina herd tends to stay within an area it knows intimately. The tracks loop through the same thickets, the same pear flats, the same water sources, again and again.

That has a big implication. Javelinas are deeply tied to specific places. Lose the right habitat in a herd's home range, and the herd doesn't necessarily just pick up and move to the next county. It may struggle where it is.

The second thing the data shows is how much javelinas depend on cover. South Texas brush, mesquite, blackbrush, granjeno, and other thorny shrubs, isn't just scenery to a javelina. It's shelter from heat, from predators, and from people. The animals bed down in dense thickets during the hottest parts of the day. In the brutal Texas summer, they're often most active in early morning, late evening, and at night. In cooler weather, they may move more during the day. Cameras on water and feeders often catch them at dawn and dusk, the herd filing through in a tight line.

The third thing is the food. If you could design a single plant to feed a javelina, it would probably look a lot like prickly pear cactus. Javelinas eat the pads, spines and all, and the fruit, called tunas, when it ripens. Prickly pear provides both food and water in dry country. Javelinas also eat roots, tubers, mesquite beans, acorns, grasses, forbs, insects, and occasionally other foods they come across. But in much of South Texas, prickly pear is the backbone of their diet.

That has another implication that ranchers notice right away. Javelinas can be tied to the same landscape features that ranchers sometimes try to remove. For decades, brush clearing and cactus control were common practices on rangeland. They can improve grazing for cattle. But they also remove exactly the cover and food a javelina herd depends on. The animals and the land management are tangled together.

Now let's go back to that difference with feral hogs, because this is where many ranchers have their eyes opened.

When a trail camera catches a dark, bristly animal at a feeder at night, it's easy to assume it's a hog. And in a lot of Texas, it often is. Feral hogs are among the most destructive invasive animals in the country. Estimates commonly put the Texas population in the millions. They tear up pastures, destroy crops, foul water, and spread disease. Their reproductive rate is astonishing. A sow can breed young, have large litters, and produce more than one litter a year. Populations can grow fast even under heavy hunting pressure.

Javelinas are almost the opposite. They have small litters, commonly one or two young. They don't have the same explosive reproductive potential. And while javelinas do root and dig, they don't usually cause the same kind of large-scale damage feral hogs do.

So when a rancher looks at a camera image and sees two very different animals, one an invasive pest and one a native game species with limited ability to bounce back, that's not a small distinction. It's the difference between a population that can absorb heavy pressure and one that might not.

This is one of the quiet surprises in South Texas. Some landowners have been controlling javelinas the same way they control hogs, without realizing that the two species are not interchangeable at all.

And there's a deeper question underneath this that researchers are trying to answer. Are javelinas competing with feral hogs?

Think about it. Both animals use similar food in some places. Both use water. Both use thick cover. Feral hogs have spread across nearly every county in Texas in recent decades. Wherever hogs move in, they bring big numbers, aggressive foraging, and a willingness to take over the best spots. It's a reasonable question whether javelinas in some areas are being pushed aside. Studying how the two species use the same landscape, which cameras and collars make possible, is part of figuring that out.

It's not a settled question. But it's exactly the kind of thing that ranchers with cameras have been seeing for years. Feeders that used to be visited by javelina herds now dominated by hogs. Waterholes taken over. Herds that used to be regular showing up less often. Those observations aren't proof of anything on their own. But combined with collar data and careful surveys, they can become real science.

There's also the matter of how javelinas live in a hot, dry climate.

Javelinas don't handle cold particularly well. They're animals of warm country, and their range in the United States reflects that. But they also have to cope with extreme heat. One of the most important behaviors the collars capture is resting. A javelina herd spends a lot of time bedded down in shade, conserving energy and water, sometimes in caves, under rock overhangs, or in deep thickets. During hot, dry periods, their movements may shrink, and the herd may stay closer to reliable water and cactus. During a drought, the prickly pear itself can become the main source of moisture.

That makes javelinas a kind of living gauge of how the land is doing. When the pear flats are healthy and the brush is thick, herds can do well. When drought hits hard, or habitat is cleared, or water disappears, the herds feel it.

And Texas has had brutal droughts. The drought of 2011 was one of the worst single-year droughts in the state's recorded history. More recent years have brought repeated dry spells and extreme heat. Researchers want to understand how those conditions affect native animals like javelinas. A collar can show whether a herd shrinks its range, shifts its activity to the night, or moves to new water when conditions get tough.

Now let's talk about something that surprises people, the social side of javelinas.

The scent gland on the javelina's back isn't just for marking bushes. Members of a herd rub against each other, often standing head to tail and rubbing their faces against the gland of the other animal. This shared scent helps hold the group together. In thick brush, with poor eyesight, a javelina relies heavily on smell and sound to keep track of its herd. That clacking of teeth and those grunts are part of a whole vocabulary.

The herd also has a reputation, partly deserved and partly exaggerated. People sometimes describe javelinas as aggressive. In reality, they usually want nothing to do with humans. Their eyesight is relatively poor, and a startled herd may scatter in several directions at once, sometimes toward a person, which can look like a charge. They will defend themselves, and they'll certainly go after dogs, which they may see as predators. But for the most part, a javelina herd is a group of nervous, near-sighted animals trying to stay together and stay out of trouble.

Predators include coyotes, bobcats, and mountain lions. A young javelina is vulnerable. The herd's defense is the group itself, the noise, the scattering, the tusks of adults.

So what did ranchers in South Texas not know before the collars and cameras started recording?

Many of them didn't know how small and fixed a javelina herd's home range can be. They didn't know how closely the animals' lives are tied to prickly pear and thick brush. They didn't fully realize how differently javelinas reproduce compared to the feral hogs that share the same cameras. And many didn't realize that, for all the javelinas they'd seen over the years, nobody had a clear picture of how the species was really doing across the state.

That last point is the heart of why this research exists.

Javelinas are a game animal. Hunters pursue them. Landowners lease hunting rights for them. Managing any game species well requires knowing roughly how many there are, where they are, and whether the population is stable. For white-tailed deer, Texas has decades of survey data. For javelinas, the picture has been much fuzzier. That's partly because they live in thick brush, in herds, on private land, which makes them hard to count.

Which is why approaches that combine methods are so valuable. GPS collars show how individual herds move. Camera surveys capture which animals use which areas and when. Helicopter surveys can give broad counts over large areas of open country. Reports from landowners and the public fill in the gaps. Historical records give a sense of how things used to be. Put all that together and a picture starts to form.

And that picture matters beyond javelinas themselves. A native animal with a low reproductive rate, living in herds, tied to particular habitat, sharing the land with an exploding invasive species, is a kind of test case. How it does may tell you something about the health of the whole South Texas brush ecosystem.

There's also a lesson here about trail cameras and ordinary landowners.

A generation ago, most ranchers knew the animals on their land through tracks, sightings, and stories. Today, many have dozens of cameras running every night. That has changed how people see wildlife. A rancher scrolling through photos might see a javelina herd file past a feeder at five in the morning, followed an hour later by a sounder of feral hogs, followed by a coyote, a bobcat, and a white-tailed buck. It's a parade that was always there, but almost nobody saw all of it before.

When those landowners share what they see with researchers, the cameras become more than a hobby. They become a statewide sensor network for wildlife, the kind no government agency could ever afford to build on its own.

And sometimes, what those cameras expose is that the animal you've been treating as a pest isn't one at all.

It's worth pausing on why GPS collars are so hard to use on an animal like this, because it explains why the data is so valuable when it works.

A collar has to fit a neck that is short and thick, on an animal that spends its life pushing through thorns. Brush tears at equipment. Batteries have to last through heat that can push past a hundred degrees. Satellites have to see the collar through dense canopy, and when a herd beds down deep in a thicket, a collar may struggle to get a clean position. Research on feral swine in Texas has shown just how hard this can be. A Texas A&M study on GPS collars on feral hogs reported a failure rate of more than fifty percent in collar performance across its deployments. Javelinas live in the same kind of country, with many of the same challenges.

Then there's the problem of catching them in the first place. Javelinas are wary, and they move as a group. Trapping one usually means trapping several, handling them carefully, and releasing them together so the herd isn't broken apart. Every collared animal represents hours of fieldwork, the cooperation of a landowner, and a fair amount of luck.

That's why cameras are such an important partner to collars. A camera doesn't need to catch an animal. It just needs to be in the right place. Put enough of them across a ranch, and you can see which areas javelina herds use, how big the groups are, whether young are present, and how often hogs, deer, and predators use the same spots. Researchers have increasingly turned to camera grids as a way to estimate wildlife populations, and the methods for doing that have become more sophisticated over the past twenty years.

Helicopter surveys add a third angle. Texas has a long tradition of counting deer from the air in South Texas brush country, where open spaces between thickets make aerial counts possible. Javelina herds, moving together, can sometimes be counted the same way. No single method gives the full answer. But when collars, cameras, aerial counts, and local knowledge line up, researchers can say something with more confidence.

And there's one more source of information that's easy to overlook. Hunters. Every javelina harvested and reported, every observation from a deer blind, every photo shared with a biologist, adds to the record. For a species that has been surprisingly hard to track, ordinary people in the field are part of the science.

There's also a history worth remembering. In the early twentieth century, javelinas were hunted heavily in Texas, including for their hides, which were valued for leather. Numbers in some areas dropped. Over time, regulations and changes in land use helped the species hold on. That history is part of why biologists don't take javelina numbers for granted today. Populations of slow-breeding animals can decline quietly, and by the time people notice, recovery can take a long time.

Let's go back to that pear flat before dawn.

The javelina herd works its way across the cactus, pulling down pads, crunching through the spines as if they're not there. The collared animal stays in the middle of the group. A young one stays close to its mother. The sky goes gray in the east. A coyote calls somewhere off to the south, and the herd pauses, heads up, noses working. Then the clacking starts again, quietly, and the group turns as one back toward the brush. By the time the sun clears the mesquite, they'll be bedded down in a thicket they've used many times before.

On the ranch, almost no one will see them. But the collar will. It'll mark the bedding site. It'll mark the trail. It'll mark the herd's slow loop through the same small piece of South Texas they've known all their lives.

And somewhere in a database, another line will be added to the story of an animal that has lived in this country for millions of years, long before the first feral hog stepped off a ship, long before there were ranches or fences or roads.

For generations, many Texans looked at javelinas and saw a wild pig. The collars and cameras are showing them something else. Not a pig. Not a pest. A native survivor, holding on in the brush, quietly depending on the cactus, the thorns, and the patience of the people who share the land with it.

The question now isn't whether ranchers will keep seeing javelinas on their cameras. It's whether, once they understand what they're looking at, they'll decide this is an animal worth making room for.
