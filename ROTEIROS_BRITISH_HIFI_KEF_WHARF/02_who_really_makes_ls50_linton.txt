Turn your speaker round. Go on, if you've got one nearby, have a look at the back.

There's a little label there. Most people never read it. It's got the model name, a serial number, some safety marks. And somewhere on that label, in tiny letters, there are three or four words that a lot of British owners would rather not see.

Two speakers in particular are on more British wish lists right now than almost anything else. The KEF LS50 Wireless — the little speaker with the copper-coloured driver that every reviewer seems to love. And the Wharfedale Linton — the big, warm, walnut box that looks like it walked straight out of a 1970s living room in Harrogate.

One sells the future. One sells the past. Both sell Britain.

So today I want to answer a simple question. Who actually makes them? Not who owns the logo. Not who writes the brochure. Who designs them, who builds them, where they come from, and how much of the "British" story is still true in 2026.

Because the answer has a twist I genuinely didn't expect. And it changes how you should think about buying either one.

[SECTION 1 — TWO SPEAKERS, TWO MYTHS]

Let's start with the myths, because every great British brand has one.

KEF's myth goes like this. 1961. Raymond Cooke, an engineer who'd worked at Wharfedale, of all places, sets up a new company in a Nissen hut on the grounds of a metalwork firm in Maidstone, Kent. Kent Engineering and Foundry — that's where the name comes from, K-E-F. He experiments with new materials, new driver designs, and within a few decades KEF is one of the most respected speaker engineering companies in the world. The BBC uses their drivers. The legendary LS3/5a monitor, the speaker that half of British hi-fi worships, used KEF drivers inside.

Wharfedale's myth is even older. 1932. Gilbert Briggs, a textile man and keen amateur pianist from Yorkshire, starts building loudspeakers in his cellar in Ilkley. He names the company after the valley — Wharfedale. He goes on to run famous live-versus-recorded demonstrations at the Royal Festival Hall in London and in Carnegie Hall in New York, proving that a loudspeaker could fool an audience. He writes books that teach a whole generation how speakers work.

Beautiful stories. And they're true. That's the important bit. Both brands have genuine, world-class British history.

The question is: how much of that history lives inside an LS50 Wireless or a Linton you can buy today?

[SECTION 2 — WHO OWNS KEF?]

Let's do KEF first.

KEF hasn't been a British-owned company for a long time. In the early 1990s, it was bought by the Gold Peak Group, a Hong Kong industrial company. Gold Peak is best known for making batteries — GP batteries, you've probably got some in a drawer somewhere.

So for more than thirty years, KEF has been owned from Hong Kong.

Now here's where it gets interesting. Gold Peak didn't strip KEF for parts and stick the badge on cheap boxes, which is what happened to quite a few British brands. They kept the engineering in Maidstone. KEF still has its research and development team there. The Uni-Q driver — the clever design where the tweeter sits in the throat of the midrange cone — has been developed and refined in Kent for decades. The famous "Metamaterial Absorption Technology", that maze-like disc behind the tweeter, was developed by KEF's engineers working with a materials company.

So the brains — or a big part of them — are still British.

The hands, though, are mostly not.

[SECTION 3 — WHERE IS THE LS50 WIRELESS BUILT?]

The LS50 Wireless, the LS50 Wireless II, the LSX, the LS60 — like most of KEF's range, these speakers are manufactured in China.

KEF's parent group has deep manufacturing roots in southern China, and that's where the volume work happens. The cabinets, the driver assembly, the electronics, the final testing.

Now, before anyone gets angry in the comments — this isn't a scandal, and KEF has never really hidden it. It's on the label. It's in the paperwork. The hi-fi press has known for years.

But here's the twist I promised you. With a wireless speaker like the LS50 Wireless, "who makes it" is a much more complicated question than with a normal speaker. Because a wireless speaker isn't just a box with drivers. It's got amplifiers inside. A digital signal processor. A streaming module. Wi-Fi. Bluetooth. An app.

So when you ask who makes the LS50 Wireless, the honest answer is: dozens of companies. The drivers are a KEF design. The amplification and the digital electronics involve chips and modules from big international semiconductor suppliers — the same kinds of companies that supply phones and laptops. The software is written, updated and maintained by teams that aren't necessarily sitting anywhere near Maidstone.

In other words: the LS50 Wireless is a British-designed loudspeaker, built in China, running on a global electronics supply chain.

And that's why I keep saying, on this channel, that buying a wireless speaker is closer to buying a phone than buying a traditional hi-fi product. Its future isn't decided only by KEF's engineers. It's decided by chip suppliers, streaming platforms and app stores.

[SECTION 4 — WHO OWNS WHARFEDALE?]

Now Wharfedale. And this one surprises more people.

Wharfedale went through several owners in the second half of the twentieth century. At one point it was part of the Rank Organisation — yes, the film company. Later it became part of the Verity Group in Britain, alongside other names like Mission and Quad.

Then, in the mid-2000s, the Wharfedale brand and its sister brands ended up as part of the International Audio Group, IAG. IAG is a Chinese-owned group, and it's huge. It also owns Quad, Mission, Audiolab and Castle. So a whole family of names that British audiophiles think of as "proper British hi-fi" sits under the same roof.

And IAG doesn't just own the brands. It owns the factories. IAG has its own large manufacturing operation in Shenzhen, in southern China. That's where most Wharfedale speakers — the Diamonds, the Evos, and yes, the Linton — are built.

[SECTION 5 — THE LINTON TWIST]

So here's the twist about the Linton. And it's a good one.

The original Linton was a Wharfedale model from the 1960s and seventies. A proper British living-room speaker, built in Yorkshire, that a lot of families owned. When Wharfedale relaunched the Linton a few years ago as the "Linton Heritage", it was deliberately designed to look like the old one. The wood, the grille, the shape, the stands with the little record storage built in. It's pure nostalgia, and it's very good at it.

But the new Linton isn't a reissue of the old Linton. It's a completely new speaker, designed with modern drivers, modern crossovers and modern measurement tools, and built in IAG's Chinese facilities.

And here's the bit nobody really talks about. The man most associated with Wharfedale's modern speaker design for many years, Peter Comeau, is a British engineer. He's been the director of acoustic design at IAG and has worked on Wharfedale, Mission and Quad designs. So, again, a lot of the brain behind the Linton is British.

So the Linton is a British-voiced, British-designed tribute to a British classic, owned by a Chinese group and built in a Chinese factory.

Is that a bad thing? Honestly, I don't think it is. I think it might be the only reason the Linton exists at all. Building a speaker that size, with that much real wood veneer, at that price, in a Yorkshire factory in 2026? It simply wouldn't be possible. You'd be looking at double the price, maybe more.

The Chinese factory is what makes the nostalgia affordable.

[SECTION 6 — SO WHY DOES IT SHOCK PEOPLE?]

So if none of this is secret, why do so many UK buyers react with shock when they find out?

I think it's because of the way these speakers are marketed. Not lies — nobody's lying. But the emphasis. You see the history. You see the founder. You see the Yorkshire valley and the Kent workshop. You see the old black-and-white photographs. And your brain fills in the rest.

You assume. And most people never turn the speaker round.

It's the same thing that happens with British car brands. Plenty of famous British names now have foreign owners, and many models are built abroad or assembled from global parts. People know that in theory, but it still stings a little when they actually think about it.

There's a word for that feeling in marketing circles. It's called "heritage gap" — the distance between the story a brand tells and the reality of how the product is made. When the gap is small, people don't care. When it's large, they feel a bit fooled, even if nobody technically fooled them.

And with KEF and Wharfedale in 2026, I think a lot of British buyers are noticing that gap for the first time.

[SECTION 7 — DOES IT AFFECT THE SOUND?]

Now, the most important question. Does any of this actually make the speakers sound worse?

Short answer: no. Not really.

A modern Chinese speaker factory can be extremely good. Tolerances can be tight. Quality control can be excellent. Some of the most expensive, most respected loudspeakers in the world are built in China now.

The LS50 Wireless II sounds superb. Reviewers loved it, owners love it. The Linton sounds big, rich, and easy to live with, exactly what it was designed to do.

But — and this is a big but — where it does matter is in two places.

The first is longevity. A passive speaker like the Linton can be repaired by almost anyone. Drivers can be replaced. Crossovers can be rebuilt. It's a box with wood and wire and magnets. It can last decades.

An active wireless speaker like the LS50 Wireless is different. If the electronics fail out of warranty, you're relying on the manufacturer having spare boards, and on the software being supported. That's not a criticism of KEF specifically — it's true of every wireless speaker from every brand.

The second place it matters is value. Part of what you pay for with a British heritage brand is the story. And if the story is only partly true, then part of what you're paying for is, well, a feeling.

[SECTION 7B — WHY THE LS50 EXISTS AT ALL]

There's a piece of this story I think most people miss, and it makes the "who really makes it" question even more interesting.

The original LS50 — the passive one, before the wireless version — was launched in 2012 for KEF's fiftieth anniversary. And it was designed as a tribute. A tribute to the LS3/5a, the little BBC monitor from the 1970s.

The LS3/5a is the holy grail of British hi-fi. It was designed by the BBC's own engineers for checking sound in outside broadcast vans. Tiny box, honest sound, especially brilliant with voices. And inside the original, the drivers were made by KEF, in Britain.

So when KEF launched the LS50, the whole idea was: here's the spirit of the BBC monitor, reborn with modern technology. The name even nods to it.

Think about that for a second. A speaker built to celebrate a British broadcasting legend, whose drivers were made in Kent — and the tribute version is built in China, by a company owned from Hong Kong, and the wireless edition depends on software and chips from all over the world.

That, to me, is the perfect picture of what British hi-fi has become. The idea is British. The memory is British. The engineering intent is British. Almost everything else is global.

And again — I'm not saying that's wrong. I'm saying it's worth knowing.

[SECTION 7C — WHAT A MODERN SPEAKER FACTORY ACTUALLY LOOKS LIKE]

When people hear "made in China", a lot of them still picture something from the 1990s. Cheap plastic, rushed assembly, no checks.

That isn't what a serious speaker factory looks like in 2026.

A modern speaker plant is closer to a laboratory than a workshop. Cabinets are cut by computer-controlled machines to fractions of a millimetre. Drivers are measured individually and matched in pairs, so the left and right speakers sound as close to identical as possible. There are anechoic chambers — rooms with no echo at all — where finished speakers are tested against a reference. Electronics are checked by automated systems before they go anywhere near a box.

Many of the people working there are highly trained. Some of the engineers have worked on speakers for multiple world-famous brands. And that's the uncomfortable bit for British pride. In a lot of cases, the manufacturing expertise for "British" speakers now genuinely lives in southern China. Not because anyone wanted it there, but because that's where the investment went over the last thirty years.

When I talk to people in the trade, they'll often say the same thing quietly: the factories in China are better equipped than most British factories ever were.

So the question isn't really "is it built well?" It usually is. The question is "what am I paying the premium for?" And that brings us back to the story.

[SECTION 7D — A QUICK BUYER'S CHECKLIST]

Before we get to the final verdict, let me give you a quick checklist. Screenshot this if you're shopping.

If you're buying an LS50 Wireless, new or used:
One — check which generation it is. The second generation is newer and has better streaming support.
Two — if it's used, ask the seller to show you the app working, on video, before you travel.
Three — ask whether it's had firmware updates, and whether there have ever been connection drop-outs.
Four — check the warranty status. With active speakers, warranty is worth real money.

If you're buying a Linton:
One — check the stands are included, because they're part of the sound and the look.
Two — check the veneer for knocks. Real wood looks lovely, but it shows damage.
Three — remember they're heavy. Bring a friend, and a car with room.
Four — and this is the big one — budget for a decent amplifier. The Linton rewards a good one.

And for both: look at the label. Know what you're buying. Then decide if it's worth it to you.

[SECTION 8 — THE REAL "BRITISH" SPEAKER TEST]

So how do you actually work out how British a speaker is? I've got a simple three-question test you can use on any brand.

Question one. Where is it designed? Not where's the head office — where do the acoustic engineers actually work, and are they named? KEF scores well here. Wharfedale does reasonably well too.

Question two. Where is it built? Look at the label. Ask the dealer. Not the box art, not the website story. The label.

Question three. Who owns it, and do they invest in the brand, or just use the name? This is the one that separates the good foreign owners from the bad ones. KEF's owners have kept serious research going for over thirty years. That counts for something.

If a brand scores well on design and investment, even if it's built abroad, I'd personally call that an honest British-designed product. If a brand only has the old name and nothing else, that's a badge, not a brand.

[SECTION 9 — THE ONES THAT ARE STILL BUILT HERE]

And just to be fair to British manufacturing, it isn't completely dead. There are still smaller companies building speakers in this country. Some of the BBC-style monitor specialists. Some boutique makers. Some of the high-end brands that build their top models in Britain while making the cheaper models abroad.

But you'll notice something. They're almost all expensive. Building in Britain costs a lot more. So, if "made in Britain" really matters to you, the honest truth is you'll probably pay a lot more for it — or you'll go vintage.

And that's exactly why old British-built KEFs and Wharfedales from the seventies and eighties are getting more attention. They're the last generation that was designed and built here, start to finish.

[SECTION 10 — SO SHOULD YOU BUY THEM?]

So, if you're a British buyer thinking about an LS50 Wireless or a Linton right now, here's my honest advice.

Buy them for what they are, not for what the brochure makes you feel.

The LS50 Wireless is a brilliant British-designed, globally-built, software-dependent speaker. Buy it if you want convenience and great sound in a small package. But understand you're buying a piece of technology with a lifespan, like a phone or a laptop. Don't buy it expecting to hand it down to your grandchildren.

The Linton is a lovely British-voiced, Chinese-built, nostalgia speaker at a price that wouldn't be possible otherwise. Buy it if you want that warm, big, old-school sound and look. It's passive, it's repairable, and it can last a very long time.

And if what you really want is the history — the actual Yorkshire wood, the actual Kent workshop — then the honest answer is: look at the second-hand market for the originals. That's where the real British story still lives.
