Two of the most famous names in British loudspeakers. One founded in a Nissen hut in Kent in 1961. The other started by a man in a Yorkshire cellar in 1932. Between them, they've been on more British sideboards, bookshelves and student desks than probably any other speakers in history.

So why, in 2026, are so many British owners quietly trying to get rid of them? And the even stranger question, the one nobody in the hi-fi press seems to want to ask out loud: why is it suddenly so hard to sell them?

Stay with me, because the answer isn't what most people think. It's not that the speakers got bad. In some ways it's worse than that. [SECTION 1 — THE SCENE IN 2026]

Let me set the scene properly, because I think a lot of people outside the hobby don't realise how big this shift is.

For about forty years, the British hi-fi market had a simple rule. You bought a good pair of British speakers, you looked after them, and they held their money. Not perfectly, but well. A pair of KEF bookshelf speakers you bought for four hundred pounds might sell for two-fifty, three hundred, ten years later. A pair of old Wharfedales from the seventies? People actually paid more for those than they cost new, once you adjusted for nostalgia.

That was the deal. British speakers were the sensible purchase. The thing your dad told you to buy. Not flashy. Not trendy. Just solid.

Now, here's what I've been noticing in conversations with sellers, on forums, in the comments under my own videos. That rule isn't holding anymore. Not the way it used to.

People are telling me they've listed their speakers and had nothing. No messages for a week. Then one message, and it's a lowball. "I'll give you half." For speakers that might be two years old.

And when the market for something used starts to look like that, you've seen this pattern before. Not in hi-fi. In cars.

[SECTION 2 — THE CAR PATTERN]

If you watch any British car channels, you'll know the titles. "Nobody wants these anymore." "This car has lost eighty percent of its value." "The used market is collapsing." "Never buy one of these."

And it's always the same story. A product that was sold as the smart, safe choice. Then the technology moves on. Then the software stops being updated, or the battery gets old, or the manufacturer changes direction. And suddenly the used buyer — the person who keeps the whole value chain alive — just walks away.

Electric cars from a few years ago are the obvious example. Brilliant when new. Then three years later, everyone's worried about the battery, the software, the range compared to the new models. And the second-hand values fall off a cliff.

Now, I want you to hold that idea in your head. Because I think something very similar is happening to a certain kind of British speaker. And it's happening for almost exactly the same reasons.

[SECTION 3 — THE WIRELESS TRAP]

Let's start with KEF, because KEF is where this is most obvious.

Over the last decade, KEF made a very clever bet. They took their famous Uni-Q driver — the one where the tweeter sits right in the middle of the mid-bass cone — and they put it into active, wireless speakers. The LS50 Wireless. Then the LS50 Wireless II. The LSX, then the LSX II. The LS60.

And honestly? They're superb. I'm not going to sit here and tell you they sound bad. They don't. You plug them into the wall, connect your phone, and you've got a proper hi-fi system without a separate amplifier, without a CD player, without a tangle of cables.

But here's the thing nobody really said out loud when they launched. When you buy a passive speaker — just a box with drivers and a crossover — you're buying something that can last forty years. There's nothing in it to go out of date. No app. No firmware. No streaming platform that might change its rules.

When you buy a wireless active speaker, you're buying a speaker and a computer glued together. And computers age.

The amplifiers are inside. The streaming board is inside. The app lives on your phone. And every year, the phones change, the streaming services change, the standards change.

So now picture the used buyer in 2026. They're looking at a pair of first-generation LS50 Wireless on a listings site. And they're thinking — not "how do these sound?" — but "will the app still work next year? What happens if the board fails? Can anyone even repair this?"

That one question kills the price. Because the used buyer isn't buying sound anymore. They're buying risk.

And that, I think, is the single biggest reason people are trying to dump them now, while they still can.

[SECTION 4 — WHARFEDALE'S DIFFERENT PROBLEM]

Now Wharfedale is a different story. And in some ways, a sadder one.

Wharfedale's problem isn't software. Most Wharfedales are good old-fashioned passive speakers. The Diamond range. The Evo range. The Linton, which came back as a retro, wooden, old-school speaker that a lot of people genuinely love.

Wharfedale's problem is something else. It's value.

The Diamond series has been, for years, one of the best-value speaker ranges you can buy in the UK. That's the whole selling point. Cheap, cheerful, surprisingly good. And because they're so affordable new, and because there's a new generation every few years, the second-hand market gets absolutely flooded.

Think about it. If a brand-new pair of Diamonds costs not much more than a nice meal out for two, and there's often a discount on top, why would anyone pay decent money for a used pair from the previous generation?

They won't. And that's what sellers are running into. You list your Diamond 12s, and the buyer just looks at the new price, looks at the next generation, and laughs.

The Linton has a slightly different issue. It's a bigger, heavier, more expensive speaker. It sold very well on nostalgia — that seventies look, the walnut, the grille. But nostalgia is a funny thing. It fills a market fast, and then it saturates it. A lot of people who wanted a Linton bought one in the first few years. Now some of them are upgrading, downsizing, moving house, and the listings are piling up.

And then there's the question that you, my viewers, keep asking in the comments. Who actually makes Wharfedale now? I did a whole video on that, and the response was huge. Because a lot of British buyers felt like they'd been buying a Yorkshire brand, and then discovered the story was more complicated. I'll come back to that in the next video, because it deserves its own deep dive.

[SECTION 5 — THE IDENTITY QUESTION]

Which brings me to the part I think really matters.

Why did British people buy KEF and Wharfedale in the first place?

It wasn't only the sound. Let's be honest with ourselves. It was the story. It was the feeling of buying British. Of buying something with history. KEF in Maidstone. Wharfedale in Yorkshire. Raymond Cooke. Gilbert Briggs. Engineers who basically invented modern speaker design in this country.

KEF has been owned by a Hong Kong company, Gold Peak, since the early nineties. Wharfedale has been part of a Chinese-owned group, IAG, for many years now. And most of the speakers, from both brands, are manufactured in China.

Now, I want to be fair here. That doesn't make them bad speakers. Some of the best engineering in the world happens in Chinese factories. KEF still does serious research and design work in Maidstone. Wharfedale still has British engineering involvement.

But for a lot of older British buyers, when they find out, something changes. The emotional premium goes. And the emotional premium was a big part of why these speakers held their value.

When the story goes, the second-hand price follows it down.

[SECTION 6 — WHAT THE NUMBERS FEEL LIKE]

Now, I'm not going to throw made-up statistics at you. I don't have a magic database of every sale in Britain. But I can tell you what the pattern looks like, from what sellers are reporting and what I can see in the listings.

Older wireless KEFs: the used price seems to be falling faster than passive speakers of the same age. Sometimes much faster. Because of that software risk.

Budget Wharfedales: there are simply so many of them that the price is set by the cheapest seller who's desperate to clear space.

Lintons: decent demand still, but more competition between sellers than a couple of years ago.

And classic, vintage, passive KEFs and Wharfedales from the seventies and eighties? This is the twist. Many of those are holding up very well. In some cases, they're the most wanted of all.

Isn't that strange? The forty-year-old speaker is easier to sell than the four-year-old one.

But it makes perfect sense once you understand it. The old one has no software. It was made in Britain. It has a story. It can be repaired with a soldering iron. The used buyer trusts it.

[SECTION 7 — SO WHO'S BUYING INSTEAD?]

So if British audiophiles are moving away from these models, where are they going?

From what you've been telling me, there are a few groups.

First, the people going back to passive. They're selling their wireless speakers, buying a separate amplifier and a pair of good passive speakers, and saying "never again" to anything with an app. They want something they can keep for twenty years.

Second, the vintage crowd. They're buying old British speakers — including old KEFs and old Wharfedales — getting them refurbished, and running them with modern streamers. Ironically, they're still buying KEF and Wharfedale. Just not the new ones.

Third, the people going smaller and cheaper. Cost of living hasn't gone away. A lot of people simply can't afford to upgrade the way they did in the past, so they're selling whatever they have to raise cash, which adds even more listings to the market.

And fourth — and this is the group the big brands should worry about — the people who've stopped caring about the badge altogether. They'll buy whatever measures well and costs least. British, Chinese, Danish, doesn't matter. The brand story means nothing to them.

Let me tell you about three people. I've changed the details a bit so nobody gets recognised, but the stories are very real, and they're the kind I get in my inbox every week.

The first is a retired engineer from the Midlands. Let's call him Peter. Peter bought a pair of first-generation LS50 Wireless a few years after they launched. He loved them. Factory resets, forums, YouTube tutorials. He got it working in the end. But something had changed in his head. He said to me: "For the first time in my life, I owned a pair of speakers I didn't trust."

Peter listed them a month later. He told me the messages he got were all the same. "Does the app still work?" "Has the board been replaced?" "Any connection problems?" Not one person asked how they sounded. He sold them in the end, for a lot less than he'd hoped. And he bought a pair of second-hand passive speakers and a little amplifier. He says he's never been happier.

The second is a young couple in Leeds. They bought a pair of Wharfedale Diamonds when they moved into their first flat. Lovely speakers, did the job brilliantly. Then they had a baby, needed the space, and tried to sell them. They told me they listed them for what they thought was a fair price, and got nothing. Not a single message for two weeks. They dropped the price. Then dropped it again. In the end they gave them to her brother. "It wasn't worth the hassle," she said. "There were twenty other pairs on there, all the same."

And the third story is the one that really got me thinking. A gentleman in Somerset — call him David — who'd had a pair of Wharfedales from the late seventies in his loft. His father's speakers. People driving from two counties away. He sold them for more than the young couple in Leeds had paid for their brand-new Diamonds.

Same brand. Forty years apart. Completely opposite outcomes.

That, in a nutshell, is the story of British hi-fi in 2026.

[SECTION 7C — THE REVIEW CYCLE NOBODY TALKS ABOUT]

There's one more thing I want to mention, because I think it quietly makes all of this worse.

Think about how speakers are reviewed and sold now. A new model comes out. The magazines and the websites review it. The YouTube channels get a pair, often on loan from the brand. There's a wave of excitement. Five stars. Award winner. Best buy.

Then, a year or two later, the next model comes out. Same cycle. Five stars. Award winner. And the review of the new model almost always says something like "an improvement on the excellent previous version" — which, if you think about it, is a very polite way of telling everyone that the previous version is now second best.

And who pays for that sentence? The person trying to sell the previous version.

In the old days, a great speaker might stay in the range for ten or fifteen years. People knew what it was. It had a reputation that didn't expire. Now, the product cycle is so fast that a speaker can go from "best buy" to "old model" in the time it takes to pay off a credit card.

It's exactly what happened with phones. And with cars, again. Every new model year makes the old one feel ancient, even when it isn't.

The brands need that cycle to keep selling new stock. I understand why. But it's the loyal British customer — the one who bought the award winner in good faith — who ends up holding the bill when they come to sell.

And I think a lot of people have finally noticed that. Which is why some of them are getting out now, before the next "excellent successor" makes their speakers worth even less.

[SECTION 8 — SHOULD YOU SELL?]

So, the practical bit. If you own a pair of KEFs or Wharfedales right now, what should you do?

Here's my honest take.

If you own wireless KEFs, and they're working, and you love them — keep them. Use them. Enjoy them. But understand that their resale value is tied to the software and electronics inside. If you're thinking of selling at some point anyway, sooner is probably better than later. Every new generation makes the old one look older.

If you own budget Wharfedales, honestly, the resale value was never going to be huge. Don't panic-sell them for pennies. They're good speakers. Keep them, give them to a family member, use them in a second room.

If you own Lintons, or higher-end Wharfedales, you're in a middle position. There's still a buyer for them, but you'll need patience and a fair price.

And if you own vintage British KEFs or Wharfedales, made in this country, in good condition — hold on. Those might be the most solid hi-fi investment you have.

And if you're buying? This is actually a brilliant time. The panic of other sellers is your opportunity. Just do your homework, check the electronics if they're wireless, and never pay the asking price on the first message.

[SECTION 9 — THE BIGGER STORY]

I want to finish with something bigger than a used price.

What's happening to KEF and Wharfedale in 2026 isn't really about two brands. It's about what happened to British hi-fi as a whole. We went from making things to licensing names. From factories in Kent and Yorkshire to design offices and distribution deals. From speakers that lasted a lifetime to speakers with an app that might not.

And British buyers — especially the older ones who remember the golden age — are starting to notice. That's what this wave of selling is, in the end. It's not anger. It's disappointment. It's people realising the thing they thought they were buying isn't quite the thing they got.

But it isn't all bad news. KEF still engineers some of the most impressive drivers in the world. Wharfedale still makes some of the best-value speakers on the planet. The sound is real. Only the myth has changed.
