Number ten. Musical Fidelity.

Founded in London in the early 1980s by Antony Michaelson, a clarinettist who started out building amplifiers with a very musical character. Picture the classic Musical Fidelity A1, a slim integrated amp with a top plate that got so hot you could famously warm your hands on it. For decades, it was a proudly British, founder-led company.

In 2018, Musical Fidelity was acquired by Audio Tuning, the Austrian company led by Heinz Lichtenegger, best known as the man behind Pro-Ject turntables. So, the brand lives on, and its products are still sold widely, but the company is now part of an Austrian group. That's not a disaster. Pro-Ject is a well-respected maker. But a name that once meant London now answers to Vienna.

Number nine. Mission.

Mission was founded in 1977 in England, and through the eighties and nineties it was one of the most familiar names in British bedroom and living-room hi-fi. Picture a pair of Mission 700 series speakers on a pine bookshelf next to a stack of cassettes, or the striking Cyrus electronics that began life as a Mission spin-off.

Mission today belongs to the International Audio Group, often shortened to IAG, which is based in China. IAG has gathered together several classic British brands, and Mission is one of them. The design heritage is still referenced. But the ownership, and much of the manufacturing, sits outside Britain.

Number eight. Wharfedale.

This one goes back to 1932, when Gilbert Briggs started building speakers in Yorkshire. The brand is named after the valley of the River Wharfe. Picture the old Wharfedale factory, rows of craftsmen fitting drive units into cabinets, all of it rooted in the Yorkshire Dales. Wharfedale became one of the biggest-selling British speaker brands of all time.

Wharfedale is also now part of IAG. It still sells a lot of speakers, and its affordable Diamond range has been a bestseller for years, but the company that once embodied northern English engineering is now owned in China.

Number seven. Quad.

Quad is one of the most respected names in audio history. It was founded by Peter Walker in the 1930s, and in 1957 it launched the Quad Electrostatic Loudspeaker, a strange, flat, almost radiator-like panel that many listeners still consider one of the most transparent speakers ever made. Picture a pair of them standing at an angle in a quiet listening room, light coming through the brown mesh grille, playing a string quartet so cleanly you'd swear the players were there.

Quad, too, became part of IAG. Its heritage is still celebrated, and IAG continues to make electrostatic speakers under the name, but the Walker family business from Huntingdon is a long way from where it started.

Number six. Leak.

Leak was founded by Harold Leak in London in the 1930s and became famous for valve amplifiers, especially the TL12 and the Stereo 20, which audio enthusiasts still restore and use today. Picture a glowing row of valves on a black chassis, a little bit of hum, and the warm orange light on a winter evening.

The original company faded out decades ago. The name was then revived by IAG, which relaunched the brand in 2020 with retro-styled amplifiers and CD players in walnut cases. So it's a British name with a real heritage, now brought back to life by a Chinese-owned group.

Number five. Tannoy.

Tannoy is so British that its name became a word. Tannoy, meaning a public address system. Founded in London in the 1920s, it later became famous for its dual-concentric drivers, where the tweeter sits inside the middle of the main cone, and for huge monitor speakers used in studios. In later decades, its production base was in Coatbridge, near Glasgow. Picture a giant Tannoy Westminster speaker cabinet, the size of a wardrobe, finished in walnut, with a horn curling inside it like the inside of a French horn.

Tannoy passed through several hands. It became part of the Danish TC Group, and then, in 2015, part of Music Group, the business founded by Uli Behringer and now known as Music Tribe. Since then, reporting in the trade press has described production and jobs moving away from the Coatbridge site. I'd encourage you to treat the detailed figures with some care, because they've been reported at different times in different ways. But the overall direction is clear. One of the most British names in sound now sits inside an international group, and its Scottish manufacturing role has shrunk.

Number four. Naim.

Naim Audio was founded in Salisbury by Julian Vereker in 1973, and for decades, its black boxes with their distinctive green logos were a byword for a certain kind of British hi-fi. Rhythmic, punchy, a bit obsessive. Picture a Naim system stacked on a glass rack, power supplies in separate boxes, thick cables running between them, and an owner who will absolutely tell you why each box matters.

In 2011, Naim merged with Focal, the French loudspeaker maker. The combined group has since been known as Vervent Audio Group, with investors behind it, and the exact shareholding has changed over time. Naim is still based in Salisbury, and it still designs and builds products there, which is worth saying clearly. But the company is now part of a larger, French-led group, not the independent Salisbury business it once was.

So we're at number four, and I owe you the brand with four owners in under ten years. Here it is.

Number three. Bowers and Wilkins.

Founded in Worthing in 1966 by John Bowers. Picture a small shop and workshop, a radio and television repairman who loved music and started building speakers. From there, it became a global brand. Its 800 series monitors were used at Abbey Road. Its Zeppelin speaker dock was everywhere in the late 2000s.

Now. The four owners. In 2016, Bowers and Wilkins was sold to EVA Automation, a Silicon Valley start-up. That didn't go well, and in 2020, Bowers and Wilkins was sold again, to Sound United, the American company that also owned Denon and Marantz. Then in 2022, Sound United itself was bought by Masimo, a US medical technology company, a deal widely reported at around a billion dollars. And in 2025, Masimo sold its whole consumer audio business, including Bowers and Wilkins, Denon, Marantz and Polk, to Harman for three hundred and fifty million dollars. That deal was completed in September 2025, according to reporting at the time. And Harman, remember, is a subsidiary of Samsung.

Four owners. EVA Automation, Sound United, Masimo, Harman. In under ten years. And the price tag for the group dropping from roughly a billion dollars to three hundred and fifty million in about three years.

Harman says the brands will continue as distinct names. Bowers and Wilkins still has its roots in Worthing. But picture the tone in the room when people in that town read the headline. A medical device maker selling their brand to a subsidiary of a Korean electronics giant. That's the reality of modern British hi-fi ownership.

Number two. Arcam.

Arcam began in Cambridge in the 1970s. The name came from A and R Cambridge, and its early amplifiers, especially the A60, became a staple of student bedrooms and first serious systems. Picture an A60 on a desk in a university room, beside a record deck and a pile of vinyl, ten-watt light bulbs glowing on the shelf above.

In 2017, Arcam was bought by Harman. So, Arcam and Bowers and Wilkins now sit under the same Samsung-owned parent. Two of the biggest British hi-fi names, both inside one Korean group. That's quite a thought.

Number one. KEF.

KEF was founded in 1961 by Raymond Cooke in Maidstone, Kent. The name comes from Kent Engineering and Foundry, whose site the company started on. Picture a Nissen hut on an industrial estate, the original workshop, where engineers experimented with new materials for speaker cones. KEF became famous for its Uni-Q driver, its LS50 bookshelf speakers and, going way back, its role in the design of the BBC's LS3/5a monitor.

KEF was acquired in 1992 by Gold Peak Group of Hong Kong, and it has been part of that group, through GP Acoustics, ever since. KEF still has engineering presence in Maidstone, and still celebrates its British design heritage. But it's been Hong Kong owned for more than thirty years. So many people buying a pair of LS50s today have no idea.

So, there's the ten. Musical Fidelity, Mission, Wharfedale, Quad, Leak, Tannoy, Naim, Bowers and Wilkins, Arcam and KEF. All British by origin. All now owned or controlled, to one extent or another, from somewhere else.

But before we get to the big question, let's ask a simpler one. Why did this happen? Why did so many of Britain's best-known hi-fi makers end up in foreign hands?

There isn't one answer, but there are a few patterns that come up again and again.

The first is succession. A lot of these companies were founded by one engineer with a very clear idea of how music should sound. Gilbert Briggs. Peter Walker. Raymond Cooke. John Bowers. Julian Vereker. When the founder retires or dies, someone has to decide what happens next. If there's no family member who wants to run it, and no management team with the money to buy it, the obvious answer is a sale. Picture a boardroom in a small English town, a founder's portrait on the wall, and a stack of offers on the table, most of them from overseas.

The second is scale. In the seventies and eighties, hi-fi was a big mass market. Picture a high street electrical shop on a Saturday afternoon, a wall of separates stacked to the ceiling, every unit with its own row of glowing lights, and teenagers comparing specs from a magazine. Then came the CD boom, then MP3s, then smartphones and streaming. The mass market for separate hi-fi shrank. What was left was a smaller enthusiast market and a huge new market for wireless speakers and headphones. To compete in that, you need software engineers, app developers and global distribution. That takes money that a mid-sized British firm often doesn't have.

The third is manufacturing. For decades, building speaker cabinets and amplifiers in Britain was normal. But as manufacturing costs rose and supply chains moved to Asia, many makers shifted production abroad, and in some cases the factory owners became the brand owners. It's not a coincidence that several groups now holding British brands have their roots in Asian electronics manufacturing.

And the fourth is that British hi-fi names have value precisely because they're British. A badge that says designed in England, with decades of history behind it, helps sell a product in Shanghai, Seoul or San Francisco. So the heritage that made these brands special is also exactly what made them attractive to buy.

Put those together and the pattern makes sense. A founder retires, the market changes, the factory moves, and a bigger group with deeper pockets sees a heritage name it can use. Repeat that across fifty years, and you get the list we've just been through.

Is any of that unique to hi-fi? Not really. You could tell a similar story about British car brands, about British motorcycles, about British chocolate. Picture a Mini, a Jaguar, a Triumph motorbike, a bar of Cadbury's Dairy Milk. All British names, all with foreign owners at some point. Hi-fi is just a smaller, quieter version of the same story, and because the people who care about it really care, it stings in a particular way.

Now, the third loop. The question everyone asks. Does any of this actually make the products worse?

Let me tell you about a man we'll call Dave. Dave is a composite, built from the kinds of stories you'll hear in hi-fi forums and repair workshops. He's sixty-one, lives in a semi in Stockport, and in 1993 he bought a British integrated amplifier with his first proper bonus. He's still using it.

Picture Dave's living room. A pair of floorstanding speakers either side of a gas fire, a turntable on a wall shelf, and an amplifier with a slightly worn volume knob that's been turned up for thirty years of Saturday nights.

A couple of years ago, a channel dropped out. One speaker just went quiet. Dave took the amp to a small independent repair workshop. Picture it. A cluttered bench, a soldering iron in a stand, an oscilloscope, shelves of old amplifiers waiting their turn. The technician opened it up, found a dry solder joint and a tired capacitor, and had it running again in an afternoon. Cost, under a hundred pounds.

So Dave did what a lot of people do. He went looking at what the same brand makes today, and he bought a new model. Different owner. Made somewhere else. And here's the honest answer to the question. The new amplifier sounds good. Measured performance on many modern products is excellent. Build quality, by most reviews, is solid. Global manufacturing, done well, can be very good indeed.

But Dave noticed something. The new amp is full of surface-mount components, tiny parts machine-placed onto a board, and a digital streaming module that relies on software updates. If it fails in twenty years, he's not sure the little workshop in Stockport will be able to fix it. Not because of where it was made, but because of how it was made.

So, does ownership make it worse? Not automatically. Some brands have done well under bigger owners, with more money for research and wider distribution. Others have lost focus, lost staff, or lost the people who knew why a product sounded the way it did. The ownership changes are real. The impact varies from brand to brand, product to product. And the bigger shift, honestly, is less about passports and more about the move from simple analogue boxes to complex, software-driven ones.
