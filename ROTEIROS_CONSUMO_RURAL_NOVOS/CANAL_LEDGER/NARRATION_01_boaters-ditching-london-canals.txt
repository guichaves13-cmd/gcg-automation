The towpath at Broadway Market in Hackney, on a Saturday morning. Narrowboats moored two abreast along the Regent's Canal, chimneys smoking, bikes chained to the roofs, a cat asleep on a coil of rope. Ten years ago, this was the cheapest way to live in London. A second-hand boat for the price of a deposit, a licence that cost less than a single month's rent on a flat nearby. Today, one boater reported her licence jumping from around nine hundred pounds to more than twelve hundred in a single year. And quietly, one by one, boats are untying and heading out of the city.

This is Canal Ledger. Today, we're going inside the slow exodus from London's waterways. Who's leaving, why now, and where they're actually going.

Before we cast off, I want to give you three things to keep an eye on as we go. First, there's a number the Canal and River Trust counts every year, by literally walking the towpaths with a clipboard, and what it's been doing tells you more than any headline. Second, I'll tell you where the boats that leave London are actually ending up, because it's not where most people assume. And third, there's a letter. A brown envelope that landed on the roof of a boat moored near Little Venice, and what it said changed one boater's whole plan. I'll get to all three. Stay on board.

Let's start with how London's canals filled up in the first place.

If you'd walked the Regent's Canal around the time of the 2012 Olympics, you'd have seen boats, sure, but you'd also have seen long stretches of empty bank. Gaps between moorings. Room to breathe. Over the next decade, that changed dramatically. As rents in London climbed, a narrowboat became a kind of escape hatch. You could pick up an older boat, a forty-five foot steel hull with a wood burner and a diesel engine that rattled the cups on the shelf, for somewhere in the tens of thousands of pounds. Less than a deposit on a one-bed flat. Then, instead of paying a landlord, you'd pay the Canal and River Trust a licence fee, buy your diesel, your coal and your gas bottles, and you were living in Zone Two with a view of the water.

The Trust's own boat counts, carried out on set dates each year, recorded the number of boats on London's waterways roughly doubling over the decade or so after 2012, to somewhere in the region of two thousand boats on a typical count. The exact figure moves around from count to count, but the direction was clear. London's canals had become one of the busiest waterways networks in the country.

Picture what that looked like. The Paddington Arm with boats lined up nose to tail. The Lee Navigation through Hackney Marshes and Tottenham with whole flotillas moored by the reservoirs. Boats double moored, even triple moored in places, with people climbing across someone else's bow to reach their own front door.

Now here's the key detail you need to understand. The vast majority of those boats didn't have a home mooring. A home mooring in London, a permanent berth in a marina or a residential spot, is rare and expensive, and waiting lists can run for years. So most London boaters licensed as continuous cruisers. That means you don't have a fixed home. You're required to move regularly, generally at least every fourteen days, and to be genuinely navigating around the system, not just shuffling up and down the same mile.

That rule matters more than anything else in this story. Hold onto it.

So, why are boats now leaving? Let's start with the money, because that's the part that's easiest to see.

In 2023, the Canal and River Trust announced changes to how it charges boat licences. Alongside general increases to the base fee, it introduced a surcharge for boats without a home mooring. In other words, continuous cruisers. According to published reporting on the schedule, for a standard narrowboat that surcharge is being phased in step by step from 2024, rising to around twenty-five percent on top of the standard fee by 2028. For wider boats, reports describe a much steeper surcharge, with figures as high as seventy-five percent quoted for some categories. Check the Trust's current licence tables for your own boat, because the numbers differ by length and width.

The Trust's argument was that it faces a serious funding squeeze. Its long-term government grant is falling in real terms, its infrastructure is centuries old, and it says it needs boaters to contribute more toward maintaining locks, bridges, embankments and towpaths. And continuous cruisers, it argued, place particular pressure on the busiest parts of the network.

Boaters' groups, led by the National Bargee Travellers Association, pushed back hard. They called the surcharge discriminatory, pointing out that many continuous cruisers are on low incomes and live aboard precisely because they can't afford anything else. A Freedom of Information response reported that around sixty percent of boaters responding to the relevant consultation question rejected the idea of surcharging continuous cruisers. There were protests. Picture a line of narrowboats at Paddington Basin with banners hanging from their roofs, hand-painted in thick black letters on old bedsheets.

But here's the thing. The licence is only part of it. For a lot of London boaters, the real cost of staying isn't on the licence at all.

Before I go on, I want to hear from you. If you live on a boat, or you used to, what made you stay in London, or what finally made you leave? Was it money, crowding, or something else entirely? Put it in the comments. The boating community is a small world, and your answer might be exactly what someone else on the towpath needs to read.

Now, let's talk about the first loop. The count.

Every year, Canal and River Trust staff and volunteers walk the towpaths and record every boat they see. It's a simple, almost old-fashioned exercise. Picture someone in a high-vis jacket at seven in the morning, walking from Little Venice toward Camden Lock with a clipboard and a phone, noting index numbers painted on the side of each boat.

For years, that London count went one way. Up. Then, in more recent counts, reporting suggested that the growth had slowed and the numbers had levelled off, with some counts even showing a dip in parts of London. I want to be careful here, because the count happens on specific days and can be affected by weather, events, and stoppages. One year's figure is a snapshot, not a census. But when a number that rose steadily for a decade stops rising, at exactly the moment costs go up, it tells you something has changed.

And when you talk to boaters themselves, in forums, at protests, on the bank at Kings Cross, you hear the same thing. People they know have gone. Not dramatically. No farewell party. Just a boat that used to be moored by the gas holders one week, and the next week isn't anywhere in London.

So, loop one, the count, is a signal. The flood into London has stopped. Some of the tide is going out.

Now let me introduce someone. I'm going to call her Maya. She's a composite, built from the kinds of experiences London boaters have described publicly, in interviews and on forums, not one real person. Maya is thirty-four. She works as a freelance illustrator. Eight years ago, she bought a fifty-foot narrowboat called something like Morning Heron, with a cream and green paint job, a Morso stove and a solar panel on the roof. She paid around thirty-five thousand pounds for it, using savings and a loan from her parents.

For most of those eight years, it worked. She paid her licence, around the same as a single month's rent for a room in a shared house, and her costs were coal, diesel, gas, pump-outs and the occasional blacking, when the boat is lifted out of the water and the hull is scraped and repainted.

Picture Maya's typical fortnight. She's moored by Victoria Park in Hackney. Two weeks later, she has to move. So she unties, chugs up the Hertford Union, turns onto the Lee, finds a gap near Springfield Park. Two weeks later, she moves again. Down to Limehouse. Then up the Regent's Canal toward Islington. It's a constant, slow loop, and in London, finding a gap to moor in had become harder and harder. Some days she'd cruise for hours looking for space, finally squeezing in alongside another boat in the rain.

Now. Let's take stock of what's really been pushing people like Maya out.

The first thing is crowding. When the count doubled, the bank didn't get any longer. More boats in the same space means double mooring, cruising for hours to find a gap, and the steady stress of never being sure where you'll be sleeping next week.

The second thing is costs beyond the licence. Over recent years, the price of coal, diesel and gas bottles rose sharply, along with the general cost of living. For a boater burning a bag or two of smokeless fuel a day in winter, that adds up fast.

The third thing is maintenance. An older steel boat needs regular blacking, and every few years, more serious work. A survey might find the hull plating is thin in places. Overplating or replacement plating can cost thousands of pounds. Picture a boat up on blocks in a dry dock somewhere on the edge of London, a welder kneeling under the hull, sparks falling into a puddle, and the owner doing mental arithmetic in a cold workshop.

The fourth thing, and this is the one most people outside the boating world don't know about, is enforcement.

Which brings us to the third loop. The letter.

One autumn, Maya came back to her boat near Little Venice and found an envelope taped to the window. It was from the Canal and River Trust. In plain, polite language, it said her cruising pattern had been reviewed. The record of where she'd been sighted over the year suggested she'd been moving within too small an area. Under the Trust's guidance for boats without a home mooring, she was expected to show a genuine progressive journey, not just moving between a handful of spots in one part of London. The letter warned that if her pattern didn't improve, her next licence might be restricted, perhaps to a shorter period, or not renewed at all.

I'm framing this as Maya's story, but letters like this are real. The Trust publishes guidance for boats without a home mooring, and it does check boats' sighting records, and boaters have shared their experiences of being issued restricted licences. The exact wording and process varies.

For Maya, the letter changed everything. Because if you're a London boater who works in London, you want to stay near London. But the guidance pushed her toward a wider range. And once you're cruising a wider range, you realise something. You don't actually have to come back.

Now here's what happened next, and this brings us to the second loop. Where are the boats actually going?

The obvious assumption is that they head somewhere pretty. The Kennet and Avon, perhaps, west toward Bath, with its golden stone and lavender gardens. Or the Oxford Canal, through Banbury. And some do. But talk to boaters who've left, and you hear something more practical.

A lot of them head north up the Grand Union, past Rickmansworth, Berkhamsted, Leighton Buzzard. Picture it. The skyline disappearing behind you. Then chalk hills, Chiltern beech woods, and wide green fields with cattle in them. Long, quiet stretches of bank where you can moor alone for two weeks without another boat in sight.

Some keep going to the Midlands. Places like Braunston, the old canal crossroads in Northamptonshire, or the waterways around Birmingham, which has more miles of canal than Venice, as the old saying goes, though that comparison gets argued about. Some go further north still, onto the Leeds and Liverpool, or the Macclesfield and the Peak Forest.

Why the Midlands and North? Because the boats there aren't packed nose to tail. Because home moorings, if you want one, are cheaper and easier to find than in London. Because local living costs, from groceries to pints, are generally lower. And because a growing number of boaters work remotely, so as long as there's 4G and a solar panel, they can earn a London salary, or at least a London-ish one, from a towpath in Staffordshire.

There's another destination too, and this one is less romantic. Land. Some boaters leave the water altogether. They sell the boat, sometimes at a loss, and move into a flat, often outside London. For them, the boat was a bridge between one life and another, and once the costs and the stress rose high enough, the bridge stopped making sense.

So, loop two. The boats leaving London are mostly heading north, up the Grand Union and toward the Midlands and beyond, chasing space and lower costs. And some are leaving the water entirely.

So, what did Maya do? She spent a winter moving through Hertfordshire. Then spring came, and she kept going. She took the Grand Union north, through the Chilterns, through Milton Keynes. Picture her standing on the back deck at dawn, tiller under one arm, a tin mug of coffee in her hand, mist lifting off the water at Stoke Bruerne. She reached Braunston in late spring. She found work kept coming over email. She found she could moor in the same stretch of bank alone for a fortnight, with nobody else's boat bumping against hers at night.

She hasn't gone back. Not because she hated London, but because once she'd left, she realised how much London had been costing her, in money, in stress, and in sleep.

But before you start picturing a happy ending for everyone, let me tell you what leaving actually involves, because it's not as simple as untying a rope.

First, there's the journey itself. London to Braunston up the Grand Union is roughly a hundred miles and well over a hundred locks, depending on your exact route. At a gentle pace, that's weeks of travel, not days. Picture a single-handed boater working a flight of locks in the rain, pushing a heavy black and white balance beam with her back, climbing a slippery ladder, then stepping back aboard to move the boat up one chamber, and doing it again, and again, all afternoon.

Second, there's winter. Out of London, the towpath gets quieter, and so does everything else. Water points, rubbish points and pump-out stations can be miles apart. Shops might be a long walk from the canal. Some stretches close for planned maintenance during the winter stoppage season, when the Trust drains sections to repair lock gates and walls. Picture a lock chamber emptied completely, men in waders standing on the mud at the bottom, a massive new oak gate swinging on a crane. Beautiful to watch. Inconvenient if you need to get through.

Third, there's selling. Some boaters who decide to quit altogether try to sell in London, where demand from people hoping to escape rent has kept prices for liveaboard boats relatively firm. But brokers and boaters have described the market as more cautious, as buyers factor in the surcharge and the stricter approach to cruising. Treat any claim about boat prices with care, because they vary hugely by age, length and condition.

And fourth, there's community. For all its problems, London's boating scene has something other places can lack. Neighbours who help you bow-haul a boat with a dead engine. Group chats warning about stoppages and thefts. Pop-up cafes and bookshops on boats. When you leave, you leave that behind, and some boaters say the loneliness of a quiet stretch of canal in February hits harder than they expected.

So, no, leaving isn't free, and it isn't easy. It's just, for a growing number of people, the least bad option.

Now, I want to be fair, because there are two sides to this.

For the Canal and River Trust, the network is enormous, around two thousand miles of waterways in England and Wales by its own description, with locks, aqueducts, tunnels and reservoirs, many of them more than two hundred years old. Its funding pressures are real. And from the Trust's point of view, some of the pressure on London's canals is precisely the problem it was trying to solve. If continuous cruising is meant to mean genuinely moving around the network, and some boats in London were effectively living in one place, then enforcing the rules is part of keeping the system fair for everyone.

On the other side, boaters' groups argue that many people living on boats in London are working-class Londoners, carers, artists, key workers, people who grew up in the city and simply couldn't afford to stay on land. For them, the surcharge and enforcement feel like being priced out of the city twice, once by landlords and once by the waterway. And they point out that the Trust is a charity, with public duties, not just a landlord with a balance sheet.

Both of those things can be true. A stretched charity trying to fix centuries-old infrastructure. And a community of people for whom the water was the last affordable corner of a very expensive city.

So, let me give you the honest picture, as far as I can.

London's canals are not emptying out. On a sunny weekend in Camden, you'll still see boats moored along the towpath, tourists eating street food on the lock gates, and paddleboarders weaving between the barges. If you walk the Regent's Canal tomorrow, it'll look busy.

But under the surface, the economics have shifted. The cheapest way to live in London is no longer as cheap as it was. The surcharge is still being phased in, with the full rate not due until 2028, so the squeeze isn't over yet. And the towpath grapevine says people are leaving in a quiet trickle, rather than a flood.

So, let's tie off all three loops.

Loop one. The count. The Trust's annual boat count in London rose for roughly a decade, then levelled off, with some recent counts suggesting a dip. One snapshot doesn't prove a trend, but the long rise has stopped.

Loop two. The destination. Most boats leaving London are heading north up the Grand Union, toward the Chilterns, the Midlands and beyond, where moorings are easier, costs are lower and the bank is quieter. And some are leaving boating altogether.

Loop three. The letter. The cruising pattern warnings and restricted licences are real tools the Trust uses. For boaters like Maya, a letter on the window was the moment staying in a small area of London stopped being an option, and once she started moving properly, she never came back.

Picture that Saturday morning on Broadway Market again. Boats two abreast. Smoke from the chimneys. A cat on a coil of rope. Now picture a gap, one boat-length of empty water, where somebody's home used to be. That's what this story looks like. Not a mass departure. Just gaps, slowly appearing.
