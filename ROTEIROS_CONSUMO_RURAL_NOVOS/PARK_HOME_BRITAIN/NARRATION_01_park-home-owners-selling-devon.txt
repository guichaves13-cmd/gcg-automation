On a residential park just outside Dawlish, on the South Devon coast, a cream-coloured twin-unit with a neat little veranda and two hanging baskets has a "For Sale" sign in the window. The asking price is around two hundred thousand pounds. And if it sells for that, the owner will hand roughly twenty thousand pounds of it straight to the site owner, before the solicitor, before the removal van, before anything. That's the law. And it's one of the reasons that, up and down Devon, a growing number of park home owners are quietly deciding it's time to sell up while they still can on their own terms.

Thousands of people live in park homes across Devon. From the parks tucked behind the seafront at Exmouth and Teignmouth, to the sites along the Exe estuary, to the quiet rural parks inland near Newton Abbot, Tiverton, and out towards the edge of Dartmoor. For a lot of them, it was the dream retirement. Sell the family house, buy a brand-new park home for a fraction of the price, bank the difference, and enjoy the sea air with neighbours your own age.

So why are so many of them now putting the sign in the window?

And third, there's a decision a lot of sellers are making about where they go next. It's not what you'd expect, and for some of them it turns out to be the best money decision of their whole retirement. Stick with me for that one.

Let's start with the basics, because if you've never lived on a park, some of this will surprise you.

A residential park home in England is not like a house. You own the home itself, the structure, but you don't own the ground underneath it. That's the pitch, and it belongs to the site owner. You live there under an agreement covered by the Mobile Homes Act 1983, which was significantly updated for England by the Mobile Homes Act 2013. And you pay the site owner a pitch fee, usually monthly, for the right to keep your home on that piece of land.

That's the word I mentioned. Pitch. Your home sits on someone else's land. Everything else in this video flows from that.

Because when you don't own the land, a few things follow. The site owner sets the park rules, within the law. A lot of parks have age restrictions, often over fifty or over fifty-five. Many have rules about pets, about vehicles, about what you can put in your garden. The site owner maintains the roads, the lighting, sometimes the drains and the communal areas. And critically, when you sell, the site owner is entitled to a commission.

Under the law in England, that commission can be up to ten percent of the sale price. So on a two hundred thousand pound home, up to twenty thousand pounds. On a hundred and fifty thousand pound home, up to fifteen thousand. That money goes to the site owner, not to you.

Now, people who bought their homes knew that rule existed. It's in the documents. But knowing it in your sixties, when you're excited about the move, and feeling it in your late seventies, when you're working out how much you'll actually walk away with, are two very different things.

Picture the scene at the solicitor's office in Newton Abbot. A couple sitting across the desk, looking at the completion statement. The sale price at the top. The ten percent line. The legal fees. The estate agent, if they used one. And the number at the bottom that they'll actually take to their next home. That's the moment it lands.

So, why sell now, and why in Devon in particular? Let's go through the reasons people actually give.

Reason one is that envelope on the doormat. The pitch fee review.

Every year, the site owner can review the pitch fee. For a long time in England, the starting presumption for that annual review was linked to the Retail Prices Index, RPI. And when inflation spiked a few years ago, RPI ran well into double figures for a while, which meant some owners faced pitch fee rises they'd never seen before.

That caused a lot of anger, and Parliament changed the law. The Mobile Homes (Pitch Fees) Act 2023 switched the presumption from RPI to the Consumer Prices Index, CPI, which usually runs lower. That was a genuine win for residents, and the campaign groups fought hard for it.

But here's the thing. Even with CPI, the pitch fee still goes up most years. And it's not the only bill. Many park homes are heated by bottled or bulk LPG, or by electricity supplied through the site owner, and residents across the country have reported real strain when energy costs climbed. Add council tax, the usual running costs, and the fact that a lot of residents are living on a fixed state pension with maybe a small private one on top, and that annual envelope starts to feel heavier every spring.

So that's the second loop explained. The envelope is the pitch fee review notice. It was never a huge amount in one go for most people. But it's a reminder, every single year, that your costs on the park keep rising and you don't control them.

Tell me in the comments, and tell me roughly which part of the country you're in. I want to build a picture of how this varies, because from what people tell me, it's all over the place.

Right. Reason two, and this is the big one in Devon specifically. Age, health, and family.

A lot of people moved onto Devon parks in their early sixties. The parks were new or newly refurbished, the homes were shiny, and everyone on the park was a similar age. Fast forward fifteen or twenty years, and the same people are in their late seventies or eighties. And Devon, for all its beauty, can be a hard place to grow old if your family lives in Birmingham, or Reading, or Manchester.

The hospitals are spread out. The roads are narrow and winding. Buses in rural Devon can be few and far between. And when one partner becomes unwell, or one partner passes away, the person left behind on the park can suddenly find themselves a long way from anyone who can help.

Imagine a couple we'll call Margaret and Dennis. They're a composite, but you'll recognise them if you've ever spent time on a Devon park. They moved down from the Midlands in their early sixties, sold a three-bed semi, and bought a brand-new home on a park a few miles from the coast. For fifteen years it was wonderful. Cream teas, walks on the beach, a social club on the park, neighbours popping round. Then Dennis had a fall. Then a stroke. Then Margaret was doing the hospital run to Exeter several times a week, an hour round trip, on her own.

Their daughter lives near Leicester. And one day, over the phone, she says what a lot of adult children end up saying. Mum, I think it's time you came home.

That's the story behind a lot of the "For Sale" signs. Not drama. Not a scandal. Just life catching up, and the realisation that a park home on the Devon coast is a wonderful place to be sixty-five and a much harder place to be eighty-five.

Now, here's where it gets more interesting, because it's not just the residents doing the maths.

Reason three is the market itself.

Park homes, in general, don't tend to behave like bricks-and-mortar houses when it comes to value. A traditional house sits on land, and over the long run land in a place like Devon has tended to rise in value. A park home is the structure without the land. And like a car or a caravan, many park homes tend to lose value over time as they age, especially older models. A brand-new home on a well-run park can hold its price well for some years. But a twenty-five-year-old home with an older layout can be harder to sell, and often for a lot less than the owners paid.

That's why some owners decide to sell earlier rather than later. The insider logic goes like this. If I'm going to have to sell eventually anyway, I'd rather sell while the home is still in good condition and attractive to buyers, than wait until it's older, I'm less able to keep it up, and the market for it is thinner.

And there's another factor. Buyers. The typical park home buyer is retired or nearly retired, and usually a cash buyer, because mortgages on park homes are hard to find. So the pool of buyers is smaller than for a normal house. If a few homes on the same park go up for sale at once, sellers can find themselves competing with their own neighbours.

So that's part of the quiet rush. Get in early, sell while the home still looks good, and before the park next door has three more signs up.

Now let's go back to the first loop for a moment. The word in the paperwork. Pitch. Because there's a second part to it that matters for sellers.

When you sell a park home in England, the buyer takes over your agreement with the site owner. And historically, there were serious problems in parts of the sector, with some residents reporting that sales were being blocked or discouraged so that the site owner could buy the home cheaply, or replace it with a new one to sell at a higher price. That's exactly the kind of thing the 2013 Act was designed to stop. Under the current rules in England, you can sell your home on the open market, and the site owner can only object to a buyer through a formal process and on limited grounds, decided by a tribunal.

Most site owners are perfectly decent operators. But the history means a lot of residents are cautious. And that caution is part of why some are choosing to sell while they're still well, still able to handle the paperwork, and still able to push back if anything goes wrong.

And that's the pitch loop closed. The word is pitch. You own the home but not the ground. And everything about selling, the commission, the process, the value, comes back to that one fact.

Let me give you a picture of what a lot of these sales look like on the ground, because this is the kind of thing you only learn by being there.

Spring on a park near Exmouth. A couple of estate agent boards on the main drive, a few more homes with handwritten signs in the windows. Some owners use specialist park home agents who know the sector. Some use local high street agents. Some sell privately, through word of mouth, a neighbour's cousin who's looking to move down. And some sell back to the site owner directly, often for less than the open market, but quickly and without hassle.

That last option is one of the big quiet decisions. A direct sale to the site owner can mean a faster, simpler exit with no viewings, no chain, and sometimes help with the move. For an eighty-year-old widow who wants to be near her grandchildren by Christmas, that might be worth more than the extra money she'd get by holding out. But it's a decision people should only make with their eyes open, ideally with independent advice, and after getting a sense of what the home would fetch on the open market.

Now let's get to the third loop, because this is the bit that surprises people. Where do they go next?

You'd expect most of them to buy another park home somewhere cheaper. And some do. But a lot of the Devon sellers I'm talking about are doing something different.

Here's the pattern. They sell the park home. They pay the commission, the legal fees, the agent. And then, instead of buying anything at all, a fair number of them rent. Often a ground-floor flat or a bungalow near family, or a place in a retirement development with a warden or support on site.

Why? Because once you've lived through the experience of owning a depreciating home on someone else's land, the logic of buying again in your late seventies doesn't always add up. Renting means no commission on the way out, no pitch fee reviews, and no maintenance on the structure. It means the money from the sale stays in the bank, earning a little, available for care costs if they're needed, or for helping the grandchildren. And it means that when the next move comes, and in your eighties there often is another one, it's simply a matter of giving notice.

It's not for everyone. Renting has its own costs and insecurities, especially with rents where they are now. But for some, it's turned out to be a much cleaner and less stressful final chapter than they expected.

Others go the other way and move closer to family by buying a small bungalow, if the sale of the park home plus their savings can stretch to it in a cheaper part of the country. Lincolnshire, parts of Yorkshire, the North East. Places where a modest bungalow costs a lot less than it would in South Devon.

And a smaller group, the younger ones, sell a park home in Devon where prices are high and buy another one on a well-run park somewhere cheaper, pocketing the difference. That's the same move they made the first time round, just repeated.

So that's the third loop closed. The surprising next step for a lot of sellers isn't another park home. It's renting near family, or a bungalow in a cheaper region, with the cash kept in reserve.

Let me take a step back and be fair to the park home lifestyle, because this isn't a video telling you park homes are bad. Far from it.

For a lot of people, a park home is a wonderful way to live. It's often a lot cheaper to buy than a bungalow in the same area. It's usually single storey, which is great for older knees. Many parks are beautifully kept, with flower beds, communal lawns, and a real sense of community. Neighbours look out for each other. If someone's curtains aren't open by ten, someone knocks. In a country where loneliness in later life is a serious problem, that matters.

And for many, the money still works. Plenty of owners sold a family home, bought a park home outright, and have lived comfortably on the difference for years.

The point is that a park home is a different kind of asset, and it rewards people who understand the rules going in and who plan the exit as carefully as the entrance.

So if you're thinking about buying a park home in Devon or anywhere else in England, here's what the insiders would tell you.

Read the written statement, your agreement, cover to cover, before you commit. Look at the park rules. Ask the site owner how the pitch fee has changed over the last few years. Speak to residents, not just the sales office. Ask what's included in the pitch fee and what's billed separately, especially energy. Check whether the site has a qualifying residents' association, because that can give residents a stronger voice. Find out whether the site owner holds the right licence from the local council. And think honestly about where you'll be in fifteen years, because the exit, with that ten percent commission, is part of the cost of the home.

And if you're already living on a park and wondering whether to sell, here's the other side. Don't rush. Get a proper valuation, ideally from someone who knows the park home market. Understand the sale process under the 2013 Act and your rights in it. Talk to family before you make the decision, not after. Get independent advice if the site owner offers to buy. And make sure that whatever comes next, a flat, a bungalow, another park, is genuinely an improvement and not just a reaction to a bad month.

There are good organisations for residents too. Independent advice services and residents' associations exist, and the government's own guidance on park homes rights in England is publicly available. It's worth a read either way.

One more thing before we wrap up, because it's the detail that catches out more sellers than anything else, and it's not in most guides.

Timing. Park home sales in Devon are seasonal, in a way normal house sales aren't. The buyers tend to come in spring and summer. They're on holiday in Sidmouth or Budleigh Salterton, they drive past a park on a sunny afternoon, they see the hanging baskets and the sea glinting at the end of the lane, and they fall in love with the idea. In February, with the rain coming sideways off the Channel and the drive half-flooded, far fewer of them are browsing.

So the insiders who sell well tend to plan backwards. They get the home tidied, the veranda painted, the gutters cleared and the paperwork ready over the winter, and they're on the market by Easter. The ones who decide in a hurry in November often find themselves waiting until the following spring anyway, paying another round of pitch fees while they wait.
