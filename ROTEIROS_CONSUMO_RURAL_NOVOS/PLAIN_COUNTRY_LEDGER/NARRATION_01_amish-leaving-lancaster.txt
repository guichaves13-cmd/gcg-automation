Lancaster County has some of the most productive non-irrigated farmland in the United States. To its credit, Lancaster County has fought hard to protect farmland. The county and private groups like the Lancaster Farmland Trust have preserved a huge amount of land through agricultural easements. Lancaster is often cited as a national leader in farmland preservation. But here's the twist. Preservation keeps the land as farmland, which is good, but it doesn't make it cheap. Preserved farmland still sells, and it still sells for a lot, because there's so much demand from Amish and Mennonite farmers who want to stay.

So you get this strange situation. There's still farmland in Lancaster. But a young couple in their early twenties, with a horse, a buggy, some savings, and maybe help from parents, simply can't pay what it costs.

Now, how have Lancaster's Amish dealt with that pressure over the years? In three main ways. And the first two let them stay. The third is the one that sends them away.

The first way was splitting farms. A large farm gets divided between sons. But you can only do that so many times before the pieces are too small to support a family.

The second way was leaving farming without leaving Lancaster. Over the last few decades, a huge share of Lancaster Amish men have moved into small businesses. Picture a metal-roofed shop behind a farmhouse, with a diesel engine running a line shaft and the sound of saws and nail guns inside. Furniture. Storage sheds. Gazebos. Kitchen cabinets. Construction crews. Harness shops. Bakeries. Quilt shops. Some of these businesses do millions of dollars a year in sales. Scholars like Donald Kraybill have written extensively about this shift from plow to workshop. It's let a lot of Amish families stay in Lancaster without needing a whole farm.

But a lot of Amish families still believe farming is the best life for raising children. Working together, close to the land, close to the home, kids doing chores alongside their parents. Many bishops and parents worry that when fathers go off to work on construction crews or in shops with outsiders, something important is lost. So for families who really want to farm, there's the third way.

They leave.

And that's where this gets interesting, because when an Amish family leaves Lancaster, they don't usually go alone, and they don't usually go to a city. They go together. A few families pool resources and buy land in a county where farmland costs a fraction of what it does at home. They start a new settlement, or join a young one. Within a few years, there's a schoolhouse, a church district, a harness maker, a produce auction. A community.
