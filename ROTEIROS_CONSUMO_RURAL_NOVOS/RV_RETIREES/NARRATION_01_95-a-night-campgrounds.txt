Let's start with how we got here.

Back in 2020, the whole country discovered camping. People couldn't fly, couldn't go to hotels, couldn't go to cruise ships, so they bought RVs. Industry shipments hit record levels around 2021, with well over half a million new RVs shipped that year according to the RV Industry Association. Picture the lots along the interstates that summer. Rows and rows of travel trailers with the plastic still on the windows, and buyers lined up for whatever was left.

All those new RVs needed somewhere to go. And the number of campsites didn't grow anywhere near as fast. You can build an RV in a factory in Elkhart, Indiana in a matter of days. Building a new campground takes land, zoning approval, septic systems, power runs, water lines, and years. So for a while you had way more rigs than sites, and when demand outruns supply, prices go up.

But here's the part people miss. Even after the pandemic rush cooled down, the prices didn't come back down. Why?

Because of who started buying campgrounds.

For most of American history, a campground was a mom-and-pop business. A retired couple, a few acres by a lake, a little camp store selling firewood and ice and fishing worms, a hand-painted sign at the road. Those places still exist. But over the last decade, a lot of them have been bought up by bigger operators. Large real estate companies and investment-backed groups saw RV parks as a steady, cash-producing asset and started buying them in bulk, renovating them, and rebranding them as "resorts."

You've seen what that looks like. The gravel pads become concrete. There's a resort-style pool with a splash pad. There's a pickleball court, a dog park, a coffee bar in the clubhouse, maybe a little bistro. And the nightly rate doubles. Some people love the upgrades. But if you're a retiree who just wants a flat spot with power and a sewer hookup for a week, you're now paying for a splash pad you'll never use.

On top of that, many of these parks have moved to what the hotel industry calls dynamic pricing. The rate goes up on weekends, up on holidays, up when the park is fuller. And many of them have added fees. Reservation fees. Fees for extra vehicles. Fees for a second pet. Charges for electric on a meter if you're staying long. By the time you get to the final screen of the online booking, that "starting at" price has grown.

The first escape is federal land. And this is where that eighty-dollar pass comes in.

It's called the America the Beautiful Senior Pass. If you're a US citizen or permanent resident aged sixty-two or older, you can buy a lifetime version for eighty dollars. That's it. One payment. It gets you into national parks and many other federal recreation areas without paying the entrance fee. But the part a lot of RVers don't realize is that at many federal campgrounds, the Senior Pass also gets you a discount on the camping fee itself, often fifty percent. That includes a lot of campgrounds run by the US Forest Service, the National Park Service, the Bureau of Land Management, and the Army Corps of Engineers. Rules vary by campground and by concessionaire, so always check the specific listing, but the discount is real and it's widespread.

Let's talk about the Army Corps of Engineers for a second, because this is one of the best-kept secrets in RV travel. The Corps runs hundreds of campgrounds, mostly on lakes and rivers created by its dams. Places across Texas, Oklahoma, Arkansas, Missouri, Kentucky, Tennessee, Georgia. Picture a wide paved site under oak trees, a picnic table, a fire ring, and a boat ramp a two-minute walk away, with the lake right there through the windshield. Many Corps sites have water and electric. Some have full hookups. And standard rates at a lot of these parks have historically sat in the twenty to forty dollar range. Now cut that in half with a Senior Pass. You're potentially looking at well under twenty dollars a night, on a lake, for a site that frankly beats a lot of those concrete resorts.

National Forest campgrounds are similar. Often simpler, often fewer hookups, but cheap, beautiful, and quiet. And with the pass, cheaper still.

So that's the first loop paid off. The eighty-dollar pass. If you're sixty-two or older and you don't have one, that's probably the single fastest way to cut your camping costs.

Now, here's where it gets really interesting, because some retirees don't want cheap camping. They want almost free camping. And that brings us to the desert.

Picture this. Late January in western Arizona. The air is dry and crisp in the morning and warm by noon. You're standing on a gravel ridge looking out across a flat valley, and spread out below you, as far as you can see, are RVs. Thousands of them. Big diesel pushers, little truck campers, old Airstreams, vans, tents. Solar panels flashing in the sun. Somebody's grilling. Somebody's walking a dog along a wash lined with creosote bushes. This is Quartzsite.

Every winter, this little town near the California border swells with RVers. And the reason is the land around it. Most of it belongs to the Bureau of Land Management. And the BLM runs something called Long-Term Visitor Areas, or LTVAs.

Here's how it works. You buy a season permit. The season runs roughly from mid-September to mid-April. The fee has been set at a few hundred dollars for the whole season, and it's been adjusted over the years, so check the current BLM number before you go. But we're talking about a few hundred dollars for up to seven months of camping. Compare that to ninety-five dollars a night. A season pass in the LTVA can cost about what you'd spend for a couple of nights at some of those resorts we talked about.

What do you get? Not hookups. This is dry camping. You bring your own water, you manage your own power with solar or a generator, and you dump your tanks at the station on site. LTVAs typically have a dump station, fresh water fill, and trash collection. The La Posa LTVA, just south of Quartzsite, is the famous one. There are others too, near Yuma and along the California side, like Imperial Dam.

And that's the second loop. The stretch of Arizona desert where you can stay all winter for a few hundred dollars.

And outside the LTVAs, BLM land in many areas allows free dispersed camping, often up to fourteen days in one spot, before you move on to another spot some distance away. Rules vary, so always check the local BLM office, but the principle is simple: a lot of public land in the American West is open to you, for free, for short stays.

Now, I want to be honest about the trade-offs, because this is not for everyone. Dry camping takes skill. You have to watch your water. You have to know your battery. Your fridge has to run on propane or solar. Your showers get shorter. And the desert can be windy, dusty, and very cold at night. Some retirees try it and hate it. Others discover it's the best thing that ever happened to their retirement. Which brings us back to Ray and Donna.

But first, I want to show you the middle ground, because between ninety-five-dollar resorts and wild desert camping, there's a whole range of options that a lot of retirees are using.

The first middle-ground option is to stop paying by the night. Almost every campground, even the expensive ones, has a weekly rate and a monthly rate, and the monthly rate is often dramatically cheaper per night. A park charging ninety-five a night might charge a monthly rate that works out to a fraction of that, though for monthly stays you'll often pay electric on a meter. If you slow down, if you stay a month in one place instead of moving every three days, your costs can drop a lot. And honestly, a lot of retirees who slowed down say they enjoy it more. You actually get to know a place. You find the good diner. You learn the walking trails.

The second middle-ground option is membership camping. There are a few kinds. One is a host network, where wineries, farms, breweries, and small businesses let members park overnight for free. The best-known is Harvest Hosts, which charges an annual membership, typically in the low hundreds of dollars. Picture pulling into a vineyard in the Texas Hill Country at sunset, parking between the rows of vines, buying a bottle in the tasting room, and waking up to roosters. You're expected to support the host by buying something, which is fair. But for a one-night stopover, it can be a lot cheaper and a lot nicer than a highway campground.

There are also discount clubs that offer reduced rates at participating campgrounds, often around fifty percent off on certain nights. They come with conditions, like blackout dates and limits on how many nights you can use the discount, but if you plan around them, they can pay for themselves fast.

Okay. The third middle-ground option is work camping, or workamping. This is where you trade some hours of work for a free site, and sometimes a little pay on top. Campgrounds, state parks, wildlife refuges, even some seasonal businesses need help. Checking people in, cleaning restrooms, running the camp store, leading nature walks, mowing, maintenance. Picture a gravel site at a state park in the mountains, a little volunteer sign on your windshield, and a golf cart parked by your door, because that's how you'll get around the park during your shift.

Many federal agencies have volunteer campground host programs. Many state parks do too. The usual deal is something like twenty or more hours a week per couple in exchange for a full-hookup site for the season. That's a free campsite in some of the most beautiful places in the country, in exchange for a part-time job that a lot of retirees genuinely enjoy. Some say it's the best social life they've had since they stopped working.

The fourth middle-ground option is location. The same night of camping can cost wildly different amounts depending on where you are and when. Coastal Florida in February, the Smokies in October, anywhere near a big national park in July, those are the expensive combinations. Go a hundred miles inland. Go in the shoulder season. Go to a state park in a less famous state. A full-hookup site at a state park in parts of the Midwest or the South can come in at a fraction of a beach resort's price. The view's different, sure. But it's still a lake, still trees, still a fire ring, still peace and quiet.

And the fifth option, and this is one more and more retirees are turning to, is to stop being fully mobile. To pick a home base. Some buy a small lot in an RV community where they own the land. Some rent an annual site somewhere they like. Some park the RV at a family member's property for part of the year. Then they travel in shorter trips from there. The idea is you're no longer paying rent every single night of the year at market rates.
