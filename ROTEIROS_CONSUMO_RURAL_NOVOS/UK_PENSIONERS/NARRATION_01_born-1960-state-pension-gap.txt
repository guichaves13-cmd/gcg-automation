For years, the State Pension age for both men and women in the UK has been sixty-six. That's been the case since the rise from sixty-five to sixty-six finished in 2020. But the law already set out the next step. Under the Pensions Act 2014, State Pension age rises from sixty-six to sixty-seven, phased in between 2026 and 2028.

The way it's phased in is by date of birth, month by month. If you were born before the sixth of April 1960, your State Pension age is sixty-six. Simple. But if you were born on or after the sixth of April 1960, you start to get extra months added on.

Born between the sixth of April and the fifth of May 1960, your pension age is sixty-six and one month. Born between the sixth of May and the fifth of June 1960, it's sixty-six and two months. And so on, one extra month for each month of birth, until you get to people born from the sixth of March 1961 onwards, whose State Pension age is a full sixty-seven.

So if you were born in, say, August 1960, your State Pension age is around sixty-six and five months. Five months longer than someone born in March 1960.

On the government's website there's a free service called Check your State Pension forecast. You'll find it on gov.uk. Two, how much State Pension you're forecast to get, based on your national insurance record so far.

That third one is where people find things they didn't know.

To get the full new State Pension, you usually need thirty-five qualifying years on your national insurance record. To get anything at all, you usually need at least ten.
