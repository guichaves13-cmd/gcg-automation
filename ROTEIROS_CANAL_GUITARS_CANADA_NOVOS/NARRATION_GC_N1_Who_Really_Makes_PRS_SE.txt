There is a guitar sitting on my bench right now with three letters on the headstock that a lot of Canadians associate with Maryland, with hand-carved maple tops, with a man named Paul Reed Smith who has been building instruments since the nineteen seventies. Those three letters are P, R, and S. And right underneath them, in smaller script, are two more letters: S and E. That little S E changes everything about where this guitar was born, who actually put it together, and what it means for you when you walk into a shop in Calgary or Halifax or Saskatoon and see a price tag somewhere between roughly seven hundred and fifteen hundred Canadian dollars.

So today we are asking a simple question. Who really makes PRS SE guitars? The answer may surprise a lot of Canadians, because it is not Maryland, it is not one single factory, and it has not always been the same country. And as always on this channel, I am going to separate what is confirmed by the company itself from what is only reported by forums, dealers, or magazines. Then I am going to tell you what any of this actually changes for you as a player, for me as a tech when one of these lands on my bench, and for your wallet when it is time to sell.

Quick note before we start. This video is not sponsored. Nobody sent me this guitar. Prices are in Canadian dollars and they move around constantly, especially with everything going on with trade and currency over the last couple of years, so treat any number I say as a ballpark at the time of recording. And always, always play an instrument before you buy it.

Let's start with the company, because to understand the SE line you have to understand why it exists at all.

PRS Guitars was founded by Paul Reed Smith, and the core USA operation is in Stevensville, Maryland, on the Eastern Shore. That is confirmed, that is public, and you can see it on the company's own materials. The USA guitars, the Core line, the Private Stock instruments, the Bolt-On and S2 models, those are made in the United States. S2, for anyone wondering, was introduced as a more affordable American-made tier, still built in Maryland, but with simpler construction and finishes. If you are shopping a Core model in Canada, you are usually looking at prices well into the four, five, or even six thousand dollar range, depending on the model and the top.

That price gap is the whole reason SE exists. SE stands for Student Edition, and that is not a nickname a forum invented. PRS itself has used that explanation. The idea was simple: give younger players and working players on a budget access to the PRS design language, the bird inlays, the shape, the scale length and the general feel, without the American price tag.

Now here is where the story gets interesting, and where a lot of people get confused.

For many years, the SE line was built in South Korea. This is widely documented and not really controversial. Korean manufacturing has a long history of doing excellent work for Western brands, and the early SE guitars that came out in the early two thousands were built there. On the forums and in the used market, you will hear people refer to the Korean SE era with a certain affection. Korean factories in that period were building guitars for a lot of brands, and the general reputation was consistent fretwork, decent finishes, and solid hardware choices for the money.

Then the SE line moved, and this is the part that matters most for anyone buying new today.

According to PRS's own support pages, SE electric guitars are now built in Indonesia, in partnership with a company called Cor-Tek. And SE acoustic guitars, according to that same official source, are built by Cor-Tek in Dalian, China. So if you only remember one confirmed fact from this video, make it that one. Electric SEs: Indonesia, through Cor-Tek. Acoustic SEs: China, through Cor-Tek.

Who is Cor-Tek? This is the surprise for a lot of Canadians. Cor-Tek is the company behind the Cort brand. Cort is a South Korean company that has been making guitars for decades, both under its own name and as an original equipment manufacturer for other brands. If you have ever played a Cort G-series in a Long and McQuade, or one of their basses, you have already touched a guitar that came out of the same corporate family that builds today's PRS SE electrics.

Now, I want to be careful here, because there is a big difference between saying "the same company" and saying "the same guitar". A Cort and a PRS SE are not the same guitar with a different decal. The specifications, the materials, the quality control targets, the pickups, the setup standards, and the design are set by PRS. Cor-Tek provides the factory, the workforce, and the manufacturing know-how. PRS provides the design, the specifications and, in public statements, PRS has said it supplies certain components and maintains its own oversight on the production line. So think of it as a partnership, not a rebadge.

PRS has actually been unusually open about this. A few years ago the company released a behind-the-scenes factory tour video from the Indonesian SE facility, and outlets like MusicRadar and Guitar.com covered it. In that coverage, you can see PRS staff talking about the process, the carving, the finishing, the inspections. That openness matters, because a lot of brands in this price range simply print "Designed in USA" on the box and hope you never ask where it was actually built.

So, timeline. Korea for the early and middle years. Then Indonesia. And here is where we move into territory that is reported but not always confirmed in the same detail by the company.

Before the dedicated Cor-Tek partnership in Indonesia, there are many reports, mostly from forums and from guitar serial number discussions, that some SE guitars were built at a different Indonesian factory, a company frequently identified as PT Wildwood. And there are older forum threads, going back to around twenty eighteen, where owners and dealers said that SE guitars were being produced in more than one factory at the same time, depending on which region of the world the guitar was being shipped to. One widely shared thread said that European distribution was still getting some guitars from a Korean factory called World Music Instruments, and from PT Wildwood, while other markets were moving to Cor-Tek.

I want to flag those clearly as reported, not confirmed. These are claims from owners, dealers and forum detectives. They might be right, and many of them seem to line up with serial numbers and labels, but I have not seen PRS itself lay out a complete factory-by-factory, year-by-year map. So if you are buying a used SE from around twenty seventeen to twenty nineteen, it is possible the factory is different from the one building today's new SEs, and the only way to be sure is to check the serial number and any documentation that came with the guitar.

What PRS has confirmed publicly is the big picture. The company has said that around twenty nineteen it set up production in Indonesia with Cor-Tek. And reporting from the guitar press says that because demand grew so much, PRS and Cor-Tek expanded with a new, large facility in the Mojokerto region of East Java, close to the original plant near Surabaya. The figure that has been reported is around ninety thousand square feet. That is a serious, dedicated operation, not a corner of a generic factory.

There is one more piece of the puzzle that I think matters a lot to Canadians, and it is about setup.

PRS has stated that SE guitars destined for the North American market get inspected and set up by PRS in the United States before going out to dealers. This has been mentioned in PRS's own communications and repeated in press coverage, including interviews with PRS leadership. So in theory, the SE that arrives at your local shop in Canada has passed through an American inspection and setup station after crossing the Pacific. Now, I say "in theory" not because I doubt the company, but because a guitar can travel across a continent after that setup, sit in a cold truck in January, then sit on a wall in a heated store with dry air, and arrive on my bench with a completely different neck relief than the one it left Maryland with. Which brings us to the part of this video that I am actually qualified to talk about, which is the bench.

Let me walk you through what I check on an SE when a customer brings one in, and what I typically find.

First, the neck. Most current SE electrics use a maple neck, and many models use a rosewood fingerboard, though PRS has used other fingerboard woods depending on the model and the year. The very first thing I do is sight down the neck and measure relief at the seventh or eighth fret while fretting the first and the last fret. On SEs coming out of a Canadian winter, I very often find the neck has moved. Not because the guitar is bad, but because wood is wood, and our climate is brutal on instruments. Humidity in a heated house in February can drop well under thirty percent. The truss rod on SEs is generally easy to adjust, and on the ones I have worked on, it has responded smoothly and predictably. That is a good sign. A truss rod that is stuck, or one that turns without the neck moving, is a red flag on any guitar at any price. I have not seen a pattern of that on current SEs, but check it before you buy, or ask the store to check it.

Second, frets. This is where budget guitars usually reveal where money was saved. On the current Indonesian SEs I have handled, the fretwork has generally been good. The ends are usually rounded off reasonably well, the leveling is usually acceptable, and I do not see the sharp sprouting fret ends that you sometimes get on cheaper imports after the first dry winter. But I will be honest with you. "Generally good" is not "perfect". I have seen SEs that needed a light level and crown at a couple of frets to get the action really low without buzzing. That is not unusual for a guitar in this price range, and it is honestly not unusual for some guitars at twice the price. If you like very low action, budget for a fret dress at some point. In Canada right now, a basic level, crown and polish from an independent tech will often fall somewhere around one hundred fifty to three hundred dollars, depending on the shop and the region.

Third, the nut. The nut is one of the most important and most ignored parts of any guitar. On SEs, the nut is typically a synthetic material. It works. It is not bone. In my experience, nut slots on budget guitars are often cut a little high from the factory, because a high nut does not buzz and a low nut does, and a factory would rather send you a guitar that plays a bit stiff in the first position than one that buzzes. So when I set up an SE, I check the height of the strings at the first fret, and quite often I take the slots down a little. That single adjustment can make a guitar feel like it went up a price category. And if you ever want to upgrade the nut to bone or to a modern synthetic like Tusq, it is a straightforward job.

Fourth, the electronics. This is where PRS has made a lot of noise, and fairly so. For years now, the company has talked about using PRS-designed pickups on SE models, pickups like the ones marketed as the TCI-equipped designs, the 85 15 S, and others, depending on the model. Without getting lost in marketing names, the point is that PRS designs the pickup and specifies it, and it is produced for the SE line. When I put an SE on the bench, I check output, check for microphonic squeal at high gain, and check the pots and the switch. In my experience the switches and pots on SEs are typical import parts. They work, but if you play every night and roll your volume knob a lot, they are the first things that will get scratchy. Replacing pots and a switch is not expensive. Parts might run you somewhere between thirty and eighty dollars, plus labour.

Fifth, the tuners and bridge. SEs typically use PRS-designed hardware, including a PRS-style tremolo on many models or a stoptail on others. The trem on an SE is not the same as the one on a Core model. It is simpler and it is built to a price. But the design is sensible and on the ones I have adjusted, it returns to pitch well when properly set up, with a lubricated nut and properly stretched strings. Tuning stability problems on SEs, when I see them, almost always come from the nut, not from the tuners.

Sixth, finish and top. Most SEs have a flame maple veneer over a mahogany body or another tonewood depending on the model. The flame top on an SE is a photo-thin layer of figured maple over the body. This is common in the industry and PRS does not hide it. It is not a carved solid maple top like on a Core guitar. Some people feel let down when they learn this. Honestly, from a sound and playability perspective, the veneer does almost nothing. It is cosmetic. Where it matters is in repair and refinishing. You cannot sand through a veneer and expect to fix it easily. If you drop an SE and chip the top, a touch-up is possible, but a full refinish that keeps the flame is not going to be economical.

So let's bring this together. What does it change, in practice, that your SE was made by Cor-Tek in Indonesia rather than by PRS in Maryland?

Let's talk about playability first.

The honest bench answer is that a well set up current SE plays very close to the feel that PRS is known for. The scale length on most PRS designs sits at twenty five inches, which is a bit of a halfway point between a Gibson-style twenty four and three quarters and a Fender-style twenty five and a half. Many SEs share that same scale. The neck carves, often labelled Wide Thin or Wide Fat depending on the model, are modelled on Core shapes. The big differences you will feel are in the fretwork precision, the nut, and how the guitar resonates unplugged. A Core guitar is built with much more hand work and tighter tolerances, and you can feel that. But the gap between a professionally set up SE and an off-the-wall Core is a lot smaller than the price gap. That is just my honest opinion from the bench.

Second, repair.

Here is something I really want Canadian players to understand. Because SEs are made in very large numbers by a large manufacturer with consistent specs, they are actually pretty friendly to repair. Parts are standard. The neck is set or bolted depending on the model, and you need to know which one you have. Many SEs are set-neck guitars. A set neck is glued in, which means if you ever break the headstock, and with an angled headstock on a mahogany neck that is a real risk, the repair is a glue job, not a neck swap. The good news is that a clean headstock break on a set neck mahogany guitar is one of the most common and most fixable repairs we do. In Canada, a proper headstock repair can range from a couple of hundred dollars to several hundred dollars if you want the finish blended. For an SE, that repair can sometimes cost a big chunk of what the guitar is worth used. Keep that in mind, and use a good stand and a good case.

Bolt-on SE models, like the SE Silver Sky, are different. Silver Sky is the model PRS developed with John Mayer, and the SE version is a bolt-on design. Bolt-on means that in the worst case, the neck can be replaced. Repair options are easier, though it is worth remembering that the Silver Sky neck pocket and headstock are specific to that design, so random replacement necks from the parts world will not drop in without work.

Third, warranty and service. In Canada, PRS SE guitars are distributed through the brand's official Canadian distribution channel and sold through authorized dealers. When you buy from an authorized dealer, you are generally covered by the manufacturer's warranty as it applies to the original owner. If you buy grey market, which means someone imports a guitar directly from another country or another region, you may not have the same support in Canada. That is a real risk, especially now, when price differences between Canada and the United States have pushed some people to buy across the border. Always ask the dealer if they are authorized, and keep your receipt.

Fourth, resale. This is where SEs are honestly one of the smarter buys in the budget world.

On the used market across Canada, on Kijiji, on Facebook Marketplace, at consignment shops, SEs tend to hold their value reasonably well compared with many other import brands in the same price range. Not perfectly. A new SE loses value like any new guitar does when you walk out of the store. But the PRS name, the bird inlays, the recognition, and the strong reputation of recent SE production all help. And older Korean SEs, from the earlier era, sometimes get a small bump in desirability because of nostalgia and reputation, though that bump depends heavily on the model, the condition, and the region. In big cities like Toronto, Montreal and Vancouver, there is more supply and more competition. In smaller markets, a clean SE can move quickly.

A practical resale tip. When you sell an SE, list the country of origin and the serial number, and if you know the factory, mention it. Some buyers specifically search for Korean-made SEs. Others want the newest Indonesian production because of the updated specs. Being upfront saves you time and helps buyers trust you.

Now let me address a few myths I keep hearing at the counter.

Myth number one. "SE guitars are just Corts with a PRS logo." As I said, Cor-Tek is the manufacturing partner. That is confirmed. But the design, the specs, the pickup choices, and the quality targets are set by PRS. It is like saying a car built by a contract manufacturer is the same as every other car that manufacturer has ever built. The factory matters, but the blueprint matters more.

Myth number two. "Indonesian guitars are worse than Korean guitars." This was a common belief ten or fifteen years ago, and there were reasons for it at the time. But the gap has narrowed dramatically, and in some cases there is no meaningful gap anymore. Indonesian production has improved a lot across the industry. What matters is the specific factory, the specific quality control process, and the specific brand standards. Judge the guitar in your hands, not the label on the back of the headstock.

Myth number three. "If I buy an SE, I will have to upgrade everything." No. Many players gig SEs stock for years. The most common upgrades I do are a proper setup, sometimes a nut, sometimes pots and switch after heavy use. Pickup swaps are a matter of taste, not necessity. If you love the stock sound, keep it.

Myth number four. "Made in Maryland means every Core guitar is flawless." Also no. I have had Core guitars on my bench that needed work too. Higher-end guitars generally have better consistency, but no factory on Earth produces only perfect instruments. Wood is alive and Canada is hard on it.

Let me now give you the practical buying advice, by budget, in Canadian dollars, keeping in mind that prices shift constantly.

If you have under around eight hundred dollars to spend, look at the entry SE models and the used market. A used SE in good shape can be a fantastic first serious guitar. Bring a friend who plays, check the neck, check the frets, plug it in, roll every knob, move the switch through every position, and make sure the truss rod has adjustment room left. If the seller will not let you check the truss rod, that is your answer.

If you are in the eight hundred to fifteen hundred dollar range, you are in the heart of the new SE lineup. This is where models like the Custom 24, the McCarty-inspired SE models, and the SE Silver Sky tend to live, depending on current pricing. At this budget, the main question is not "is it good enough". The main question is "does this neck shape fit my hand". Play a few. Different SE models have different carves. And if you buy new, ask whether the store does a setup, or budget around eighty to one hundred fifty dollars for a professional setup with a tech you trust. That setup is the best money you will spend on the guitar.

If you are above fifteen hundred dollars, now you are in a very interesting zone. You can find used S2 models, which are American-made, sometimes approaching this price range, and you might find used Core models at the very top of a stretched budget. My honest bench opinion is this. If what you want is the feel and the look of PRS and you are going to play in a band, a set-up SE gets you ninety percent of the way. If what you want is the craftsmanship, the resonance, the carved top, the heirloom quality, and long term collectability, then you save and you buy American.

There is one more angle I want to cover, and it is something that has been in the news a lot over the past couple of years. Tariffs and trade.

Since twenty twenty five, there has been a lot of disruption in North American trade, with tariff announcements and counter announcements between Canada and the United States, and tariffs from the US on goods from various Asian countries. The guitar press has reported that several major brands raised prices partly because of tariff costs. Now, for a guitar made in Indonesia and shipped to Canada, the duty picture is different from one made in the US and shipped to Canada, and different again from one made in Indonesia, routed through the US for setup, and then shipped north. I am not a customs broker and I am not going to pretend to know exactly how every SE that reaches a Canadian store is classified, routed, and taxed. That is between the distributor and the Canada Border Services Agency. What I can tell you is that the price you see on the wall reflects all of that, and if you see a huge price difference between two Canadian dealers on the same model, ask why. It may be old stock bought before a price increase, it may be a sale, or it may be grey market.

Let me give you my final summary, in the same order I promised at the start.

Confirmed facts. PRS is headquartered in Stevensville, Maryland, where Core, S2 and Private Stock instruments are made. SE stands for Student Edition. SE electric guitars are built in Indonesia in partnership with Cor-Tek, the company behind Cort. SE acoustic guitars are built by Cor-Tek in Dalian, China. PRS has publicly shown its Indonesian SE production, and has said that SE guitars for North America are inspected and set up in the US before going to dealers.

Reported facts, not fully confirmed in official detail. That earlier Indonesian SEs were built by PT Wildwood. That for a time, SEs were made in different factories depending on the destination market, including Korean production at World Music Instruments for some regions. That the newer facility in the Mojokerto area of East Java is around ninety thousand square feet. These come from forums, dealers, and press reporting. They are credible, but I treat them as reported.

What it changes. For playability, a set-up SE plays much closer to a Core than the price suggests, with the biggest gaps in fretwork precision, nut work, and hand-finished details. For repair, SEs are repair-friendly, but set-neck headstock breaks can be expensive relative to the value of the guitar, and the veneer top makes cosmetic refinishing tricky. For resale, SEs hold value better than many budget imports, and knowing the origin and the serial number helps you sell.

And my honest bench verdict? The letters S and E do not mean second class. They mean a different factory, a different price, and a different set of compromises. Cor-Tek is a serious manufacturer with decades of experience, and PRS has put real effort into making the SE line feel like part of the family. Most of the difference between an average SE and a great SE is not in Indonesia. It is in the hands of the person who sets it up after it reaches Canada.

So if you own one, go check your neck relief tonight, because it is November and the furnaces are on. If you are shopping for one, play several and pick the one that feels right in your hands. And if you have an SE with an interesting serial number or a factory story, tell me about it in the comments, because the more of these stories we collect, the clearer the real picture gets.
