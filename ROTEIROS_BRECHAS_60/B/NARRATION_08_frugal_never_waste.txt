On a kitchen windowsill, there is a row of small glass jars. One holds sprouting green onions, their roots sitting in a little water. Another holds loose change. A third holds rubber bands saved from the morning newspaper. Beside them sits a handwritten shopping list on the back of an old envelope, with a few items already crossed out because they were found at the back of the pantry. Nothing on that windowsill cost very much. But every item there tells a story about a person who has learned, over a lifetime, exactly where money tends to leak away, and how to quietly plug those leaks.

Today we are going to look at ten things that frugal retirees simply refuse to waste money on, and what they do instead. Some of these are small, everyday habits. Others could add up to a meaningful amount over the course of a retirement. And there is one item near the end of this list that most people never even think of as spending at all, yet it can cost a great deal. Keep watching, because that one might change the way you look at your week.

A quick note before we begin. This video is general information, not personalized financial advice. Everyone's circumstances are different, so please look at your own numbers and, if you need to, speak with a qualified professional.

Let's get started.

Number one. Frugal retirees never waste money on food that goes in the bin.

Open the refrigerator in many homes and you will find it. A bag of salad leaves turning soft at the bottom of the crisper drawer. Half a lemon wrapped in plastic, slowly drying out. A container of leftovers from last week, pushed to the back behind the milk. It is not that anyone meant to waste it. Life simply got busy, plans changed, and the food was forgotten.

Now picture that same food as money. A few coins here, a note there, dropped straight into the kitchen bin. When you see it that way, it becomes much harder to ignore.

Frugal retirees tend to treat their refrigerator like a small shop that they manage. So what do they do instead? First, they plan. Not elaborate plans, just a rough idea of the week's meals, written on a notepad stuck to the fridge door with a magnet. Second, they shop with a list, and they check the cupboards before they go. Third, they use the oldest food first, moving older items to the front of the shelf so they are seen. And fourth, they love leftovers. Last night's roast chicken becomes today's sandwiches and tomorrow's soup.

Picture a large pot of vegetable soup simmering on a stove, made from the odds and ends at the bottom of the vegetable drawer, a bit of celery, a carrot, half an onion, a handful of lentils. That soup costs very little, it tastes wonderful, and it means nothing went to waste.

The freezer becomes a best friend, too. Bread that will not be eaten in time goes into the freezer. Bananas that are getting too ripe are peeled and frozen for baking or smoothies. Extra portions of a stew are labeled with the date and stacked neatly for an easy dinner on a tired evening.

It all sounds simple, because it is. But simple habits repeated every week are exactly where frugal people find their quiet advantage.

Now, item number two is something many people pay for every single month, often for years, without ever really using it.

Number two. Frugal retirees never waste money on subscriptions they have forgotten about.

Picture a mobile phone screen showing a list of small charges on a banking app. A streaming service. A music service. An app that was downloaded for a single trip. A magazine. A software program. Cloud storage. Each one looks small on its own. And because they are small, they slip by unnoticed.

The trouble is that small recurring charges are designed to be easy to forget. Many begin as a free trial and quietly turn into a monthly payment. Some renew automatically each year. And because nothing arrives in the mail, there is no physical reminder that you are still paying.

Frugal retirees have a wonderful habit here. Every so often, they sit down with their recent statements and a cup of tea, and they go through every recurring charge, one by one. For each one, they ask a simple question. Have I used this in the last month? If the honest answer is no, they cancel it. If they are not sure, they cancel it anyway, knowing they can always sign up again later if they truly miss it.

Picture a pen ticking off lines on a printed statement, with a little note in the margin that says cancelled, beside three or four of them.

Some frugal people also rotate their entertainment subscriptions. They sign up for one service, watch the programs they were interested in, then cancel and move to another. That way they are paying for one at a time instead of several at once.

And here is a lovely tip that costs nothing. Your local library may offer much more than books. Depending on where you live, many libraries provide free access to e-books, audiobooks, magazines, and sometimes even films, using just a library card. It is worth asking at your local branch what is available.

If you have ever found a subscription you completely forgot you were paying for, tell me in the comments. I suspect you are far from alone, and your story might prompt someone else to check their own statement tonight.

Now, item number three is something that is sold to us with words like convenience and speed. And it can be surprisingly expensive.

Number three. Frugal retirees never waste money paying a premium for convenience they do not need.

Picture a coffee shop counter early in the morning. A line of people waiting, a paper cup with a name written on it in marker, a little card reader blinking at the till. Now picture a kitchen at home, with a kettle just coming to the boil, and a favorite mug waiting beside it.

Convenience is not always a bad thing. There are days when paying for convenience is worth it, when you are traveling, when you are unwell, or when you simply want a treat. But many convenience purchases become automatic habits. The pre-cut fruit instead of a whole melon. The single-serving snacks instead of a larger pack. The delivery fee on a takeaway meal. The bottle of water bought on the way out because one was not filled at home.

Frugal retirees think of convenience as something to choose deliberately, not something to fall into. So what do they do instead? They make their own coffee and carry it in a flask when they go out. They fill a reusable water bottle at the tap. They cut up their own vegetables and fruit. They cook in batches. They keep a small bag in the car with a snack and a drink so they are not caught hungry and tempted by the nearest drive-through or service station.

Picture a canvas tote bag on a car seat, with an apple, a few crackers wrapped in a cloth, and a stainless steel bottle tucked inside. That little bag can save more over a year than people might expect.

And here is the part that frugal people know well. Treats feel more special when they are occasional. A coffee out with a friend once a week feels like an event. A coffee out every day just feels like a bill.

Now, item number four is something many of us were taught to buy as a reward for working hard. And it can be one of the largest costs in a household.

Number four. Frugal retirees never waste money on car payments for a vehicle they do not need.

Picture a monthly statement from a car finance company, sitting in a stack of mail on a hallway table. Every month it arrives. Every month it is paid. And the car it is paying for spends much of its time parked.

For many working people, a car was essential, the daily commute, the school run, the trips to the shops. But in retirement, driving patterns often change. There is no commute. There may be fewer appointments. And yet the cost of owning a car, the payments, insurance, registration, servicing, tires, fuel, and depreciation, all of that keeps adding up.

Frugal retirees look at their car with fresh eyes. Some choose to keep their existing car for as long as it stays safe and reliable, rather than trading it in. Some buy a good used car outright instead of financing a new one. Some households that once had two cars decide one is enough. And some, particularly in places with good public transport, decide to live without a car at all, using buses, trains, walking, cycling, taxis, or the occasional rental when they need it.

Picture a bus pass in a wallet, next to a library card, both slightly worn from regular use. Depending on where you live, there may be reduced fares or concessions for older residents, so it is worth checking what is available in your area.

The right answer depends entirely on where you live and how you live. Someone in the countryside may truly need a car. Someone in a city centre may not. What matters is running your own numbers honestly and making a choice on purpose.

Now, item number five is something that hides in the background of almost every home. You probably cannot see it right now, but it is quietly costing you money.

Number five. Frugal retirees never waste money on energy they are not using.

Picture a living room late at night. The television is off, but a small red light glows on the front. A phone charger is still plugged into the wall with nothing attached. A lamp is on in an empty room. A draft is coming under the back door, gently moving the curtain.

Energy waste is easy to overlook because it is invisible. You do not see heat escaping through a gap around a window. You do not see the trickle of electricity going to devices on standby. But it all appears, eventually, on the bill.

Frugal retirees make small, steady changes. They switch off lights when they leave a room. They unplug chargers and devices that are not in use, or put several on a power strip that can be switched off at once. They use draft stoppers at the bottom of doors. They close curtains in the evening to keep warmth in during winter, and close them during the day to keep heat out in summer. They wear a warm jumper and slippers before turning up the heating. They run the washing machine and dishwasher with full loads.

Picture a knitted draft stopper shaped like a long sausage dog, lying across the bottom of a front door. It is a small, almost funny object. But it is doing real work, keeping warmth inside where it belongs.

Some frugal households also compare energy plans every so often, to make sure they are not paying more than they need to. And depending on where you live, there may be programs or grants to help older homeowners with insulation or energy efficiency. It is worth looking into what applies in your area.

Now, the next item on our list involves something many of us have done at some point. And it often happens in a single moment at a checkout.

Number six. Frugal retirees never waste money on impulse buys.

Picture the end of a supermarket aisle, with a bright display of chocolates, magazines, and gadgets stacked right next to the checkout. Or picture a phone screen late at night, with an online shop showing an item marked limited time only, and a big glowing button that says buy now.

Shops are designed, very carefully, to encourage spending we did not plan. Special displays. Countdown timers. Free delivery if you spend just a little more. And online shopping has made it easier than ever to buy something in seconds, from a comfortable chair, without ever reaching for a wallet.

Frugal retirees have a beautifully simple defense. They wait. Many use some version of a waiting rule. If they see something they want that was not on their list, they give it a day, or a week, or even a month. They might write it down on a little notepad or add it to a wish list. If, after waiting, they still want it and can comfortably afford it, they buy it with a clear conscience. But very often, the urge simply fades away.

Picture a small notebook on a bedside table, with a page titled things I thought I wanted. Down the page are items with dates beside them, and most of them have a line drawn through them, because the desire passed.

Some frugal people also unsubscribe from shopping emails, remove saved card details from websites, and avoid browsing shops just for something to do. Out of sight really can mean out of mind.

And if you have a favorite trick for avoiding impulse buys, please share it in the comments. Some of the cleverest ideas on this channel come from viewers who have been practicing these habits for decades.

Now, item number seven is one that many people pay for out of loyalty. And loyalty, sadly, is not always rewarded.

Number seven. Frugal retirees never waste money by automatically renewing insurance and service contracts without checking.

Picture an envelope on a doormat with a renewal notice inside. Home insurance. Car insurance. Broadband. Mobile phone. The letter says your policy will renew automatically, and nothing needs to be done.

That phrase, nothing needs to be done, is very convenient. But in many markets, people who simply allow their policies and contracts to roll over each year can end up paying more than new customers do for similar cover. That is not true everywhere or for every company, but it happens often enough that it is worth checking.

Frugal retirees treat every renewal letter as a small invitation to review. So what do they do instead? They note the renewal date on a calendar. A few weeks beforehand, they compare what else is available. They make sure they are not paying for cover they do not need, or missing cover they do. And often, they simply call their current provider, explain that they are looking around, and ask politely whether a better rate is available.

Picture a wall calendar hanging in a kitchen, with small notes written on certain dates. Home insurance renews. Phone contract ends. Check broadband. Those little notes can be worth a surprising amount over the years.

The same goes for mobile phone plans. Many people continue paying the same monthly amount long after the cost of their handset has been paid off. Moving to a simpler plan, perhaps with just the data and minutes you actually use, may lower the bill considerably. Check your own contract to see what applies to you.

A word of caution. The cheapest option is not always the best. Frugal people care about value, not just price. They read what is covered, and they make sure they are not trading peace of mind for a small saving.

Now, item number eight is a little bit of a surprise. It involves money that many people pay simply for holding onto things.

Number eight. Frugal retirees never waste money storing things they do not use.

Picture a row of metal storage units behind a chain-link fence. Behind one of those orange roll-up doors is a stack of boxes, an old dining table, a rocking chair, some framed pictures wrapped in blankets. Every month, someone pays to keep them there. Sometimes for years.

Even without a storage unit, many homes are full of things that cost money in quieter ways. A garage so full that the car cannot fit inside. A spare room crammed with furniture. Duplicates of kitchen tools, three of the same thing, because nobody could find the first two.

Frugal retirees understand that clutter has a cost. It can mean paying for storage. It can mean needing a bigger home. It can mean buying things again because the original is lost somewhere in a pile. And it can mean stress.

So what do they do instead? They declutter, slowly and kindly. One drawer at a time. One cupboard at a time. Items that are useful but unused might be sold, donated to a charity shop, or given to family members who will actually use them. Precious items are kept, treasured, and displayed. Everything else is gently let go.

Picture a cardboard box by the front door, with the word donate written on it in thick marker, half full of neatly folded clothes, a few kitchen gadgets, and some books.

There is often an unexpected gift in this process, too. Many people find that selling a few things they no longer need brings in a little extra money, and the home feels lighter and calmer afterwards.

Now, we are coming to item number nine. This is the one I mentioned at the very beginning, the one most people never think of as spending at all.

Number nine. Frugal retirees never waste money by neglecting their health and their home.

That might sound strange at first. How is neglect a kind of spending? Let me explain.

Picture a small brown water stain on a bedroom ceiling. It is not very big, so it is easy to ignore. But above that stain, a slow leak in the roof may be getting worse. Or picture a car dashboard with a small warning light glowing orange, week after week. Or a tooth that has been a little sensitive for a while, but there never seems to be a good time to see the dentist.

Small problems left alone have a way of becoming large, expensive problems. A minor roof repair can grow into replacing ceilings and insulation. A car that misses its servicing can develop a much bigger fault. A health concern that is caught early is often simpler to manage than one that is left for years.

Frugal retirees are not cheap about the things that protect them. They do the small maintenance tasks on time. They clean the gutters, or pay someone trustworthy to do it. They get the boiler or furnace checked. They service the car. They keep up with check-ups and appointments. They look after their teeth, their eyes, and their hearing.

Picture a pair of well-worn walking shoes by the back door, ready for the morning walk. A bowl of fruit on the counter. A reusable pill organizer with the days of the week printed on the lids. These are small, ordinary things, but they represent a choice to invest in staying well.

Walking, gardening, cooking simple meals at home, staying connected with friends, getting good sleep, many of the healthiest habits cost very little. And they may help you avoid much larger costs later on, not just in money, but in independence and quality of life.

This is the heart of true frugality. It is not about spending as little as possible on everything. It is about spending wisely, and that sometimes means spending a little now to save a great deal later.

Now, our final item. It is the one that ties all of the others together.

Number ten. Frugal retirees never waste money trying to keep up with other people.

Picture a quiet suburban street. One neighbor has a brand-new car in the driveway. Another has just had a new kitchen fitted, with the builder's van still parked outside. Another has posted photos online from an expensive cruise. It is so easy to look around and feel that you should be doing the same.

But here is a secret that frugal retirees understand very well. You never really know what is happening behind someone else's front door. The new car might be on a long finance agreement. The new kitchen might have been paid for on credit. The cruise might have been a once-in-a-lifetime splurge. Comparing your life to the outside of someone else's is a game nobody truly wins.

So what do frugal retirees do instead? They decide what matters to them, and they spend on that, happily, without apology. For one person, it might be travel. For another, it might be their garden. For another, it might be helping grandchildren, or a good pair of binoculars for birdwatching, or season tickets to the local theatre. And then they let the rest go.

Picture an older man sitting on a garden bench in the late afternoon, a mug of tea in one hand, watching birds at a feeder he built himself from scrap wood. He is not wondering whether his neighbor's car is newer than his. He is content.

That contentment is perhaps the most valuable thing a frugal retiree owns. It means they are not easily tempted by advertising. They are not unsettled by what others buy. And they have the freedom to enjoy their money on the things that truly bring them joy.

So let's quickly look back over all ten.

Frugal retirees never waste money on food that ends up in the bin, and instead plan meals and love their leftovers. They never waste money on forgotten subscriptions, and instead review their statements regularly. They never pay for convenience they do not need, and instead choose treats deliberately. They never waste money on car payments they do not need, and instead keep cars longer or rethink whether they need one at all. They never waste energy, and instead make small changes around the home. They never fall for impulse buys, and instead use a waiting rule. They never let insurance and contracts renew without checking, and instead review and compare. They never pay to store things they do not use, and instead declutter gently. They never neglect their health and home, and instead invest in small, steady maintenance. And they never waste money keeping up with others, and instead spend on what genuinely matters to them.

None of these habits require a large income. None of them require a complicated spreadsheet. They simply require attention, a bit of patience, and a willingness to ask, is this really worth it to me?

Maybe it is the waiting rule for your next online purchase. Then check your own numbers after a month and see what difference it made.
