There's a pair of jeans at the bottom of almost every man's drawer. Light blue, faded at the thighs, a little frayed at the back of the hem where they've dragged on the ground for years. The legs are wide enough to fit a second leg inside, and the fabric pools in soft folds over the tops of the shoes. They're comfortable. They're familiar. One quick thing before we start. We're not here to make anyone feel bad about their body. Our shapes change over the years. Waists move, seats flatten, legs get thinner or stronger depending on how we've lived. That's normal and it's fine. The whole point of good trousers is to work with the body you have right now, so that you look comfortable, upright, and well put together.

So first, why do baggy jeans age a man so much?

There are three reasons. The first is volume. Extra fabric around the seat and thighs doesn't hide anything. It actually adds width, and it makes the lower half look heavier and shorter. The second is the break, which is how the bottom of the trouser meets the shoe. When jeans are too long and too wide, they collapse into a pile of folds at the ankle. That crumpled fabric makes the legs look shorter and the whole outfit look sloppy. And the third is the wash. Light, faded, and heavily whiskered denim looks worn out, and it reflects light around the thighs and knees, drawing attention exactly where we don't want it.

Put those three together, and baggy jeans make a man look like he's given up a little. Which is the opposite of how most of us feel.

So what do we wear instead? Let's go through seven pants, one at a time.

Number one. Dark, straight-leg jeans in a proper fit.

You don't need to give up jeans. You just need better ones. And the change is mostly in three details.

Start with the wash. Choose a dark indigo or a deep rinse denim with little or no fading, no whiskering on the thighs, and no artificial distressing. Dark denim looks smarter, it's slimming, and it pairs with almost everything, from a grey crewneck to a navy blazer. Next, the cut. Look for a straight leg, sometimes called a classic or regular straight. Not skinny, not tight, just a clean line from the hip to the ankle, with a little room in the seat and thigh for comfort. And finally, the length. The hem should just touch the top of your shoe, with at most a small, gentle break. If your jeans are too long, a tailor can shorten them for very little.

Why does this work? Because a straight, dark leg creates one clean vertical line from your waist to your shoes. That line makes you look taller, steadier, and more deliberate.

That's the easy upgrade. But the next pant is where things really change for most men, because it's the backbone of a classic wardrobe.

Number two. Chinos in a mid-rise, tapered fit.

Chinos are cotton twill trousers, lighter and softer than jeans, and they're one of the most versatile things a man can own. The problem is that many of us own the wrong version: pleated, very wide, and in a beige that's almost exactly the color of our skin.

Here's what to look for instead. A flat front, so there's no extra fabric bunching at the stomach. A mid or slightly higher rise, so the waistband sits comfortably at your natural waist, not below the belly. A gentle taper from the knee to the ankle, so the leg looks tidy without feeling tight. And a little stretch in the cotton can help with comfort when you sit, drive, or kneel in the garden.

Color matters too. Instead of pale beige, choose stone, a deeper khaki, navy, olive, or a soft grey. These colors look richer, they contrast better with your skin, and they work with blue shirts, white shirts, and nearly every sweater you own.

Picture it: a pair of olive chinos and a pair of navy chinos hanging side by side on wooden hangers. A man chooses the navy pair, adds a white oxford shirt and brown leather loafers, and walks into a sunny garden for a family lunch.

Number three is the trouser that wealthy men have relied on for generations, and almost nobody talks about it on this side of the water anymore.

Number three. Grey wool flannel trousers.

If there is a single pair of trousers that says quiet, old money style, it's this one. Grey flannel is a soft, brushed wool fabric with a slightly fuzzy, matte surface. It's warm in the cooler months, it drapes beautifully, and it looks expensive even when it isn't.

Why does it work so well after sixty? Because the matte surface absorbs light softly, so it doesn't show every crease or highlight. The weight of the wool makes it hang straight, so it skims the body rather than clinging. And mid grey is one of the most flattering colors there is for men with grey or silver hair, because it echoes the hair and ties the whole look together.

What to look for: a mid grey or charcoal flannel, a flat front or a single soft pleat if you prefer a little more room, a mid or higher rise, and a clean straight or lightly tapered leg. Wear them with a navy jacket, a camel sweater, a blue shirt, and brown shoes, and you have an outfit that would look right almost anywhere.

Number four. Linen trousers for warm weather.

When the temperature rises, a lot of us switch to shorts or to jeans that are far too heavy for the heat. Linen trousers are the elegant answer.

Linen is woven from the flax plant, and it's light, breathable, and cool against the skin. It does wrinkle, and that's part of its charm. On a well-fitted linen trouser, the creases look relaxed and natural, not untidy. The key is the cut. Look for a straight or slightly relaxed leg with a drawstring or a simple button waist, ending at the ankle or just above. Avoid anything too wide or too long, so the fabric doesn't flap or drag.

For color, choose natural ecru, sand, stone, light grey, or navy. A pale linen trouser with a navy shirt, or a navy linen trouser with a white shirt, is about as close to timeless summer style as we can get.

Halfway there. And number five is a trouser most men have heard of but very few actually own, which is a shame, because it might be the most comfortable on this list.

Number five. Corduroy trousers.

Corduroy is cotton with soft, raised ridges running down the length of the fabric, called wales. It has a reputation for being old-fashioned, but in deep, rich colors and a modern cut, it's one of the warmest, most characterful trousers a man can wear in autumn and winter.

Why it works for us: the vertical ridges create subtle lines that lead the eye up and down, which naturally lengthens the leg. The texture catches light gently, giving the fabric a warm depth that flat cotton doesn't have. And corduroy is soft and comfortable, often more forgiving than denim.

What to choose: a medium or fine wale rather than a very chunky one, in colors like chocolate brown, olive, navy, burgundy, or a warm camel. Keep the cut straight or lightly tapered, with a clean hem at the top of the shoe.

Picture it: a pair of chocolate-brown corduroy trousers with a cream cable-knit sweater, worn by a man walking along a quiet country lane lined with autumn leaves. A tweed cap, brown leather boots, a dog trotting just ahead.

Now number six. And this is the one that surprises men, because they think it's only for the office.

Number six. Tailored wool trousers for everyday wear, not just suits.

Many of us only own wool trousers as half of a suit, so we only wear them for formal occasions. But a pair of standalone wool trousers, sometimes called odd trousers, can be worn far more casually than you'd think.

Look for a lightweight wool or wool blend in a fabric with a little texture, such as a tropical wool for summer or a hopsack or twill for the rest of the year. Navy, mid grey, brown, and a muted olive are all wonderful. Choose a flat front or a single pleat, a mid-rise, and a slightly tapered leg. A neat crease down the front of each leg makes them look sharper and gives the leg a long, clean line.

Wear them with a knitted polo, a sweater, or an open-collared shirt, and you'll look relaxed but considerably more refined than you would in jeans. Add a jacket and you're ready for dinner.

And now, number seven. The one I promised at the start. The one nobody talks about.

Number seven. The higher-rise, pleated trouser.

For years, men were told that pleats were old-fashioned and that low-rise trousers were modern. But here's the truth that well-dressed older men have always understood: a higher rise and a soft pleat can be wonderfully flattering after sixty.

Let me explain why. A low-rise trouser sits below the stomach, so the waistband gets pushed down and the shirt bulges over it. That creates a soft horizontal line right across the middle, which is the least flattering line there is. A higher rise, on the other hand, sits at or near the natural waist, covering the stomach smoothly and creating a longer, cleaner leg line from the waist down. The trouser holds everything in place, the shirt tucks in neatly, and the whole silhouette looks taller and more balanced.

And the pleats? A single forward-facing pleat on each side adds a little room at the hips and thighs, which means more comfort when sitting, and it lets the fabric drape straight down instead of pulling across the front. The key is a modest pleat, not a deep, puffy one, and a leg that tapers gently toward the ankle so it doesn't balloon out.

Many of these trousers come with side adjusters, small buckles or tabs on the waistband, instead of belt loops. They let you tighten or loosen the waist without a belt, which creates a cleaner line at the middle. Or you can wear them with braces, which hold the trousers at the natural waist all day.

For fabric and color, start with a mid grey wool, a navy, or a warm brown. Wear them with a tucked-in oxford shirt or a fine merino knit.

Picture it: a man buttoning a light blue oxford shirt, then stepping into a pair of high-rise grey trousers with a single pleat. He fastens the side adjusters, tucks the shirt in, and turns to the mirror. His legs look longer, his waist looks neater, and he stands a little straighter. You can see it on his face.

So that's the seven. But there's one more thing that makes all of these work, and it's worth a couple of minutes, because without it, even the best trousers will disappoint.

It's fit, and specifically, it's tailoring. Almost no trouser fits perfectly straight off the rack. That's true at any age. But after sixty, it's even more true, because our proportions often change in ways standard sizes don't account for. A waist that's moved, a seat that's flatter, legs that are a little shorter or longer than the pattern assumes.

A good local tailor can make a modestly priced pair of trousers look made for you. The three most common adjustments are simple. Taking in or letting out the waist. Shortening the length so the hem sits just at the top of the shoe. And tapering the leg slightly from the knee down for a cleaner line. These small changes often cost little, and they transform how trousers hang.

Picture it: a tailor in a small shop, chalk in hand, kneeling to pin the hem of a pair of grey trousers while a man stands patiently on a low wooden platform in front of a three-way mirror.

There's also the question of the seat and the back. Turn around in the mirror. If there's a lot of loose fabric sagging below your seat, the trousers are too big there, and a tailor can often take that in. If the fabric pulls into horizontal lines across the front of the thighs, they're too tight. What you want is fabric that falls smoothly from the seat to the back of the knee, with no pulling and no sagging.

And then there's the break, which we mentioned earlier. The break is the fold that forms where the bottom of the trouser meets the shoe. For most men over sixty, a slight break or no break at all looks the cleanest. That means the hem just kisses the top of the shoe at the front, and sits a little lower at the back. No pooling, no stacking, and no fabric dragging on the ground.

Now, trousers never work alone. They're always part of a picture with the shoes below and the shirt or sweater above. So let's spend a moment on how to put them together.

Start at the bottom. Slimmer, cleaner trousers need slimmer, cleaner shoes. If you pair a beautiful grey flannel with a chunky white running shoe, the trouser hem will sit awkwardly on top of the thick sole and the whole line breaks. Instead, choose a shoe with a sleeker profile. Suede loafers, leather derbies, chukka boots, or a minimal leather sneaker all sit neatly under a straight or tapered leg. And match your socks to your trousers, so the line from waist to shoe stays unbroken when you sit down.

Then move to the middle. The waist is where trousers and tops meet, and it's where a lot of outfits go wrong. If you tuck a shirt in, use a simple leather belt in a color close to your shoes, or side adjusters on a higher-rise trouser. If you leave a sweater or an overshirt untucked, make sure it ends around the belt line or just below, so it doesn't cut your body in half at the hips. A crewneck sweater that ends neatly at the waist, over a high-rise trouser, is one of the most flattering shapes a man our age can wear.

And finally, the top. Here's a simple rule that helps enormously. Let either the top or the bottom be the lighter color, not both, and not neither. Dark trousers with a lighter shirt or sweater look fresh and balanced. Lighter trousers, like stone chinos or ecru linen, look best with a darker top, like a navy polo or a navy jacket. When both are very dark, the outfit can look heavy. When both are very pale, it can look washed out.

There's also the question of jeans with jackets. Dark straight-leg jeans with a navy or brown jacket, a light blue shirt, and brown leather shoes is one of the most useful combinations a man can own.
