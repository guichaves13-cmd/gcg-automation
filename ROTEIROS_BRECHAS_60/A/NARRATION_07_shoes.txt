Look down at the mat inside your front door. Most of us have the same little pile there. A pair of grey and white running shoes with the laces still tied, a pair of rubber clogs for the garden, and maybe a pair of square-toed black shoes that only come out for weddings and funerals. Now imagine you had to wear one of those pairs to a nice lunch tomorrow. Which would you pick? And how would you feel walking into the restaurant?

That little moment of hesitation is the reason for this video. Shoes are the first thing many people notice and the last thing most men think about. And after sixty, the wrong pair can quietly add years to the whole outfit, no matter how good the shirt or jacket is. The right pair does the opposite. It makes you look grounded, deliberate, and comfortable in your own skin.

So today we're going through seven shoes that tend to work against men over sixty, and for every one of them, the shoe that old money men, the men whose families have dressed well for generations, wear instead. And I'll tell you right now, shoe number seven is the one nobody talks about. It isn't a style you'd guess, and I'd bet a good number of you are wearing it as you watch this.

One promise before we begin. Comfort matters. Our feet have carried us a long way, and nothing on this list asks you to suffer. Every replacement we'll talk about can be comfortable, supportive, and easy to walk in. It's not about pain. It's about choosing better. Let's start.

Number one. The chunky, cushioned athletic sneaker worn with everything.

This is the most common shoe in the world for men our age, and I understand why. It's soft, it's easy, and many of them are very well made for walking. The problem isn't the shoe itself. The problem is wearing it to dinner, to church, to the theater, and to a grandchild's graduation.

Here's why it reads older. The sole is thick and wide, often flaring out past the foot, so it makes your feet look bigger and heavier than they are. That pulls the eye down and visually shortens the leg. The mesh uppers, plastic overlays, and reflective details are designed for the track, not the street, so they clash with trousers and collared shirts. And bright white or neon accents catch the light and become the loudest thing in the room.

What to wear instead is a minimal leather sneaker. Look for smooth leather or suede, a slim cupsole that follows the shape of your foot, and very few seams or logos. Off-white, white, light grey, navy, or a soft brown suede are all excellent. If you need extra support, many of these sneakers will take a removable cushioned insole, so you can keep the comfort without the bulk. Save the running shoes for actual walking, the gym, or the trail, where they belong and where they look right.

That's a big change, but an easy one. Shoe number two is something many of us bought specifically because we thought it was the dressy option.

Number two. The square-toed, shiny black dress shoe.

You know this shoe. Broad, blocky toe, a thick rubber sole, and a plastic-like shine that never changes no matter how you polish it. It was popular for a long stretch of years, and a lot of us still have a pair for formal occasions.

Why it reads older: the square toe makes the foot look short and wide, like a little box at the end of your leg. Corrected-grain leather, the kind with that glassy coated finish, reflects light in a hard, artificial way, and it creases in sharp, cracked lines rather than softening with age. And the overall shape tends to look dated, which is exactly the message we want to avoid.

What to wear instead is a dress shoe with a gently rounded or slightly almond-shaped toe, made from full-grain or calfskin leather that takes polish and develops a soft glow over time. For formal events, a black or dark brown cap-toe oxford. For everything else, a dark brown or burgundy derby, which has a slightly more open lacing and tends to be roomier and more comfortable across the top of the foot. A leather or rubber-studded sole, stitched on so it can be replaced, is the classic choice.

Now here's where it gets interesting, because shoe number three is a summer favorite, and a lot of men will want to defend it.

Number three. Sport sandals with Velcro straps.

The rugged hiking sandal with thick nylon straps, hook-and-loop fasteners, and a heavy rubber sole. Often worn with socks. Often worn to places nowhere near a river.

Why it reads older: the straps are wide and technical, so they draw attention to the feet and add visual bulk. Nylon webbing fades, frays, and stains with time. The thick sole makes the foot look heavy. And when they're paired with socks, which plenty of us do for comfort, the whole look becomes a punchline, which is the last thing we want.

What to wear instead in warm weather is a leather sandal or a slip-on shoe that breathes. For sandals, look for a simple slide or a two-strap design in brown leather, with a cushioned footbed and a slim sole. If you'd rather keep your toes covered, try a canvas or linen espadrille, a suede loafer worn without socks, or a deck shoe in brown leather. All of these keep you cool and comfortable while looking relaxed and deliberate.

Number four. The rubber clog, worn outside the garden.

Bright plastic clogs with the holes in the top. They're very practical for gardening, for the boat, or for slipping on to take the rubbish out. But many of us have started wearing them to the shop, to lunch, even on trips.

Here's why they read older. Molded plastic has no structure, so it makes the foot look round and shapeless. The bright colors and the perforations are designed to be playful, which can look out of step with the rest of a grown man's wardrobe. And because there's no heel shape, the walk changes. It becomes more of a shuffle, which reads older faster than almost anything else.

What to wear instead is a comfortable slip-on shoe that still has shape. The suede loafer is the hero here. A soft unlined suede penny loafer or driving shoe in tobacco brown, navy, or a deep green slips on and off just as easily as a clog, but it has a real heel and a real shape, so you walk taller. For house shoes, a pair of proper leather or wool-felt slippers with a sole is far more comfortable and dignified than plastic. Keep the clogs by the garden door, where they are perfectly at home.

Number five. Wide, orthopedic-looking walking shoes in grey or black.

Some of us genuinely need extra support, a wider fit, or space for an insert, and that is nothing to be embarrassed about. The issue is that many shoes made for support look clinical. Bulky soles, hook-and-loop closures, and flat grey or black uppers that look as though they came straight from a waiting room.

Why it reads older: these shoes often put function so far ahead of form that they signal medical need before anything else. The wide, rounded shape and the dull synthetic leather absorb light without any richness, and the hook-and-loop strap looks practical, but not personal.

What to wear instead is a well-made leather shoe that happens to be supportive. Many traditional shoemakers offer wider fittings and deep, removable footbeds that can take an orthotic. Look for a lace-up derby or a chukka boot in a soft brown leather or suede, with a cushioned crepe or rubber sole. A chukka boot, also called a desert boot, is a quiet, ankle-height boot with two or three eyelets, and it's one of the most comfortable, versatile shoes a man can own. If laces are difficult, choose a Chelsea boot with elastic sides and a pull tab, or a loafer in a wider fit. The point is support you can't see.

There's also a simple test you can do in the shop. Stand in front of a full-length mirror wearing the trousers you wear most often, and look at the shoe from a few steps back. Does it look like part of the outfit, or like a separate piece of equipment attached to the bottom of it? A good supportive shoe disappears into the outfit. A clinical one sticks out. And ask about the insole. If it lifts out, you can bring your own orthotic and try it inside the shoe before you buy. Take your time. A shoe that feels right in the shop, with your own insert, will usually feel right for years.

Suede deserves a special mention here, because many men avoid it out of worry. Suede is softer than smooth leather, it molds to the foot quickly, and it tends to be gentler on sensitive toes and joints. With a protective spray when it's new and a quick brush now and then, it's far more durable than its reputation suggests.

Shoe number six is a fashion shoe, and it's a trap that catches men of every age, not just us.

Number six. The overly trendy, ultra-pointed or heavily distressed shoe.

Long, sharply pointed toes that stretch out past the foot. Artificially scuffed or "antiqued" leather. Bright contrast stitching, chunky buckles, or exotic finishes. The kind of shoe that's designed to be noticed.

Why it reads older, strangely enough: when a man over sixty wears a very trendy shoe, it can look like he's trying hard to keep up, and that effort is visible. Elongated toes make the feet look disproportionately long. Artificial distressing looks worn in the wrong way, and loud details distract from the calm, confident look that suits us so well.

What to wear instead is the timeless version. A plain-toe or cap-toe shoe with a classic, gently rounded shape. Natural leather that ages honestly, gaining creases and patina from your own walking rather than a factory. Colors that have worked for a hundred years: dark brown, mid brown, burgundy, black, and suede in tobacco or navy. Old money style is famously unbothered by trends. The shoes are good, they're cared for, and they're often worn for many years.

And now, shoe number seven. The one I promised at the start. The one nobody talks about.

Number seven. Any shoe, worn with the wrong socks.

That's right. It isn't a shoe at all. It's what happens between the shoe and the trouser. And it changes everything.

Think about how many of us wear thick white athletic socks with everything. With leather shoes, with loafers, with dark trousers. Or socks that have gone thin at the heel and slouched at the ankle. Or very short socks that reveal a band of bare shin whenever we sit down.

Why it reads older: white athletic socks create a bright flash between a dark trouser and a dark shoe, breaking the leg line in half and drawing the eye straight to the ankle. Slouched socks gather in wrinkles. A bare stretch of shin when you cross your legs looks unfinished. And all of this happens right next to the shoes we've worked so hard to get right.

What to wear instead is simple. With dress shoes and trousers, a mid-calf or over-the-calf sock in fine cotton or merino wool, in a color that matches or relates to your trousers. Navy socks with navy trousers. Charcoal with grey. Brown or burgundy with tan chinos. This creates one unbroken line from your waist to your shoes, which makes you look taller and more streamlined. For loafers and sneakers in warm weather, a no-show sock that sits below the shoe line keeps things clean and comfortable. And save the white athletic sock for the gym.

Picture it: a drawer of neatly rolled socks in navy, charcoal, brown, and burgundy. A man chooses a navy pair, sits on the edge of the bed, and pulls them up over his calf before putting on his dark brown derbies. When he crosses his legs later at dinner, all you see is a clean line of navy.

Now let's talk about something that ties all seven together, because shoes need a little more than choosing the right pair.

The first thing is fit. Our feet can change as the years go on. They can widen, lengthen a little, or become more sensitive. That means the size you wore at forty may not be the size you need now. Next time you shop, have both feet measured, preferably in the afternoon, when feet are naturally a little larger. Try shoes on with the socks you'll actually wear. And walk around the shop for a few minutes. A good shoe should feel snug at the heel, with room to wiggle your toes.

The second thing is care. Old money men don't necessarily own the most shoes. They own good shoes and they look after them. Cedar shoe trees inside every pair of leather shoes help them hold their shape and absorb moisture. A horsehair brush after each wear. A little cream or wax polish every few weeks. A suede brush for loafers and chukkas. And when the soles wear down, a good cobbler can replace them, which means a pair of well-made leather shoes can last for many years.

Picture it: a row of five pairs of shoes on a low wooden rack, each with a pair of cedar shoe trees inside. Dark brown derbies, burgundy loafers, tobacco suede chukkas, off-white leather sneakers, and a pair of black cap-toe oxfords at the end. That is genuinely all most men need.

The third thing is rotation. Leather shoes last much longer when you don't wear the same pair two days in a row. Giving them a day to rest and dry out keeps the leather supple and the shape intact. It also means you'll naturally start choosing shoes to match the day, the weather, and the outfit, which is exactly the habit that makes well-dressed men look so effortless.

Let's make this practical, because knowing what to avoid is only half of it. Let's walk through an ordinary week and see which shoe fits each moment.

Monday morning, a trip to the bank and the hardware store, then coffee with a neighbor. This is a day for the minimal leather sneaker or the suede chukka. Pair either one with dark jeans or chinos and a crewneck sweater. You're comfortable on your feet all morning, and you look like a man with somewhere to be.

Wednesday, lunch with old colleagues at a nice restaurant. Reach for the suede loafer or the brown derby. With grey trousers and a navy jacket, that shoe makes the whole outfit feel finished. Friday, a walk in the countryside or along the coast. This is where a sturdy shoe earns its place. A leather boot with a lugged rubber sole, in a dark brown or oiled tan, handles mud, gravel, and wet grass beautifully, and it looks far more natural on a country lane than a bright running shoe. Picture a pair of dark brown leather boots, flecked with mud, set down on newspaper by a farmhouse door at the end of the walk.

Saturday, a family barbecue in the summer. Leather slides, espadrilles, or unlined loafers without socks. Comfortable for standing around the grill, easy to slip off on the grass, and relaxed in exactly the right way.

Sunday, a wedding, a christening, or a service. The black or dark brown cap-toe oxford, polished, with dark socks that match your trousers. This is the one pair you don't wear often, and so it should be the pair that's always ready. Notice what happened there. Five or six pairs cover an entire week, every season, and every occasion. That's the old money approach in a nutshell. Not many shoes. The right shoes.

And one more thought about color, because it's where men often get stuck. Brown shoes are far more versatile than most of us were taught. Dark brown works with navy, grey, olive, tan, and even charcoal. Mid-brown and tobacco suede are the most relaxed and summery. Burgundy is a wonderful middle ground, richer than brown and softer than black. Black shoes are best saved for formal events and evenings. If you own one pair of black shoes and three or four in shades of brown, you'll almost never be stuck.

A small note on laces, too. Flat waxed cotton laces in a color close to the leather look neater than thick round ones. And if bending to tie laces has become uncomfortable, there's no shame at all in a long-handled shoehorn by the door, or in choosing loafers and Chelsea boots for most days. Picture a polished wooden shoehorn hanging on a hook beside the coat rack. It's one of those small, civilized objects that makes daily life easier and keeps the backs of your good shoes from being crushed.
