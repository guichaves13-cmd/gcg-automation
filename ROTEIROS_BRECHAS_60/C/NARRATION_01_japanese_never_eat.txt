It is just after seven in the morning in a quiet kitchen in the Japanese countryside. A small bowl of miso soup is steaming beside a piece of grilled mackerel, its skin crisp and golden. There is a little mound of rice, a few slices of pickled cucumber, and a cup of green tea that is still too hot to drink. The woman setting this table is well past seventy. She moves slowly, but she moves with purpose. And here is what you will not see on that table. No box of sugary cereal. No glass of orange soda. No stack of buttered toast with jam.

That breakfast is not a special occasion. In many traditional Japanese homes, it is simply a Tuesday.

Today we are going to walk through eight foods and habits that you rarely see in traditional Japanese homes, especially among older adults who still eat the way their parents and grandparents did. And for every single one, we will look at what they eat instead, so you can borrow the idea for your own kitchen, wherever you live, whether that is Ohio, Manchester, Toronto, or Perth.

Stay with me until the very end, because the eighth item is not really a food at all. It is a simple habit at the table, and many people say it is the one that changed the way they feel after every meal. I will explain exactly what it is when we get there.

Before we begin, one quick and honest note. This video is general information, not medical advice. Everyone is different. Your doctor knows your full picture. Think of this video as inspiration to bring to that conversation.

Now, let us talk about how traditional Japanese eating actually looks. And it is important to say this clearly. Not every person in Japan eats this way. Modern Japan has convenience stores, burger chains, and plenty of sweets, just like everywhere else. What we are describing is a traditional pattern, the kind of meals you would often find in an older family home, in a rural village, or at a grandmother's table. It is a pattern built over generations, and it has a lot to teach us.

Let us start with the first one.

Number one. Sugary breakfast cereal.

Picture the cereal aisle in a typical supermarket. Bright boxes, cartoon characters, colorful loops and flakes coated in sugar. For many of us, that is what breakfast looked like for decades. Pour, add milk, eat quickly, and head out the door.

In a traditional Japanese home, you rarely see that. Breakfast is treated as a real meal, not a snack in a bowl. Instead of sweet cereal, the morning often begins with a warm, savory spread. A small serving of rice. A bowl of miso soup with soft cubes of tofu and a few strands of seaweed floating in it. A piece of grilled fish. Perhaps a rolled omelet, cut into neat golden slices. And something pickled on the side.

Why does that matter, especially after sixty? Because a breakfast like this brings protein to the very start of the day. The fish, the egg, the tofu. As we get older, our muscles need steady support, and protein is one of the building blocks they rely on. Many older adults eat very little protein in the morning and then try to catch up at dinner. A savory breakfast spreads that protein out more evenly.

And there is something else. A warm savory breakfast tends to feel satisfying in a calm, steady way. There is no sugary rush followed by that mid morning slump, when you find yourself reaching for a biscuit at ten o'clock.

You do not need to cook a full Japanese breakfast every day to borrow this idea. You could simply have two eggs with some sautéed spinach. A bowl of plain yogurt with nuts. A piece of leftover salmon with a slice of whole grain toast. The principle is simple. Make breakfast savory and protein forward, and let the sugar be the exception, not the rule.

Now, the next one surprises a lot of people, because it is not about what is on the plate. It is about how much of it there is.

Number two. Oversized portions.

Imagine a restaurant meal in many Western countries. A large dinner plate, filled edge to edge. A big pile of pasta, a thick steak, a mountain of fries. And often, a basket of bread before the meal even arrives.

In traditional Japanese homes, the table looks very different. Instead of one huge plate, you see several small dishes. A little bowl of rice. A small plate with a piece of fish. A tiny dish of simmered vegetables. Another with a few pickles. Each one is modest. Together, they make a complete meal, full of variety, but not overwhelming.

There is a well known phrase from the island of Okinawa, hara hachi bu. Roughly translated, it means eat until you are about eighty percent full. It is a gentle reminder to stop eating when you are satisfied, not when you are stuffed. Many older people there grew up hearing it at the table, said almost like a little blessing before the meal.

Think about how that feels. You finish dinner, and you feel comfortable. You could go for a short walk. You are not loosening your belt or reaching for antacids. That is the feeling hara hachi bu is aiming for.

Small dishes help make this natural. When food comes in little bowls, your eyes see a full table, but your stomach receives a reasonable amount. It is a quiet trick, and it works at any age.

Here is something you can try tonight. Use a smaller plate, or even a few small bowls. Serve yourself once. Eat slowly, and when you feel about four fifths full, set your chopsticks, or your fork, down. Give it ten minutes. Most people find they are perfectly content.

Now let us move on, because number three is something many of us drink every single day without thinking twice.

Number three. Sugary soft drinks with meals.

Picture a fast food tray. A burger, some fries, and an enormous paper cup of cola, beads of condensation running down the side. For many people, a sweet fizzy drink with lunch or dinner is just normal.

In a traditional Japanese home, the drink on the table is usually much simpler. Green tea, poured from a small clay teapot into little cups without handles. Or roasted barley tea, called mugicha, served cold in summer from a tall glass pitcher in the refrigerator. Sometimes just water.

These drinks have no added sugar. They are calm, gentle, and they go well with food without overpowering it. Green tea is served after meals, between meals, when guests arrive, and in quiet moments in the afternoon. It is part of daily life in a way that feels almost like a ritual.

Limiting sugary drinks is one of the most widely agreed upon pieces of nutrition advice. Sweetened sodas and juices can add a lot of sugar to the day very quickly, without making you feel full. For older adults, especially those watching their blood sugar, that is worth paying attention to.

If you love a fizzy drink, you do not have to give up the bubbles. Try sparkling water with a slice of lemon or a few crushed mint leaves. Or brew a pitcher of unsweetened iced tea and keep it in the fridge, the way Japanese families keep barley tea ready on a hot day. One small note. Green tea does contain caffeine and some vitamin K, so if you take blood thinners or are sensitive to caffeine, that is a good thing to mention to your doctor. Barley tea is naturally caffeine free, and many people enjoy it in the evening.

So far we have looked at breakfast, portions, and drinks. But the next item is about the main course itself, and it is one that many traditional cooks simply do not build their meals around.

Number four. Processed meats every day.

Imagine a classic diner breakfast. Strips of bacon sizzling in a pan. Sausages lined up on the grill. Or at lunch, a sandwich layered with salami and ham. Many of us grew up eating processed meats almost daily.

In traditional Japanese cooking, the everyday protein is more often fish. A fillet of salmon brushed with a little soy sauce and grilled until the edges caramelize. A whole small mackerel, salted and broiled, served with a wedge of lemon and a little grated daikon radish on the side. Simmered sardines. A few slices of fresh fish on a small plate. Alongside fish, you see eggs, tofu, and soybeans in many forms.

Health organizations widely recommend limiting processed meats like bacon, ham, hot dogs, and deli slices. That does not mean you can never enjoy a slice of ham at a holiday meal. It simply means that it is wise not to build your daily menu around them.

Fish, on the other hand, is one of the foods most often encouraged. Oily fish like salmon, mackerel, and sardines bring protein and healthy fats. For older adults, having fish a couple of times a week is a classic recommendation in many countries.

And here is the good news. You do not need to visit a fish market in Tokyo. Tinned sardines, tinned salmon, and frozen fish fillets are affordable and easy to keep in the pantry or freezer. Mash some tinned salmon with a little lemon and pepper and spread it on whole grain crackers. Bake a frozen fillet with a drizzle of oil and some vegetables on the same tray. Simple.

Tofu is another wonderful option. Soft, mild, and easy to chew, it absorbs whatever flavors you give it. A few cubes in soup, a slice pan fried until golden, or crumbled into scrambled eggs. Tofu is a good source of plant protein, and in many Japanese homes, it appears on the table nearly every day.

If you take blood thinners, please speak with your doctor before adding a lot of fish oil supplements, but fish as part of normal meals is something most people can enjoy. And if you have kidney disease, your doctor or dietitian may want to guide how much protein is right for you, so that conversation matters.

Now, number five is all about the way food is cooked. And once you notice it, you will see it everywhere in traditional Japanese kitchens.

Number five. Deep fried fast food as a daily habit.

Think of a drive through window. A paper bag, warm and a little greasy, filled with fried chicken and fries. It is quick, it is tasty, and for many people it has become a weekly, or even daily, habit.

Now, to be fair, Japan has fried foods too. Tempura, crispy and light, is famous all over the world. Breaded pork cutlets are popular. But in traditional homes, these are often treated as a treat or something for a special meal, not the foundation of everyday eating.

The everyday cooking methods are gentler. Simmering, steaming, grilling, and quick blanching. Picture a shallow pot on the stove, with chunks of pumpkin and carrot gently bubbling in a light broth of soy sauce and a little mirin. Picture a bamboo steamer releasing a cloud of steam, with dumplings or vegetables inside. Picture spinach dipped briefly in boiling water, then squeezed, cut into neat bundles, and sprinkled with toasted sesame seeds.

These methods let the taste of the food itself shine through. They use far less oil than deep frying, and they keep vegetables soft and easy to eat, which many older adults appreciate, especially if chewing has become a little more difficult.

Simmered dishes, called nimono, are a wonderful example. The vegetables become tender, they soak up the savory broth, and they reheat beautifully the next day. Many grandmothers make a big pot and serve a small portion at each meal for a few days.

You can try this at home with whatever vegetables you have. Carrots, potatoes, onions, green beans, even mushrooms. Simmer them gently in a little low sodium stock with a splash of soy sauce, and you have a warm, comforting side dish that feels like a hug in a bowl.

Okay. We are past the halfway point, and number six is something that many of us were raised to expect after every dinner.

Number six. A big dessert after every meal.

Picture a slice of chocolate cake on a plate, with a scoop of ice cream melting beside it. Or a bowl of pudding with whipped cream. In many Western homes, dinner is not quite finished until something sweet arrives.

In traditional Japanese homes, the end of a meal is usually much quieter. Often, it is simply a cup of green tea. Sometimes there is fruit. A few slices of a ripe persimmon in autumn, its flesh bright orange. A mandarin orange peeled at the table in winter, the scent filling the room. A small cluster of grapes, or a wedge of melon on a special day.

Traditional Japanese sweets do exist, and they are lovely. Little cakes made from sweet bean paste, beautifully shaped and colored to reflect the seasons. But they tend to be small, and they are often served with tea in the afternoon, as a moment of pleasure, rather than piled up after every dinner.

The idea of enjoying fruit as dessert is one that almost every nutrition expert can agree with. Fruit brings natural sweetness along with fiber, water, and vitamins. A bowl of berries, a sliced apple, or a ripe pear can satisfy that craving for something sweet at the end of a meal.

And when you do want a real dessert, make it a small one, and really enjoy it. Sit down. Take your time. Notice the flavor. A little treat eaten slowly can be far more satisfying than a large one eaten quickly in front of the television.

If you take diabetes medication, it is worth talking with your doctor about how fruit fits into your day, because even natural sugar can affect your levels. But for most people, swapping a big dessert for fresh fruit is a gentle and positive step.

Number seven is all about the moments between meals, the hours when many of us reach into the cupboard almost without thinking.

Number seven. Packaged snack foods.

Imagine the snack shelf at a gas station or corner shop. Bags of potato chips. Cheese puffs. Packaged cookies and sweet bars wrapped in shiny plastic. These ultra processed snacks are designed to be easy to eat, and very hard to stop eating.

In traditional Japanese homes, snacks were historically simpler, and often closer to real food. A small bowl of edamame, the young green soybeans, boiled in their pods and sprinkled with a little salt. You squeeze each pod and the bright green beans pop right out. A few sheets of roasted seaweed, crisp and light. A handful of roasted soybeans or nuts. A rice ball, called onigiri, wrapped in seaweed, with a little piece of salmon or pickled plum tucked inside. A small roasted sweet potato, still warm, its skin wrinkled and its flesh soft and sweet.

That roasted sweet potato is a lovely example. In Japan, you can still sometimes hear the call of a sweet potato vendor on a cold evening, selling them hot from a stone oven on the back of a small truck. It is a snack that feels like a treat, but it is simply a vegetable, cooked slowly.

Limiting ultra processed foods is one of the most widely shared recommendations in nutrition today. These foods often bring a lot of refined starch, added sugar, salt, and fats, with very little fiber or protein to keep you satisfied.

So what can you keep in your own kitchen? Frozen edamame, which cooks in a few minutes. A bag of unsalted nuts, portioned into small cups so you do not eat the whole bag. Plain popcorn. Hard boiled eggs in the fridge. Carrot sticks and cucumber slices with a little hummus. A baked sweet potato reheated with a sprinkle of cinnamon.

These snacks bring protein, fiber, or both, which helps keep you steady until the next meal. And they feel like real food, because they are.

And now we have reached number eight. Remember, I said at the beginning that this one is not really a food. It is a habit at the table. But before we get there, there is one food detail tied to it that you will want to hear.

Number eight. A plate with no vegetables, no fermented foods, and no time to enjoy it.

Picture a familiar Western dinner. A large piece of meat, a pile of mashed potatoes, perhaps a little gravy. And not much else. No greens. No color. And it is often eaten quickly, sometimes standing at the counter, sometimes in front of the evening news.

In a traditional Japanese meal, vegetables are everywhere. Not just one side of peas, but several little dishes. Spinach with sesame. Simmered daikon radish. A small salad of seaweed and cucumber dressed with rice vinegar. Mushrooms in soup. Pickled vegetables in a tiny bowl. Even when the portions are small, the variety is wide.

And notice the fermented foods. Miso, made from fermented soybeans, in that steaming bowl of soup. Natto, a sticky fermented soybean dish that many people find surprising at first, stirred until it becomes stringy and served over rice. Pickled vegetables, called tsukemono, crisp and tangy. Soy sauce itself is a fermented product. Fermented foods have been part of the Japanese table for centuries, and they add deep, savory flavor without needing much else.

There is one balancing point I want to share honestly. Miso, soy sauce, and pickles can be quite salty. So if you have high blood pressure or kidney concerns, you will want to enjoy these in small amounts, and look for reduced sodium versions where you can. A little goes a long way. Ask your doctor what is right for you. And natto, like leafy greens, contains vitamin K, so if you take blood thinners such as warfarin, please talk to your doctor before you add it to your regular meals.

Now. Here is the habit I promised you.

In traditional Japanese homes, meals are often eaten slowly, seated, and together. Before eating, people say a simple word, itadakimasu, a way of expressing gratitude for the food and everyone who helped bring it to the table. After eating, they say gochisousama, a thank you for the meal.

These are small words. But they create a pause. A moment of attention. You sit down. You notice the food in front of you. You eat with chopsticks, one small bite at a time. You put them down between bites to talk, or to sip your tea.

Eating slowly gives your body time to notice that it is full. It pairs naturally with hara hachi bu, eating until you are about eighty percent satisfied. And eating with others, or even simply eating with attention when you are alone, can make a meal feel more nourishing in every sense of the word.

You do not have to learn Japanese words to try this. Before your next meal, simply sit down at a table. Turn off the television. Take one breath. Look at your plate and say thank you, out loud or in your head, for the food in front of you. Then eat slowly, and notice how you feel at the end.

Many people find that this one habit changes more than any single food ever could.

So let us bring it all together. Picture that kitchen we started in. The steaming bowl of miso soup, the grilled mackerel, the little mound of rice, the pickles, and the cup of green tea. Every item on that table reflects the ideas we have walked through today.

Number one. Instead of sugary cereal, a savory breakfast with protein, like fish, eggs, or tofu.

Number two. Instead of oversized portions, several small dishes and the gentle wisdom of hara hachi bu.

Number three. Instead of sugary soft drinks, green tea, barley tea, or water.

Number four. Instead of daily processed meats, fish a few times a week, along with eggs, tofu, and soybeans.

Number five. Instead of daily deep fried fast food, gentle cooking. Simmering, steaming, grilling.

Number six. Instead of a big dessert after every meal, fresh fruit, and small sweets enjoyed slowly.

Number seven. Instead of packaged snacks, real food snacks like edamame, nuts, rice balls, and roasted sweet potato.

And number eight. Instead of a plain plate eaten in a hurry, plenty of vegetables, a little fermented food, and the simple habit of eating slowly with gratitude.

None of this requires you to move to Japan, buy special equipment, or give up the foods you love from your own culture. You can make a pot of vegetable soup the way your mother did and still borrow the idea of small bowls. You can enjoy a Sunday roast and still add two kinds of vegetables and a smaller portion of meat. You can keep your morning coffee and simply add an egg to your breakfast.

The traditional Japanese table is not about strict rules. It is about balance, variety, modest portions, and respect for food. Those are ideas that belong to everyone, at every age.

If you would like a simple place to start this week, pick just one. Maybe it is fish twice this week. Maybe it is fruit instead of dessert three nights out of seven. Maybe it is simply sitting down at the table without the television on. One small change, done with care, is worth more than ten changes you abandon by Friday.
