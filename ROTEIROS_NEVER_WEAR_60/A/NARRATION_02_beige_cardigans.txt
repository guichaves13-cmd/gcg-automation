There is a beige cardigan hanging on the back of a chair in my kitchen. It is long, it is soft, it falls almost to my knees, and the pockets have stretched into two little sad pouches from years of holding tissues and reading glasses. I bought it because it went with everything. And one morning, a few winters ago, I caught my reflection in the window of a bakery on rue Cler while I was wearing it, and for a moment I did not recognize myself. I looked like someone waiting for something to happen. Not someone making it happen.

That was the morning I started paying very close attention to what the women I admire in Paris actually wear over their shoulders when the air turns cool. Because they do get cold, just like us. They do want comfort, just like us. But almost none of them reach for that long, shapeless beige cardigan.

So today I am going to show you seven layers that French women over sixty wear instead. Seven real pieces, with colors, fabrics, and exactly how they put them together. And stay with me for the fifth layer, because it is the one I now wear more than anything else, and it solved a problem I had been struggling with for years, the problem of looking put together on the days when I feel anything but.

First, though, I want to be fair to the cardigan. Because I do not think we choose it by accident.

The long beige cardigan feels like a hug. It covers the parts of ourselves we feel shy about, the upper arms, the tummy, the hips. It goes with grey, with black, with brown, with denim. It does not ask anything of us. And that is precisely the problem. It does not ask anything, and so it gives nothing back.

Let me explain what happens visually, in terms of light, line, proportion and fabric.

Light first. Most of these cardigans are a flat, slightly greyish beige, the color of oatmeal or of an old envelope. Next to the face, especially as our skin becomes paler and our hair turns silver, that tone drains color out of the complexion. There is no contrast and no warmth. The face and the garment blur into one soft, tired shade.

Line next. A long open cardigan creates two vertical curtains hanging from the shoulders, and nothing in between holds them. So they swing, they sag, and the eye reads the whole body as wider than it is, because there is no waist, no edge, no structure anywhere.

Proportion. When a cardigan falls to the mid-thigh or the knee, it cuts the leg right at its least flattering point. The legs look shorter, the torso looks longer, and the whole figure sinks downward.

And fabric. These cardigans are often a loose acrylic blend that pills quickly, stretches at the pockets, and loses its shape in the wash. After a few months it looks lived in, and not in the charming way.

So, what do French women wear instead? Let us go one by one.

The first layer is the navy cashmere crewneck.

If I had to choose one knit for the rest of my life, it would be this. A fine, close-fitting crewneck sweater in navy cashmere or in a soft merino if cashmere is not for you. Not oversized, not tight, but skimming the body, ending at the hip bone or just above.

Why navy rather than beige? Because navy is the gentlest dark color you can put near a mature face. It is softer than black, which can be harsh and cast shadows under the eyes, and it is richer than grey. It makes silver hair look bright and intentional. It makes the whites of the eyes look clearer. It gives the face a frame without stealing the light.

And the crewneck line, sitting neatly at the base of the neck, gives a clean horizontal edge that draws attention up toward the face. The shorter length shows the waist, or at least suggests it, and lets the legs look their full length.

How do they wear it? Over a white shirt with the collar peeking out, the cuffs folded back over the sleeves. With grey flannel trousers and loafers. With dark jeans and a long silk scarf. Tucked loosely just at the front into a midi skirt. Some women I know drape a second sweater over the shoulders, sleeves knotted in front, which is very French and adds a layer of warmth without any bulk at all.

Picture a woman at a corner table in a café on Place des Vosges, reading a newspaper. Navy cashmere crewneck, a little white collar showing, a pair of tortoiseshell glasses pushed up into her silver hair. The arcades behind her are golden in the afternoon light. She looks calm, clear, and entirely herself.

The second layer is the camel blazer.

Now, I know a blazer can sound formal. You might be thinking, I am not going to an office anymore, why do I need a blazer? But this is not a corporate blazer. It is a soft, slightly relaxed jacket in camel wool or a wool blend, single-breasted, with a shoulder that is gently structured, not padded like a linebacker.

Here is why it works where the cardigan fails. The blazer has a shoulder. That small piece of structure lifts the whole upper body and gives you a posture you did not even have to work for. It has lapels that create a gentle V, which elongates the neck and the torso. And it has a defined length, usually at the hip, which gives the body a clear proportion.

And camel, unlike flat beige, is a warm color. It has gold and caramel in it. It reflects warmth back onto the skin, especially in autumn light. Camel next to silver hair is one of the loveliest combinations there is.

How do they wear it? Over a white T-shirt and jeans on a weekend. Over a navy crewneck with grey trousers for lunch with a friend. Over a black turtleneck with a black skirt for the theater. With sleeves pushed up slightly to show the wrist and a watch. The camel blazer is the piece that makes even the simplest outfit look like a decision.

Picture a woman crossing the courtyard of the Palais-Royal between those striped columns, her camel blazer open over a white T-shirt, dark jeans, white sneakers, a small leather bag across her body. She is walking quickly. She has somewhere to be.

But a blazer is only one kind of structure. The third layer adds something else, something playful, and I want you to watch how it changes the mood of an outfit.

The third layer is the breton striped top, layered.

The marinière, the striped boat-neck top, is perhaps the most famous French garment of all. And many women of our age think of it as a summer piece, a seaside piece. But in Paris, the women I watch wear it all year as a layering piece, and that is the clever part.

Why does it work instead of the cardigan? Stripes bring energy. Horizontal navy and white stripes create movement and light. The white bounces light up onto the face, and the navy gives definition. Where a beige cardigan blends you into the background, stripes say I am here, and I have a sense of humor.

The boat neckline, wide and shallow, is also wonderfully flattering. It shows the collarbones, which are often one of the most elegant parts of a woman's body as she gets older, and it widens the shoulders just a little, which balances the hips.

How do they layer it? Under a navy blazer. Under a trench coat. With a navy crewneck over it and just the stripes showing at the hem and the neck. Under a denim jacket on a spring day. Some women wear it under a thin black sweater, so only a narrow band of stripes peeks out at the collar, like a secret.

The trick is to choose a striped top in a substantial cotton jersey, not a thin stretchy one. A heavier cotton holds its shape, does not cling, and falls in a clean straight line from the shoulder.

Picture a woman on a bench along the Canal Saint-Martin, a paper cup of coffee in her hands. A navy and white striped top under an open navy peacoat, cropped black trousers, ballet flats. The water is green and still, and the iron footbridge arches behind her. She looks relaxed and alive.

Before we move on to the fourth layer, I would love to know something. Tell me in the comments: are you a cardigan woman? Do you have one you love, or one you secretly want to give away? And what color is it? I have a feeling a lot of us are in the same beige club, and there is no shame in it. We are just ready for something new.

The fourth layer is the cropped boiled-wool jacket.

Boiled wool is a fabric I think every woman over sixty should know. It is knitted wool that has been felted, washed and compressed until it becomes thick, dense and almost cloth-like. It is warm like a sweater but holds its shape like a jacket. It does not wrinkle. It does not sag. It does not cling.

A cropped boiled-wool jacket is short, usually ending at the waist or just above the hip, with a round or small stand-up collar, and often just a few buttons. It looks a little like a soft Austrian or alpine jacket, and the French have been wearing it for generations.

Why does it work so beautifully? Because of proportion. The cropped length shows where your waist is and makes your legs look longer. It is the exact opposite of the long cardigan, which hides the waist and shortens the legs. And because the fabric is dense and matte, it creates a smooth, clean surface that skims over the body rather than clinging to it or collapsing.

What colors? Charcoal, navy, a deep forest green, a soft red, an oatmeal that is warmer and creamier than the flat beige we are leaving behind. Bottle green with silver hair is truly lovely.

How do they wear it? Over a fine turtleneck with wide-leg trousers. Over a shirt dress with boots. With a midi skirt and tights. Buttoned all the way up on a cold morning, or open over a striped top on a mild one.

Picture a woman at the Saturday market on Boulevard Raspail, choosing tomatoes with great seriousness. A forest green boiled-wool jacket buttoned up, a grey turtleneck, charcoal trousers, a wicker basket on her arm. She asks the vendor a question, laughs at his answer, and moves on to the cheese. Everything about her looks warm and deliberate.

Now I promised you that the fifth layer is the one I wear more than any other. It is the one that saved me on those mornings when I feel unfinished, a little slow, not ready to face the day.

The fifth layer is the trench coat.

Yes, I know, the trench coat is technically a coat, not a layer. But in Paris, the trench is worn the way many women wear a cardigan. It goes on in the morning and comes off in the evening. It is thrown over everything. It is the garment that takes whatever you are wearing underneath, even the oldest sweater and the simplest trousers, and makes it look intentional.

Why does it work? Line, above all. A trench has a belt. And a belt, tied loosely at the waist, gives the body a shape instantly. It also has a collar that frames the face, epaulettes that lift the shoulders, and a long, clean vertical line from the shoulder to the knee or calf. Where a long cardigan hangs, a trench drapes. Where a cardigan droops at the pockets, a trench holds its edges, because it is made of a tightly woven cotton gabardine.

Color matters here, too. The classic trench is a sand or stone color, which is a cousin of beige, but crisper, a little warmer, with a slight sheen in the cotton. If that tone still feels too pale next to your face, look for a trench in navy, in khaki, or in black. All of them work.

How do they wear it? Never buttoned all the way. Open, with the belt tied loosely behind, or knotted at the front rather than buckled. Sleeves pushed up a little. Collar slightly turned up at the back. Over jeans and a striped top. Over a black dress. Over a navy sweater and grey trousers. A silk scarf at the neck in a color that brings life to the face.

This is the layer I reach for on the days when I do not feel elegant. I put it on over whatever I am wearing, I tie the belt, I turn up the collar, and somehow, the woman in the mirror looks ready.

Picture a woman walking along the Seine near the bouquinistes, those green booksellers' boxes along the river wall. A stone-colored trench open and flowing behind her, belt knotted loosely, navy sweater, dark jeans, brown loafers. There is a light drizzle. She does not open an umbrella. She just turns up her collar and keeps walking.

The sixth layer goes back to knitwear, but a very different kind of knit than the cardigan we started with.

The sixth layer is the ivory cable knit.

A cable-knit sweater in ivory or cream, thick, textured, with those twisted rope patterns running vertically down the front and the sleeves.

You might ask, isn't ivory just another kind of beige? Not at all. Ivory is a white with a little warmth in it. It is light, it is luminous, and it reflects light up onto the face like a gentle reflector. Beige absorbs and dulls. Ivory brightens. Hold both under your chin in front of a mirror, and you will see the difference immediately.

And the cables are not just decoration. Those vertical ribs and twists create long lines running up and down the body, which lengthen the figure. The texture also catches light and shadow in a beautiful three-dimensional way, so the sweater looks rich and alive, even in the plainest outfit.

The key is the fit. The sweater should be pullover, not an open cardigan, and it should end at the hip. If it is a little roomy, tuck just the front into your trousers or wear a narrow belt over it. That small gesture creates a waist and stops the volume from overwhelming you.

How do they wear it? With wide grey flannel trousers and loafers. With dark jeans and suede boots. With a camel coat over the top. With a long tweed skirt. Ivory and camel, ivory and grey, ivory and navy, all three are wonderful.

Picture a woman in a little wine bar in Saint-Germain on a winter evening, candlelight on the zinc counter, a glass of red wine in front of her. An ivory cable knit, small gold hoops, silver hair pulled back loosely. The warm light catches the twists in the wool and the shine of her earrings, and she looks like she is glowing from the inside.

Now we come to the seventh and final layer, and it is the most relaxed of them all. It is the one that proves you do not need to be formal to stop looking frumpy.

The seventh layer is the denim shirt worn as an overshirt.

A classic button-down shirt in a mid-blue or washed denim, worn open like a light jacket over a T-shirt, a striped top or a fine sweater. Sleeves rolled to the elbow. Collar relaxed.

Why does it work instead of the cardigan? Because denim is a fabric with character. It has a slight texture, a natural fade, and a cool blue tone that flatters almost every skin and every hair color, especially grey and silver. It is casual, but it is crisp. It has a collar that frames the face and a straight hem that ends at the hip. It never sags the way a knit does.

There is also something wonderfully modern and youthful about a denim shirt, without being young. It looks like you might be on your way to the garden, or to an art class, or to meet a grandchild for lunch. It suggests a life full of doing.

How do they wear it? Open over a white T-shirt with ivory trousers. Over a black turtleneck with black jeans. Knotted at the waist over a summer dress. Under a navy blazer, with the collar out. With a red silk scarf at the neck for a little drama.

Picture a woman in the Jardin des Plantes on a soft spring morning, walking slowly between the flower beds with a small notebook. A washed denim shirt open over a white T-shirt, ivory wide-leg trousers, white sneakers, a straw hat. She stops to sketch a peony. She looks completely at home in her own life.

Before I sum everything up, let me tell you about a friend of mine. She is seventy-two, a retired piano teacher, and for as long as I had known her, she wore a long grey cardigan to every lunch we had. One autumn she came to meet me at a brasserie near the Gare de Lyon, and I almost walked past her table. She was wearing a navy crewneck, a narrow belt over dark trousers, and a stone trench tossed over the back of her chair. Nothing expensive. Nothing new, she told me, except the trench. But she was sitting differently. Her shoulders were back. She was laughing more easily. She said that without the cardigan, she felt as if she had stopped apologizing for taking up space.

I have thought about that phrase many times since. Because I think that is what the long shapeless cardigan often does for us. It lets us disappear a little. And there are days when disappearing feels safe. But most days, we deserve to be seen.

One more practical thought, because you may be wondering how to combine these layers when the weather changes three times in one day, as it does here. The French approach is to build from thin to structured. A fine knit or a striped top close to the body. Then something with an edge, a blazer, a boiled-wool jacket, a denim shirt. Then, if it is cold, the trench or a coat on top. Each layer is thinner and neater than you might expect, so you can take one off at lunch and still look complete. That is the difference between layering and bundling. Bundling hides you. Layering builds you.

And one note on color. You do not need seven colors. Most of the women I watch keep to three: one dark, like navy or charcoal, one warm neutral, like camel, and one light, like ivory or white. Then they add a single accent, a red scarf, a green jacket, a pair of burgundy shoes. With that small palette, every layer goes with every other layer, and getting dressed in the morning takes two minutes.

So let us bring all seven layers together.

Instead of the long shapeless beige cardigan, French women over sixty reach for a navy cashmere crewneck, a camel blazer, a breton striped top layered under something structured, a cropped boiled-wool jacket, a trench coat thrown over everything, an ivory cable knit, and a denim shirt worn open like a jacket.

And here is what I want you to notice about all of them. Every one of these layers has an edge. A shoulder, a hem, a collar, a belt, a clear length. Not one of them simply hangs. That is the principle. A layer should hold its own shape, so that you do not have to hold it up.

Every one of them also brings light near the face, whether through the depth of navy, the warmth of camel, the brightness of ivory, the play of stripes, or the cool blue of denim. Not one of them blurs you into the background.

And every one of them shows, at least a little, where your body actually is. They give you a waist, or a shoulder, or a long line. They tell the truth about your shape instead of hiding it in a fog of knitwear.

What about my beige cardigan, the one hanging on the kitchen chair? I kept it. I wear it on Sunday evenings at home, with a book and a cup of tea. And that is exactly where it belongs. It is a comfort garment, and comfort at home is a beautiful thing. But when I walk out the door, I leave it on the chair, and I reach for the trench.

If you are going to try one of these layers first, I would start with the navy crewneck or the trench. They are the easiest to find, the easiest to wear, and they make the biggest difference in the shortest time.
