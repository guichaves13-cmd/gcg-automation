Last spring I was sitting at a small round table outside a café near the Luxembourg Gardens, and a woman walked past me who must have been close to seventy. Silver hair cut just below the ear, a navy coat, a plain leather bag on her shoulder. But it was not the coat I noticed. It was her shoes. A pair of soft brown leather loafers, a little worn at the toe, polished just enough to catch the afternoon light. She walked over those uneven paving stones like she owned them. And I thought, that is it. That is the whole secret, right there, at the bottom of her legs.

Because here is something I have learned after many years of living around Paris and watching women who seem to get more elegant every year: your shoes tell people how you move through the world before your face ever does. They set the rhythm of your walk. They decide the line of your leg. They decide whether your outfit looks finished or looks like you gave up halfway down.

So today I want to walk you through seven shoes that the French women I admire over sixty simply do not wear. And for every single one, I will tell you exactly what they wear instead, so you never feel like I took something away without giving you something better. Stay with me until the sixth shoe, because that one surprises almost everyone. It is a shoe many of us were told was the very definition of elegance, and it is the one I see quietly disappearing from the most stylish feet in the city.

Before we start, let me say one thing clearly. This is not about rules that you must obey. This is about what I have observed, and about principles of light, line, proportion and fabric that you can use or ignore as you like. You are a grown woman. You choose. I am simply sharing what I see.

Let us begin with the first shoe.

The first shoe they avoid is the chunky orthopedic sneaker. You know the one. Thick foam sole, often two or three colors, mesh panels, a big padded tongue, sometimes a reflective strip on the heel. It is built for a treadmill, and somehow it has ended up at lunch, at the museum, at the theater.

Now, I understand why we buy them. Our feet change. Our knees complain. Comfort becomes not a luxury but a necessity. I would never tell you to suffer. But look at what that shoe does visually. The sole is so thick and so wide that it makes the foot look heavy and swollen. It adds visual weight at the very lowest point of your body, and that weight drags the eye downward. The mesh and the plastic panels catch the light in a flat, dull way, so the whole shoe reads as athletic equipment rather than clothing. And when you pair it with a nice pair of trousers or a midi skirt, the shoe and the outfit are speaking two different languages. The result is not sporty. It is tired.

So what do French women wear instead? A white leather minimalist sneaker. Clean, low profile, a slim rubber sole, smooth leather upper with no logos shouting at anyone. The difference is enormous. Smooth leather reflects light softly, the way skin does, so it looks polished rather than plastic. The slim sole keeps your foot looking like a foot. And white, a clean white, lifts the whole outfit and makes it feel fresh.

Here is how they style it. Straight-leg trousers in navy or grey that stop right at the ankle bone, so you see a little sliver of skin or a fine sock. Or a midi skirt in a soft wool with a fitted sweater. Or simple dark jeans with a crisp shirt. And the sneakers are kept clean. That is the key. A scuffed white sneaker says you stopped caring. A clean one says you chose this. Many women I know wipe theirs down after every walk, the way their mothers polished their good shoes.

Picture this for a moment. A woman crossing the Pont des Arts in the early morning, the river grey and silver beneath her. She is wearing wide navy trousers, a striped top under a camel coat, and those white leather sneakers flashing with every step. She looks like she is on her way somewhere interesting. That is the feeling you want.

But comfort is only the first part of the story. The second shoe is one so many women love that I almost hesitated to include it.

The second shoe they avoid is the square-toe clog. The heavy wooden or thick rubber sole, the wide boxy front, often in patent or a stiff leather. Sometimes it comes with a strap across the back, sometimes not.

I know these feel sturdy. I know they feel practical. But look at the line. A square toe cuts the foot off abruptly. It ends the leg in a hard horizontal line, like a full stop at the end of a sentence that was not finished yet. The wide front makes the foot look wider and shorter. And the heavy sole lifts you in a clunky way, so your walk becomes a clomp instead of a stride. You actually hear these shoes before you see them, and that sound becomes part of how people perceive you.

What do French women wear instead? A low block-heel Mary Jane. A rounded or slightly almond toe, a single strap across the instep, and a heel of maybe three or four centimeters, wide and stable. In black leather, in burgundy, in a deep brown, sometimes in a soft suede.

The block heel gives you all the stability you were looking for in that clog. You can stand at a gallery opening for two hours. You can walk to the market and back. But the rounded toe lets the line of your leg continue gracefully, and that little strap across the foot adds a touch of charm, almost a schoolgirl wink, which on a woman of sixty or seventy becomes something witty and confident rather than childish.

How do they style it? With cropped trousers, so the strap shows. With a pleated midi skirt and opaque tights in winter, black tights with black Mary Janes making one long unbroken line. With cuffed straight jeans and a fine merino sweater. A woman I know wears hers in deep red with a grey flannel suit, and it is the red shoe everyone remembers.

Imagine her on rue du Bac, stepping out of a little bookshop with a paper bag under her arm. Grey wool skirt swinging just below the knee, black tights, those burgundy Mary Janes clicking softly on the stone. It is a small detail, and it changes everything.

Now, the third shoe is the one that quietly ruins summer outfits, and you may be wearing it right now if it is warm where you are.

The third shoe they avoid is the flip-flop. The thin rubber sole, the plastic thong between the toes, the slapping sound with every step.

On the beach, at the pool, of course. Nobody is judging what you wear on wet sand. But in the city, on a summer afternoon, at a terrace, in a shop? The flip-flop does something unkind. It offers no structure at all, so the foot looks flat and bare and unprotected. Your toes grip to keep it on, which changes your walk into a shuffle. The rubber reads as cheap and disposable no matter what it cost. And because the shoe has no shape of its own, it does nothing to elevate the outfit above it. A beautiful linen dress with flip-flops looks like you are on your way to take out the trash.

So what do they wear instead? Raffia espadrilles. The classic rope sole, a canvas or raffia upper, sometimes a flat, sometimes a gentle wedge, sometimes with ribbons that tie at the ankle. In natural, in navy, in black, in a soft striped canvas.

The texture is the magic here. Raffia and jute have a matte, woven surface that catches the light in a warm, natural way. They speak of summer, of the south, of long lunches, without looking careless. They hold your foot, so you walk properly. And they pair with everything that summer brings out: white linen trousers, a striped dress, a denim skirt, a simple cotton shirt knotted at the waist.

If you have trouble with the back of the shoe slipping, look for a closed-heel version, or one with a slingback strap. And if you need more cushioning, a thin gel insole tucked inside is invisible and makes all the difference.

Picture a woman on the Île Saint-Louis in July, an ice cream in one hand, a straw basket over her arm. Wide white linen trousers rolled once at the ankle, a navy cotton shirt, natural espadrilles. She does not look like she is on holiday. She looks like holiday lives inside her.

Before we go to the fourth shoe, I want to ask you something. Tell me in the comments: which shoe do you reach for most when you leave the house in the morning? Is it comfort first, or style first? I read your comments and I love seeing how different our lives and our feet are. And if you are watching from a city with cobblestones like mine, tell me which city. I am always curious.

Now, the fourth shoe. This one is sneaky, because it looks almost right. It is actually a cousin of one of my favorite shoes.

The fourth shoe they avoid is the worn-out ballet flat with no support. The paper-thin sole, the elastic that has gone loose, the leather stretched and creased across the top, the little bow hanging on by a thread.

Ballet flats are deeply French, I know. We all have the picture in our minds. But the picture is of a young dancer's foot in a soft new shoe. When a ballet flat gets old and flat and thin, it collapses. The leather wrinkles in deep lines across the front, which is not flattering on a shoe and certainly not near anything we want to look youthful. The sole is so thin that you feel every stone, so you walk carefully, slightly hunched, and that posture adds years in a second. And the shape spreads and splays until the shoe looks more like a slipper you forgot to take off.

So what do they wear instead? A soft leather ballerina with a cushioned insole. Not a paper-thin flat, but a ballet shoe that has been built for a grown woman: a slightly thicker sole, a small heel of maybe one centimeter, a padded footbed, and leather that holds its shape. Sometimes with a pointed or almond toe, which elongates the leg beautifully, sometimes with a rounded one. In nude close to your skin tone, in navy, in a soft metallic like pewter or bronze.

And they replace them. That is the habit I notice. A French woman over sixty would rather have two good pairs of ballerinas that she rotates and resoles than five tired ones crammed in a closet. When the shape goes, the shoe goes.

The nude tone is particularly clever. When the shoe is close to the color of your skin, the leg simply continues into the foot without interruption, and your leg looks longer. It is an old trick and it still works.

How do they style it? With cigarette trousers in black or camel. With a full midi skirt. With cropped jeans and a trench in spring. A pointed-toe ballerina in pewter with a navy dress is one of the most elegant and quiet combinations I know.

Imagine a woman in the Tuileries, sitting on one of those green metal chairs by the fountain, her legs crossed at the ankle. Camel cigarette trousers, a white shirt, and a pair of pointed nude ballerinas, perfectly shaped. The sun is on her face, and her feet look as composed as she does.

So far we have talked about shoes that are too heavy, too clunky or too flat. The fifth shoe is the opposite problem, and it might surprise you.

The fifth shoe they avoid is the stiletto pump. The very high, very thin heel, the steep arch, the narrow toe box.

Now, I am not saying a woman over sixty cannot wear a heel. Absolutely not. But watch a woman walk in a high stiletto when her body is not enjoying it. The knees bend slightly to compensate. The hips push back. The steps get small and cautious. The face tightens with concentration. And all of that, the bent knee, the tight face, the careful walk, reads instantly as discomfort, and discomfort looks older than anything else. Elegance is ease. The moment you look like you are surviving your shoes, the elegance is gone.

There is also the question of proportion. A very high, very thin heel can look a little like trying too hard, like holding on to an image from a different decade of life rather than living fully in this one.

So what do they wear instead? Kitten heels. A heel of three to five centimeters, slim but not needle thin, often slightly curved. A pointed or almond toe. In black suede, in patent, in a slingback, in a soft animal print, in red.

The kitten heel gives you that lift, that little arch in the foot that makes the leg look elegant and the posture straighten. But you can walk in it. Really walk, with long, confident steps, across a whole evening. And because it is low, it looks intentional and refined rather than desperate. There is a certain wit to a kitten heel. It says I know exactly what I am doing, and I do not need to prove anything.

How do they style it? With a slim pencil skirt and a cashmere sweater. With tailored trousers that break just over the heel. With a simple black dress for dinner. A black slingback kitten heel with straight dark jeans and a white shirt is one of the most Parisian outfits I can think of, and it works at sixty, seventy, and beyond.

Picture a woman stepping out of a taxi in front of the opera house on a winter evening. A long black coat, a silk scarf, and as her foot touches the pavement, you see it: a black suede kitten heel with a pointed toe. No wobble. No hesitation. She walks straight up the steps.

Now we come to the sixth shoe, the one I mentioned at the very beginning. It is the one that surprises people. But first, let me remind you of the loafers on the woman at the café near the Luxembourg, because we are going to come back to them very soon.

The sixth shoe they avoid is the foam slide. The thick molded slipper sandal, one wide band across the top, sometimes in a pastel, sometimes with a brand name stamped on it, sometimes puffy like a pillow.

Why is this surprising? Because so many people think of it as the new elegant comfort shoe. They are everywhere. I see them in airports, in supermarkets, in restaurants. And they are comfortable, I will not pretend otherwise.

But here is what they do. The foam has a matte, rubbery surface that absorbs light and looks flat, almost like a toy. The thick sole and the puffy band make the foot look large and blocky. And because there is nothing at the heel, you drag your feet, so your walk becomes a shuffle. That shuffle, that sound of a slide scraping the floor, is one of the fastest ways to look like you have stepped out of the house without meaning to.

So what do they wear instead? This is where we come back to our café. They wear leather loafers. Classic, soft, low leather loafers with a slim sole and a structured heel. In brown, in black, in burgundy, in a rich tan, in suede for the warmer months.

The loafer is the shoe I see most often on the women I find most elegant in this city. It is a slip-on shoe, so it has all the ease of a slide. You step in and go. But it holds your heel, so you walk properly, with a heel-toe stride that lifts your posture. The leather has that soft sheen that looks better the more you wear it. And the loafer is unisex in spirit, a little borrowed from the gentlemen, which gives a woman a relaxed, intelligent confidence. It says I have places to be and I intend to enjoy getting there.

How do they style them? With absolutely everything. With straight jeans and a navy sweater. With grey flannel trousers and a white shirt. With a midi skirt and bare legs in late spring. With a fine sock in a contrasting color, burgundy loafers with grey socks, for example, which is very charming. Many women I know keep a brown pair and a black pair and wear nothing else most of the year.

Picture a woman on rue de Rivoli under the arcades on a damp October morning. A camel coat, dark straight jeans with a clean hem at the ankle, a grey cashmere scarf, and polished brown loafers stepping lightly over the wet stone. She is not dressed up. She is simply dressed. And she looks wonderful.

And now the seventh and final shoe. This one is all about shine.

The seventh shoe they avoid is the shiny patent mule. The backless shoe with the high-gloss patent finish, often in a bright color or in a metallic, often with a chunky heel or an embellishment on top.

Two problems happen at once here. The first is the backless shape. Like the slide, a mule with no heel strap makes you grip with your toes and slap with your heel, and after a certain age, many of us also have dry or rough heels that we would prefer not to show to the whole world from behind. The second problem is the shine. High-gloss patent reflects light very harshly. It throws a hard little spotlight onto the feet, and that attention is not always kind. In a bright color, it starts to look costume-like, almost theatrical, and it competes with your face for attention. You want people to look at your eyes, not at a glare coming from the floor.

So what do they wear instead? Suede ankle boots. A soft suede boot that ends just at or just above the ankle bone, with a modest block heel or a flat sole, a slim shape, and an almond toe. In taupe, in chocolate brown, in black, in a deep olive.

Suede is the opposite of patent. Where patent reflects light, suede absorbs and softens it. It has a velvety, matte texture that looks rich and warm and quietly expensive without saying a word. The ankle boot covers the heel and the ankle, which gives support and a clean, continuous line. And because the boot is closed, it gives you structure and warmth from autumn right through the cool days of spring.

The proportion trick is to make sure the top of the boot meets your trousers or your skin in a pleasing way. With cropped trousers, the boot should either tuck just under the hem, or leave only a very small gap. With a midi skirt, the boot and opaque tights in the same color create a long, slimming line. With straight jeans, the hem should graze the top of the boot rather than bunch over it.

How do they style it? A taupe suede boot with ivory wide trousers and a camel coat is one of the softest, most flattering winter looks for silver or white hair. A chocolate suede boot with dark denim and a navy blazer is effortless. A black suede boot with a black midi dress and a long grey scarf is pure Paris.

Picture a woman in the Marais on a cold November afternoon, a paper cone of roasted chestnuts in her gloved hand. Charcoal wool coat, a burgundy scarf, dark jeans, and taupe suede ankle boots. She stops in front of a window display, tilts her head, and moves on. Nobody would ever guess her age from her silhouette, and that is not because she is hiding anything. It is because everything about her is in proportion.

So let us gather all seven together, because I want you to leave with a clear picture.

Instead of the chunky orthopedic sneaker, a clean white leather minimalist sneaker.

Instead of the square-toe clog, a low block-heel Mary Jane.

Instead of the flip-flop, raffia espadrilles.

Instead of the worn-out ballet flat, a soft leather ballerina with a cushioned insole, replaced when it loses its shape.

Instead of the stiletto pump, a kitten heel.

Instead of the foam slide, a leather loafer.

And instead of the shiny patent mule, a suede ankle boot.

Do you notice what all the replacements have in common? They are all made of materials that look better with time, leather, suede, raffia. They all hold your foot so you can walk with your head up. And they all have a slim, gentle line that lets your leg continue gracefully into your foot instead of ending in a block.

That is really the principle under all of this. Not youth. Not trends. Just ease, line and good materials. A French woman over sixty is not trying to look thirty. She is trying to look like herself on a very good day, every day. And her shoes carry her there.

One more small thought before I go. You do not need to throw out everything in your closet tomorrow. Start with one pair. If you only change one thing after this video, let it be the shoe you wear most often. If that is a foam slide, try a loafer. If that is a chunky sneaker, try a white leather one. Wear it for a week. Notice how you walk. Notice how you stand. I think you will be surprised by how differently you feel, and by how differently people look at you.

When I think back to that woman at the café near the Luxembourg Gardens, I do not remember her face very clearly anymore. I remember her walk. The easy rhythm of her steps. The soft gleam of those brown loafers. That is what stays with people, and that is what can stay with them about you too.

Now I would love to hear from you. Tell me in the comments which of these seven shoes you are ready to say goodbye to, and which replacement you are going to try first. Is it the loafer? The kitten heel? The suede boot? And if you have a pair of shoes you have loved for years and years, tell me about them. I find those stories so beautiful.

If you enjoyed this, stay with me, because next time we are going to talk about something many of us wear every single day and almost never question. Until then, walk tall, walk easy, and take good care of your feet. À bientôt.
