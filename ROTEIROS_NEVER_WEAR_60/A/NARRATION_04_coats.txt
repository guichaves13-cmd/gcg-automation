A few winters ago, at the end of a concert in a small church on the Île de la Cité, I stood in the line for the cloakroom and watched the coats come back across the counter one by one. A black puffer that reached to the knees. A grey fleece with little balls of lint all over it. And then the attendant lifted a long camel coat off a hanger, and a woman of perhaps seventy-five stepped forward, slipped her arms into it, wrapped it closed, tied the belt, and walked out into the snow. I swear the whole line turned to watch her go.

That was the moment I understood something about winter in Paris. In the cold months, your coat is your outfit. It is the only thing most people will see of you from November to March. You can wear the most beautiful sweater in the world underneath, but on the street, at the market, on the bus, the coat is you.

So today I want to show you six coats that the French women I admire over sixty do not wear, and for each one, exactly what they choose instead, with the color, the fabric and how they style it. And stay with me until the fifth coat, because the replacement is a shape many women have never even considered, and it is the most forgiving coat I know for a body that has changed over the years.

Let me say what I always say. These are observations and principles, not commandments. If you live somewhere with deep snow and twenty degrees below zero, you need what keeps you warm, and that comes first. But for city life, for errands, for lunch with a friend, for walking along a river on a grey afternoon, let us talk about what really works.

The first coat they avoid is the puffer that reaches to the knee. The long quilted down coat, with horizontal channels stitched all the way from the shoulders to the shins, often in black, often with a big hood.

I understand the appeal completely. It is warm, it is light, it is easy to throw in the washing machine. But look at what it does visually. Those horizontal quilted bands run around the whole body like rings on a tree, and every one of them widens you. The puffiness adds volume everywhere, so the body becomes one big rounded shape with no shoulder, no waist and no line. And the fabric, that shiny nylon, catches light in hard little highlights along every ridge, which emphasizes the volume even more. At the knee, it cuts the leg at its widest point. The overall effect is a sleeping bag with legs. Practical, yes. Flattering, no.

What do French women wear instead? A camel wrap coat. A long wool coat in a warm camel color, with no buttons, or with hidden ones, that wraps across the body and ties with a belt at the waist. It usually ends somewhere between the knee and the mid-calf.

Why does it work so beautifully? Fabric first. A good wool or wool-blend coat has a matte, soft surface that absorbs light gently and falls in clean vertical folds. There are no ridges, no shine, no puffiness. Line next. The wrap creates a long diagonal from the shoulder to the hip, which is one of the most slimming lines there is. The belt marks the waist, wherever your waist happens to be these days, and gives you a shape. And the color. Camel is warm, like the inside of a croissant, and it reflects warmth onto the face. Silver hair against camel looks rich and deliberate.

How do they wear it? Wrapped and belted with a simple knot, never with the buckle pulled tight. Over a black turtleneck and black trousers for a long, unbroken dark line underneath. Over jeans and a white shirt with loafers. With a grey or burgundy scarf. The belt loose or tight depending on the mood. Collar turned up when the wind blows.

If you are worried about warmth, a thin down vest or a fine merino layer underneath gives you the warmth of the puffer without any of the bulk showing.

Picture a woman on rue de Rivoli under the stone arcades, on a sharp, bright December morning. A long camel wrap coat tied at the waist, a burgundy scarf, black trousers and black leather boots. She is carrying a little bunch of white tulips wrapped in brown paper. The arcade shadows stripe across her as she walks. She looks warm, and she looks magnificent.

The second coat they avoid is the shapeless fleece. The zip-up polar fleece jacket, often in grey, navy or a muted purple, sometimes with a contrasting zip and pockets.

The fleece is the cardigan of outerwear. It is easy, it is comfortable, it is always there. But the fabric is the problem. Fleece is a synthetic pile that pills quickly, attracts every hair and every crumb, and loses its loft after a few washes. It has a dull, slightly fuzzy surface that absorbs light without any richness, so it always looks a little tired. And the shape is boxy, with elastic at the hem and cuffs that gathers and puffs. It makes the torso look wide and the hips look wider.

What do French women wear instead? A navy peacoat. The classic double-breasted wool coat, ending at the hip or the top of the thigh, with a wide collar, two rows of buttons, and a straight, structured shape.

Why does it work? Structure. A peacoat is cut from a thick, dense wool, so it holds its shape like a piece of architecture. It has a shoulder. It has a collar that frames the face beautifully, especially when you turn it up. The double row of buttons creates two vertical lines down the front of the body, which lengthens the torso. And it ends at the hip, so your legs keep their full length.

Navy, as I often say, is the gentlest dark color for a mature face. It is deep without being harsh, and it makes silver, white and grey hair look luminous.

How do they wear it? With a striped top and jeans, which is the classic sailor look, and timeless. Over a grey turtleneck with grey flannel trousers. With a red scarf for color. With white sneakers on a weekend, with loafers in the week. Buttoned up on a windy day, open over a sweater on a mild one.

Picture a woman at the Canal Saint-Martin on a Sunday morning, leaning on the green iron railing of a footbridge, looking down at the water. A navy peacoat, collar up, a striped top showing at the neck, dark jeans, white sneakers. A seagull lands on the railing beside her. She laughs.

Now, the third coat is one many of us bought years ago for a very good reason. And we kept it long after the reason disappeared.

The third coat they avoid is the faded parka. The long cotton or nylon parka in khaki or olive, often with a fake fur hood, lots of pockets, a drawstring at the waist, and a color that has faded with years of washing and sunlight.

A parka in good condition can be a nice, practical coat. The issue is the fading. When a khaki or olive fabric fades, it turns a greyish, yellowish, uncertain color that does no favors to a mature complexion. It drains the face. The cotton softens and crumples, so the coat loses its shape and starts to look slept in. The fur on the hood mats and clumps. And all the pockets, flaps and drawstrings add bulk exactly where we do not want it.

What do French women wear instead? A belted trench coat with a warm liner. The classic double-breasted trench in sand, stone or navy, but in a version with a removable wool or quilted liner that buttons inside for the cold months.

Why does it work? Because the trench keeps every quality of the parka, its practicality, its rain protection, its casual spirit, and adds line and polish. The cotton gabardine is tightly woven, so it holds a crisp shape and has a subtle sheen. The belt shapes the waist. The collar frames the face. And because the liner is hidden inside, you stay warm without looking bulky.

How do they wear it? Belt knotted at the front, never buckled. Collar turned up at the back. Sleeves pushed up a little. Over a cable sweater and wide trousers. Over a black dress and tights. With a silk scarf knotted loosely at the neck, in a color that brings light to the face: a soft blue, a coral, a cream.

Picture a woman walking along the quai beside the bouquinistes on a damp afternoon in late autumn, the green bookseller stalls closed against the rain. A stone trench, belted, collar up, a navy and white silk scarf at her neck, dark jeans, brown leather boots. She is not hurrying. She stops to look at the river, the grey water moving fast.

Before we go to the fourth coat, tell me something in the comments. What is the coat you have worn the longest in your life? The one you just could not let go of? I have a feeling many of you have a coat with a whole story behind it, a first winter in a new city, a gift from someone you loved. I would love to read them.

The fourth coat they avoid is the boxy duffle coat. The thick wool coat with wooden toggles and rope loops, a big hood, and a wide, square, straight shape from the shoulders to the hem.

There is a certain charm to a duffle coat. It feels like school, like a child's winter. And that is part of the problem. On a woman over sixty, it can look a little juvenile, or a little like a costume. But the bigger issue is the shape. A duffle coat is cut very wide and very square. It hangs from the shoulders straight down, with no waist, so it turns the body into a rectangle. The toggles add little bumps all down the front. And the heavy hood bunches up behind the neck and shortens it, pushing the head forward.

What do French women wear instead? A houndstooth car coat. A coat that ends at the upper thigh or just above the knee, single-breasted, with a small collar, in a classic houndstooth wool, that little broken check pattern in black and white or brown and cream.

Why does it work? Pattern first. Houndstooth is small and graphic, and from a distance it reads as a soft, textured grey or brown. Up close, it has energy and wit. It is a pattern with real history in fine tailoring, so it looks quietly cultivated. And because it is a small repeating pattern, it disguises the shape of the body underneath rather than outlining it. Line next. A car coat is cut straight but slim, with a defined shoulder and a length that shows the legs. It is neat and compact, the opposite of the bulky duffle.

How do they wear it? With black cigarette trousers and black ankle boots, so the coat becomes the whole story. With a black turtleneck. With a red or burgundy scarf, which looks wonderful against black and white houndstooth. With a small leather bag across the body. Some women wear it with a matching beret in black, which is a little playful and very Parisian.

Picture a woman stepping out of a bakery on rue des Martyrs, a baguette under her arm. A black and white houndstooth car coat, black trousers, black boots, a deep red scarf, her short white hair just visible under a black beret. She tears off the end of the baguette and eats it as she walks. Of course she does.

Now we come to the fifth coat, the one I promised at the beginning. The replacement is the most forgiving coat I know for a body that has changed with time. But first, the coat it replaces.

The fifth coat they avoid is the shiny quilted vest. The sleeveless puffer gilet, in glossy nylon, worn over a sweater or a fleece.

The vest is meant to give warmth to the core while leaving the arms free. That is sensible. But visually, a shiny quilted vest does something very unkind. It adds puffy volume around the torso, exactly the zone where many of us have gained a little softness, and it leaves the arms bare by comparison, so the body looks top-heavy and the arms look thin. The glossy fabric reflects light along every quilted ridge, which emphasizes the bulk. And it cuts off at the hip in a stiff horizontal line. The silhouette becomes a barrel on two sticks.

What do French women wear instead? A charcoal cocoon coat. A wool coat shaped like a soft egg, rounded at the shoulders, gently fuller through the body, and tapering slightly toward the hem, which falls around the knee. Often with dropped shoulders, a simple collar or no collar, and hidden buttons.

Now, you might think, a fuller coat, isn't that the opposite of what we want? Here is why it works. The cocoon shape creates its own beautiful outline that has nothing to do with your body underneath. It does not cling, it does not reveal, it does not mark the waist or the hips. It simply floats around you in a soft, sculptural curve, and that curve reads as intentional design, not as volume. Because it tapers at the hem, the eye sees the narrowing at the knee and then your leg, which looks slim by comparison. It is the most forgiving coat for a body that has changed, because it never asks the body to be anything. It just drapes.

The charcoal is important. A deep charcoal grey is softer than black and richer than mid-grey. It makes the coat look like a single sculpted shape. And it is beautiful with silver hair.

How do they wear it? Over slim trousers and ankle boots, so the narrow leg balances the rounded coat. Over a midi skirt and tights. With a bright scarf, a mustard, a red, a cobalt blue, tucked inside the collar. With a bold gold chain over a black sweater underneath. Sleeves sometimes pushed up to show a cuff bracelet.

Picture a woman walking slowly through the Jardin du Palais-Royal on a misty January morning, the bare trees in perfect rows. A charcoal cocoon coat, slim black trousers, black suede boots, a cobalt blue scarf at the neck. Her silver hair is cut in a sharp bob. She looks like a piece of sculpture that decided to go for a walk.

The sixth and final coat they avoid is the too-short bomber jacket. The cropped zip-up jacket in shiny satin or nylon, with ribbed cuffs, a ribbed waistband and a ribbed collar, ending above the waist.

The bomber had its moment, and on a certain kind of young woman it can look fun. But on a mature body, the short length and the elastic ribbed hem create a puff of fabric that sits above the waist and then cinches tightly, which makes the stomach and hips look bigger by comparison. The satin shine is unforgiving. And because it is so short, it leaves the lower back and hips cold, which is simply not practical in a Parisian winter. It looks borrowed from a teenager's wardrobe.

What do French women wear instead? A long wool coat in ivory. Yes, ivory. A straight or slightly relaxed long coat in winter white, falling to the mid-calf.

I know what many of you are thinking. Ivory? In winter? In the city? It will get dirty! And you are right that it needs a little care. But let me tell you why the women I admire love it. Light. An ivory coat is a giant reflector. It bounces soft light up onto the face all day long, on the darkest, greyest afternoon. It makes the skin look brighter, the eyes clearer, the hair whiter and more luminous. Next to a sea of black and grey coats on the Métro, a woman in an ivory coat looks like she has her own personal sunshine.

Line. A long coat creates one long vertical column from shoulder to calf, which is the most elongating line you can wear. And proportion. Unlike the short bomber, it covers the hips and the lower back, keeps you warm, and gives the body a calm, unbroken silhouette.

How do they wear it? Over ivory or cream trousers and a cream sweater, which is called a tonal look and is incredibly chic. Over camel and brown. Over grey. With brown suede boots. With a camel scarf. And yes, they take care of it. They brush it after wearing it. They avoid eating croissants on the bus. They keep a lint roller by the door.

Picture a woman crossing the Place de la Concorde on a grey February afternoon, the obelisk behind her, the sky low and heavy. She is wearing a long ivory wool coat, cream trousers, a camel cashmere scarf, chocolate suede ankle boots. Everything around her is grey and rushing. She is the only bright thing in the frame, and she walks as though she knows it.

So let us bring all six together.

Instead of the puffer to the knee, a camel wrap coat.

Instead of the shapeless fleece, a navy peacoat.

Instead of the faded parka, a belted trench with a warm liner.

Instead of the boxy duffle coat, a houndstooth car coat.

Instead of the shiny quilted vest, a charcoal cocoon coat.

And instead of the too-short bomber, a long wool coat in ivory.

What do all six replacements have in common? First, they are made of wool or tightly woven cotton, fabrics that have a soft, matte surface and that age beautifully. Not one of them is shiny nylon. Second, they all have a clear, intentional line: a wrap, a belt, two rows of buttons, a cocoon curve, a long column. Not one of them is a puffy, shapeless blob. And third, they all bring something good to the face, whether it is the warmth of camel, the depth of navy and charcoal, or the brightness of ivory.

That is the principle. In winter, your coat is your silhouette. It should be the most beautiful shape you wear, because it is the one the world sees most.

Now, a practical word, because I know a good coat is a significant purchase for many of us. You do not need all six. Most of the elegant women I know in Paris own two or three coats, and they wear them for many, many years. If I had to choose only one, I would choose either the camel wrap or the navy peacoat, because they go with almost everything. If you can have a second, add a trench with a liner for the in-between seasons. And if you are feeling brave, the ivory coat will change the way you feel in winter more than anything else on this list.

When you shop, check three things. Put the coat on and lift your arms, as if you were reaching for a shelf. If it pulls at the back or the shoulders, it is too small. Then look at it from the side in the mirror. It should fall straight, not cling to the stomach or flare out at the back. And finally, look at the fabric up close. A good wool should feel dense and spring back when you squeeze it.

And take care of the coat you choose. Brush it. Hang it on a proper wide hanger, not a wire one. Replace a missing button. Get it cleaned once a season. A coat that is well cared for looks elegant for a decade. A coat that is neglected looks tired in a year.

When I think back to that woman at the cloakroom of the church on the Île de la Cité, I remember how she wrapped that camel coat around herself and tied the belt in one easy movement, as if she had done it ten thousand times. And she probably had. That is what a good coat becomes. A gesture. A habit. A part of you.

Now I want to hear from you. Tell me in the comments: which of these six coats are you going to look for this winter? The camel wrap? The cocoon? Or are you brave enough for the ivory? And tell me where you live, because I know winter in Paris is very different from winter in Minnesota or in Melbourne, and I love learning how you all keep warm and keep elegant at the same time.
