There is a tiny cobbler's shop on a side street not far from my apartment, with a hand-painted sign and a window full of shoe trees and little tins of polish. One rainy Thursday I was waiting there for a heel repair, and a woman of about seventy came in with a pair of brown loafers wrapped in a cloth. She unfolded them on the counter like a precious thing. The leather was creased softly across the top, deep and glossy like a chestnut. The heels were worn down. And she said to the cobbler, these are my twenty-year shoes, can you give them another ten? He turned them over, looked at the sole, ran his thumb along the stitching, and said, with these, Madame, easily.

There was no logo on those loafers. No gold hardware, no famous name stamped in the lining. And yet they were, without a doubt, the most beautiful shoes I saw that whole month.

So many women ask me about loafers. Should I buy the famous designer pair, the one with the gold horsebit that everyone recognizes? Is it worth it? And I want to be very fair here. I have nothing against any great fashion house, including the one in the title of this video. They have made iconic shoes, and if you own a pair and you love them, wear them with joy. But what I hear again and again from women who spend their lives dressing other people, and what I see on the most elegant feet in Paris, is this: the beauty of a loafer is not in the logo. It is in a handful of quiet details that you can look for in any shoe, from any maker, at many different price points.

So today, I am going to walk you through seven features to look for in a loafer, the things that make a pair look elegant, feel wonderful and last for years. And stay with me until the sixth feature, because it is the one almost nobody talks about, and it is the secret to wearing loafers comfortably all summer long, even in the heat of July.

Why do loafers matter so much after sixty? Because they may be the most perfect shoe for a woman of our age. They slip on, so there are no laces to struggle with. They have a low heel, so they are kind to the knees and the back. They hold the heel, so you walk with a proper stride, not a shuffle. And they go with absolutely everything, jeans, trousers, skirts, dresses. The loafer is the shoe I see most often on women over sixty in this city who look effortless.

But a bad loafer, a stiff, shiny, badly shaped one, can look clumsy and feel terrible. So let us talk about what to look for, one detail at a time. As always, these are observations and principles, not rules, and no single maker owns any of them.

The first feature is full-grain leather.

Leather comes in many qualities. Full-grain leather is the top layer of the hide, kept whole, with its natural surface intact. You can often see tiny pores and a slight natural variation in the surface. It has not been sanded down or covered with a heavy plastic coating to hide imperfections.

Why does it matter? Light. Full-grain leather has a natural, soft luster. It does not shine like plastic. It glows, the way good wood glows. And with time and polish, that glow deepens. Lower-quality leathers are often coated with a thick, shiny finish that reflects light harshly and looks stiff, like a doll's shoe. That surface cracks and peels instead of aging.

Fabric, or in this case, material. Full-grain leather breathes. It molds to your foot. It develops creases across the top that become soft and personal, like the lines on a well-loved face. It is the reason that woman's loafers at the cobbler had that deep chestnut glow after twenty years.

How do you check? Look closely at the surface in daylight. Do you see a natural, slightly irregular grain? Press it gently with your thumb. Does it wrinkle softly, like skin, rather than crease sharply like plastic? Smell it. Real leather has a warm, rich scent.

How do they wear a good leather loafer? In a deep brown, with straight dark jeans, a white shirt and a camel coat. In black, with grey flannel trousers and a navy sweater. In burgundy, with a grey skirt and grey tights. Polished once a week with a good cream, and they look better every year.

Picture a woman walking along the Rue du Faubourg Saint-Honoré on a crisp autumn morning, past the elegant shop windows. Deep brown full-grain leather loafers, softly creased across the top, glowing in the low sun. Dark jeans to the ankle, a navy coat. She does not glance at the windows. She does not need to.

The second feature is a stitched sole.

Turn a loafer over. Look at the edge where the upper meets the sole. On a well-made shoe, you will often see a line of stitching running all around the sole, holding the layers together. On a cheaper shoe, the sole is simply glued on.

Why does it matter? Longevity. A stitched sole can be removed and replaced by a cobbler. When the sole wears thin, as it will, the cobbler unpicks the stitching, attaches a fresh sole, and stitches it back. The shoe gets a new life. A glued sole is much harder to repair well, and when it starts to separate, the shoe is often finished.

And there is beauty. A stitched sole has a clean, crafted edge that gives the shoe a finished, quietly expensive look. It sits flat and neat. The edge of the sole can be polished too, a little detail that catches the light and frames the foot.

That is exactly what the cobbler was looking at when he ran his thumb along the edge of that woman's loafers. The stitching told him the shoe could be renewed.

How do you check? Look for a visible line of stitches along the sole, either on the top edge, around the welt, or on the bottom of the sole itself. Ask in the shop whether the shoe can be resoled. A good shop will know the answer.

How do they wear it? With everything, of course. But a stitched leather sole has a slim, refined profile that is especially lovely with cropped trousers, where the whole shoe shows. A black loafer with a stitched sole and black cigarette pants is the very definition of polished.

Picture a woman stepping off a city bus on the Boulevard Saint-Michel, one foot on the pavement. Black cigarette pants ending at the ankle, and a black loafer with a slim, polished sole edge catching the light, the line of stitching just visible. A small detail. But it makes the foot look neat and light.

But a beautiful sole is nothing if the shoe hurts the back of your heel. The third feature is the one that makes the difference between a shoe you wear once and a shoe you wear every day.

The third feature is a soft heel counter.

The heel counter is the part of the shoe that wraps around the back of the heel. In some shoes, it is reinforced with a very stiff, hard material that holds its shape like a shell. In others, it is softer, more flexible, while still supporting the heel.

Why does it matter after sixty? Comfort, above all. Many of us have more sensitive skin on our heels now, and perhaps a bony spot or two. A very stiff heel counter can rub, blister, and dig in, especially in the first weeks. We end up with bandages on our heels and the shoe in the back of the closet. A soft, well-shaped heel counter cups the heel gently, holds it in place so you walk properly, but moves with you instead of against you.

And there is the walk. When the heel is held comfortably, you step forward with confidence, heel then toe, standing straight. When the heel hurts, you start to walk carefully, a little crooked, and that shows in your whole posture.

How do you check? Squeeze the back of the shoe between your thumb and fingers. It should give a little, but spring back. It should not collapse completely, and it should not be rock hard. Try the shoe on and walk around the shop for several minutes, not just a few steps. Pay attention to the back of your heel.

How do they wear it? A soft-heeled loafer is the one you can walk in all day, from the market in the morning to a museum in the afternoon to dinner. In a soft suede, it is wonderful with wide-leg trousers and a cable knit for a long Sunday walk.

Picture a woman strolling slowly through the long galleries of the Louvre on a quiet weekday afternoon, stopping in front of a painting for a long time. Wide grey flannel trousers, an ivory cable sweater, and soft tan suede loafers. She has been walking for three hours. She looks as fresh as when she arrived.

The fourth feature is an almond toe.

The shape of the front of the shoe matters enormously. A very round toe can look a little childish and blunt. A very square toe looks heavy and abrupt. A very pointed toe can pinch. The almond toe sits in between, gently tapered, slightly elongated, rounded at the very tip, like the shape of an almond.

Why does it matter? Line. An almond toe extends the line of the foot gracefully. It makes the foot look a little longer and slimmer, which in turn makes the whole leg look longer. It is softer and more feminine than a square toe, more modern than a perfectly round one, and kinder to the toes than a sharp point. Proportion. The slight taper balances beautifully with a wide-leg trouser or a full skirt, because the slim front of the shoe peeks out from under all that fabric and anchors the silhouette.

And comfort. A good almond toe still leaves room for the toes to lie flat. Many women over sixty have wider forefeet or a sensitive joint at the big toe. Look for an almond toe that tapers gently, not a narrow one that squeezes.

How do they wear it? An almond-toe loafer in black with wide black trousers, so just the tip of the shoe shows beneath the hem. In burgundy with a navy midi skirt. In cognac with white jeans in spring.

Picture a woman sitting on one of the green chairs around the octagonal pond in the Tuileries, her legs crossed at the ankle. Wide black trousers, a black turtleneck, a silk scarf, and from under the hem, the gently tapered almond toe of a black leather loafer. Children push little wooden sailboats across the water in front of her. It is the most elegant ankle in the garden.

The fifth feature is the penny strap.

The penny loafer is the classic shape. A band of leather sewn across the top of the shoe, with a little slit cut into it. It got its name, the story goes, from the old habit of tucking a coin into that slit. It is the simplest decorative detail there is.

Why does it matter? Because this little strap is what gives the loafer its quiet character, without any logo or hardware. It creates a gentle horizontal line across the top of the foot, which gives the shoe a structured, tailored look. It frames the instep. It catches a little light and shadow along its edges. And because it is made of leather, it ages with the shoe, softening and darkening together.

There is also something deeply timeless about the penny loafer. It has been worn for generations by students, professors, artists, by women and by men. It looks right in every decade. A shoe with a big piece of metal hardware is often a statement of its season. A penny strap simply belongs to all of them.

How do you choose? Look for a strap that is neatly stitched, with clean edges, sitting smoothly across the shoe without puckering. The slit should be cleanly cut. Some loafers have a little decorative stitching around the vamp, the top front part of the shoe, which is lovely. Some are completely plain, which is also lovely.

How do they wear it? Penny loafers in dark brown with grey socks and cropped straight jeans, which has an easy, intellectual charm. In black with a pleated skirt and black tights. In oxblood, that deep red-brown, with a navy blazer and ivory trousers.

Picture a woman sitting on the steps of the Panthéon on a sunny autumn afternoon, a paper bag of pastries beside her, her face tipped toward the sun. Cropped navy trousers, a fine grey sock, and oxblood penny loafers, the little strap catching the light. Students rush past her up the steps. She is in no hurry at all.

Now the sixth feature, the one I promised you. The one almost nobody talks about, and the secret to wearing loafers all summer long.

The sixth feature is unlined suede for summer.

Most loafers are lined inside with a second layer of leather, which adds structure and durability. That is wonderful for autumn and winter. But in summer, a fully lined leather loafer can feel hot and heavy, and many women put their loafers away in June and reach for flip-flops or slides. You already know how I feel about those.

The solution is an unlined suede loafer. Suede is leather with a soft, brushed, velvety surface. An unlined suede loafer is made from a single layer, with no extra lining inside. It is light, flexible, almost like wearing a soft glove on the foot.

Why does it matter? Comfort, first. An unlined suede loafer is incredibly soft from the very first wear. There is almost no breaking in. It bends with your foot. It breathes. Your feet stay cooler. You can wear it barefoot, or with a very thin invisible sock, on the hottest days. Light. Suede absorbs light and gives a soft, matte, velvety look that feels relaxed and summery, quite different from the polished gleam of a winter leather loafer. And color. Unlined suede loafers often come in lovely light summer shades: sand, tobacco, a soft olive, navy, even pale blue or dusty pink.

How do they wear it? With white linen trousers and a navy striped top. With a soft cotton dress and bare legs. With mid-blue cropped jeans and a white shirt. With a straw bag and sunglasses.

A small tip: spray new suede with a protective spray before wearing it, and keep a soft suede brush near the door. A quick brush after each walk keeps them looking fresh all season.

Picture a woman walking along the Promenade Plantée, the old elevated railway turned into a garden, on a hot July morning, roses climbing over the railings. White linen wide trousers, a navy striped top, a straw basket, and a pair of sand-colored unlined suede loafers on her bare feet. She walks for an hour beneath the trees. Her feet are cool, and she looks like summer.

And now, the seventh and final feature. It is the smallest one of all, and the one that makes your loafers last for years.

The seventh feature is a rubber heel tap.

The heel tap, sometimes called the heel lift, is the small piece at the very bottom of the heel, the part that touches the ground first with every step. On many fine leather-soled shoes, that piece is made of leather, which looks beautiful but wears down quickly and can be slippery on wet pavement. On a well-made, practical loafer, the heel tap is often made of a thin piece of rubber, sometimes inset into the leather.

Why does it matter? Safety, first. Paris pavements are often wet. Marble entrances, polished floors, the stone steps of the Métro, all of them can be slippery. A rubber heel tap grips. It gives you a steady, confident step. After sixty, that is not a small thing. Confidence in your step changes how you walk, how you carry your head, how you feel about going out at all.

Longevity. The heel tap is the part of the shoe that wears out first. A rubber tap wears more slowly and is quick and simple for a cobbler to replace. You keep the rest of the shoe beautiful and simply change that small piece every season or two. That was part of what the woman at the cobbler's was doing with her twenty-year loafers.

Sound. A rubber heel tap makes a softer, quieter step than a bare leather or hard plastic heel. You do not clack. You glide.

How do you check? Turn the shoe over and look at the heel. If it is all leather and you plan to walk a great deal, ask your cobbler to add a thin rubber tap, and perhaps a thin rubber layer on the front of the sole too. It is invisible from above and makes the shoe practical for everyday city life.

How do they wear them? Every day. To the market, to the Métro, to lunch, on rainy days and dry ones. Rubber-tapped brown loafers with a trench and dark jeans on a wet October morning are perhaps the most practical elegant outfit in Paris.

Picture a woman coming down the stone steps into the Métro station at Saint-Germain-des-Prés on a rainy morning, closing her umbrella with one hand, her other hand lightly on the rail. A belted trench, dark jeans, polished brown loafers. Her step is quick and sure on the wet stone. She does not even look down.

So let us bring all seven features together, so you have a little checklist the next time you shop.

Full-grain leather, with a natural surface that glows and ages beautifully.

A stitched sole, so a cobbler can renew it.

A soft heel counter, so the back of your heel is supported but never rubbed.

An almond toe, which lengthens the foot and leaves room for the toes.

A penny strap, the quiet, timeless detail that needs no logo.

Unlined suede for summer, soft, light and cool.

And a rubber heel tap, for a safe, quiet, confident step and an easy repair.

Do you see? Not one of these features is a logo. Not one is a piece of hardware. Every one of them is about material, construction, comfort and line. These are the things that make a loafer beautiful, and they are the same things the most famous makers rely on underneath their famous details. You can find them in shoes from small workshops, from classic makers, from shops in many countries, at many different prices. You simply need to know what to look for.

That is the principle. Pay for the leather, the stitching and the shape. Not for the name.

And when you find a pair that has all seven, take care of them. Use shoe trees, wooden ones if you can, to keep the shape and absorb moisture. Let them rest a day between wearings. Polish the leather once a week with a good cream. Brush the suede. And take them to a cobbler before the heel or the sole wears all the way through. A good loafer, cared for, can easily be your companion for many, many years. Like the woman at the cobbler's shop, you may find yourself one day unwrapping your own twenty-year shoes on the counter and asking for ten more.

If you are buying your first pair, I would choose a dark brown or black full-grain leather penny loafer with an almond toe and a stitched sole. It will go with nearly everything you own. Then, for summer, add an unlined suede pair in sand or navy. With those two, you are dressed for almost every day of the year.

When I think back to that rainy Thursday at the cobbler, the moment that stays with me is not the shoes themselves. It is the way she held them, with so much affection. And the way the cobbler turned them over and knew, just by looking, that they would last. That is what quality really means. Not what people see when they look at your feet, but what you feel, every day, for years, when you put them on.
