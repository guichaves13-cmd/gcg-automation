The wind on the Pont Neuf can be wicked in March. One afternoon, I was halfway across the bridge when a gust lifted a square of silk right off the neck of the woman walking in front of me. It flew up, a flash of navy and coral against the grey sky, and landed on the stone parapet just a few steps ahead. I picked it up and handed it back to her. She must have been in her late seventies. She thanked me, shook it out once, folded it into a triangle, rolled it, and knotted it loosely at her throat, all in about four seconds, without a mirror, without even looking down. Then she smiled and said, it has been trying to escape for forty years.

That little scene has stayed with me, because it captures something so essential about how French women wear scarves. The scarf is not an accessory you think about. It is a gesture. It is as natural as buttoning a coat. And because it sits right at the neck, directly beneath the face, it may be the single most powerful piece of clothing a woman over sixty can wear. The right scarf lights you up like a lamp. The wrong one can make you look tired, heavy or simply lost.

So today, I am going to share five scarves that the French women I admire over sixty never wear, and for each one, the scarf they wear instead, with the fabric, the color, and exactly how they tie it. And stay with me until the fourth one, because I am going to show you a knot I learned from watching women on the Métro, and it takes about three seconds and works on almost any outfit. It is the knot I use every day now.

Before we start, let me say why the scarf matters so much after sixty. Our necks change. The skin softens, lines appear, and many of us start to feel a little self-conscious about that part of the body. A scarf can soften that area, frame the jaw, and add color and light right where the face needs it most. It is like having a little reflector under the chin. But only if the scarf itself has the right fabric, the right size and the right way of falling.

As always, these are observations and principles, not rules carved in stone. Let us begin.

The first scarf they avoid is the polyester printed scarf. The thin, slippery square or rectangle in a synthetic fabric, often with a busy, bright print, sometimes with a little shine, sometimes bought at an airport or a tourist shop.

Let me explain what goes wrong. Fabric first. Polyester does not behave like silk. It is stiff in a slippery way, so it does not knot softly. It either slides undone or sits in a hard little lump. It does not drape against the neck, it sticks out from it. Light next. Polyester has a flat, plasticky shine, a hard sheen that sits on the surface rather than glowing from within. Right under the chin, that shine looks cheap and can even emphasize lines on the neck. And the prints. Very busy, very bright synthetic prints often fight with the face. The eye goes to the scarf, gets confused, and never quite arrives at your eyes.

And there is the comfort. Polyester does not breathe. In summer it makes you warm, in winter it makes you cold, and it can create static in fine hair.

What do French women wear instead? A silk carré, tied loose. The carré is the classic square silk scarf, usually about ninety centimeters on each side, in a pure silk twill. Silk twill has a fine diagonal weave, a soft weight, and a luster that seems to glow rather than shine.

Why does it work? Silk is a living fabric. It warms to the body, it breathes, it moves with every turn of the head. Its natural luster reflects soft light upward onto the face, which brightens the complexion. And because it is so supple, it knots softly and falls in gentle folds, so it frames the neck rather than choking it.

The key word is loose. French women do not tie a silk scarf tight. They fold it into a triangle, roll it loosely from the long edge into a soft band, wrap it once around the neck, and tie a small, loose knot to the side, letting the ends fall. Or they simply drape it in a triangle with the point in front and the ends crossed behind and brought forward again. The scarf should look like it might come undone, as the woman on the Pont Neuf found out.

What prints and colors? A print that has one or two dominant colors that flatter you. Navy and white. Coral and cream. Soft blue and grey. A pattern with a bit of space in it, not wall-to-wall chaos. And hold it up to your face before you buy it. If your eyes look brighter, it is yours.

How do they wear it? With a navy peacoat, knotted to the side. With a trench, the collar up and the scarf inside it. With a simple white T-shirt and jeans, the carré knotted at the throat like a little flourish. Tied on the handle of a bag on days when you want just a touch of color.

Picture a woman at a café on the Place de la Contrescarpe on a bright April morning, a crème in front of her. A stone trench, a silk carré in coral and navy knotted loosely to one side of her neck, her white hair cut short. The square is full of light and bicycles. Every time she laughs, the silk moves.

The second scarf they avoid is the giant blanket scarf. The enormous rectangle of thick, chunky fabric, often plaid, often fringed, wrapped around and around the neck and shoulders until it reaches the ears.

The blanket scarf seems so cozy, so practical. And it is warm. But think about what it does to the silhouette. Proportion first. A huge scarf piled around the neck and shoulders adds an enormous amount of volume to the top of the body. The head seems to sink into it, the neck disappears completely, and the shoulders look broad and heavy. It shortens the whole figure. Line next. All those wraps and folds create a thick horizontal mass right where the face meets the body, which cuts you off. And when the ends hang down in front, they cover the waist and flatten the whole shape of the torso.

There is also the color issue. Many blanket scarves are in big plaids of brown, grey and beige, and those muddy colors, piled up right under the face, can make the skin look sallow.

What do French women wear instead? A long cashmere scarf in grey. A long, narrow scarf, maybe thirty to forty centimeters wide and nearly two meters long, in a fine, soft cashmere or a cashmere and wool blend, in a heathered grey, from a soft dove grey to a deep charcoal.

Why does it work? Fabric. Fine cashmere is incredibly warm for its weight. A thin layer of it keeps you warmer than a pile of thick acrylic. And because it is fine, it does not bulk up. It drapes in soft, fluid folds. Line. A long, narrow scarf creates vertical lines down the front of the body, which elongates the whole figure, the opposite of the blanket scarf's heavy horizontal mass. And color. Grey is the most forgiving neutral next to a mature face, especially with silver or white hair. A heathered grey has little flecks of light and dark in it, which gives depth and softness.

How do they wear it? Wrapped once around the neck, with both long ends hanging in front, down to the hips. Or folded in half, placed around the neck, with the two ends pulled through the loop, which is called the Parisian knot or the European loop, a very classic tie. Or simply thrown over one shoulder, one end in front, one behind. With a camel coat, a navy peacoat, a black coat. With a white shirt and jeans on a cool spring day.

Picture a woman walking briskly along the Boulevard Haussmann in January, past the big department store windows decorated for the sales. A long charcoal coat, a dove grey cashmere scarf wrapped once with the ends floating behind her in the wind, black leather gloves, a small bag on her shoulder. Her cheeks are pink with cold. The scarf moves like smoke behind her.

The third scarf they avoid is the infinity knit loop. The circular, closed scarf, usually in a thick knit, sometimes chunky, that is worn twisted around the neck in a double loop.

The infinity scarf was invented to be easy. You throw it on, it stays put. But that is exactly the problem. Because it is a closed circle, it can only be worn one way, in a thick doubled ring sitting tight around the neck, like a collar. Line. It creates a heavy horizontal band right around the throat, which shortens the neck and pushes up against the jaw. If you have any softness under the chin, the loop pushes it up and outward, and makes it more visible, not less. Fabric. Most infinity scarves are in a chunky knit acrylic, which pills, loses its shape, and stretches into a limp tube after a few months.

What do French women wear instead? A cotton-linen gauze scarf. A soft, lightweight rectangle of woven cotton and linen, or a cotton and silk gauze, very airy, almost transparent, often slightly crinkled, with a frayed or rolled edge.

Why does it work? Fabric, above all. Gauze is light and airy. It drapes and floats rather than sitting heavily. It creates soft volume without bulk, like a cloud around the neck. You can wrap it loosely two or three times, and it still looks light, because air passes through it. Light. The open weave lets light through and diffuses it softly around the face, which is the most flattering lighting there is. It is like a photographer's soft-box under your chin.

And because it is an open rectangle, not a closed loop, you can wear it many ways: loosely wrapped, draped, knotted, thrown over the shoulders like a little shawl on a summer evening.

What colors? Soft, natural tones work beautifully: chalk white, ecru, sky blue, sage green, a dusty pink, a soft indigo. And in summer, a gauze scarf in white with a navy striped top is pure Brittany.

How do they wear it? Wrapped loosely twice around the neck, with the ends falling unevenly. With a white T-shirt and a denim jacket. With a linen shirt and wide trousers. Over a little black dress on a cool summer night. It is the scarf for the in-between seasons, spring and early autumn, when the air is soft but the evenings are cool.

Picture a woman at an outdoor table at a café along the Canal Saint-Martin on a warm evening in June, a glass of rosé in front of her. A navy linen shirt, a sky blue cotton gauze scarf looped loosely around her neck, the ends falling over her shoulder, her hair loose and silver. The evening light turns the gauze almost transparent. She looks relaxed, cool, and completely at peace.

Before we go to the fourth scarf, and the knot I promised you, I want to ask. Tell me in the comments: do you wear scarves often? Or are you one of the many women who own a drawer full of them and never quite know how to tie them? Be honest. I think that drawer is one of the most common secrets in the world. And if you have a favorite scarf with a story, tell me that too.

The fourth scarf they avoid is the tiny neckerchief tied tight. The little square of fabric, folded into a narrow band and knotted snugly at the front or side of the neck, almost like a choker.

There is a certain charm to a small neckerchief. It recalls old films and airline uniforms from long ago. But tied tightly, it does several unkind things after sixty. Line. A tight band at the neck draws a hard horizontal line exactly where the neck is most delicate, and squeezes the skin around it, which can emphasize lines and folds. Proportion. A very small scarf on a mature face can look a little too small, a little too cute, out of scale with a woman's presence. And the tightness itself. A scarf tied snugly looks stiff and uniform-like, and you can feel it all day. It makes you hold your head stiffly.

What do French women wear instead? A bandana-knot silk. A silk square, not tiny but not huge, often a medium size of around sixty-five to seventy centimeters, folded into a triangle and tied the bandana way: the point in front, the two ends wrapped behind the neck and brought back to the front, then knotted loosely to the side or behind. The triangle falls softly over the collarbones like a little bib of color.

And here is the knot I promised you, the one I watched women tie on the Métro. You take a silk square and fold it in half diagonally to make a triangle. Hold the triangle with the point facing down. Place it on your chest with the point toward your waist. Bring the two ends around behind your neck, cross them, and bring them back to the front. Then tie a small, loose knot just off to one side, under the jaw, and let the ends hang. Finally, give the triangle a little tug so it sits softly over the collarbones. That is it. Three seconds. It looks effortless, it covers the neck gently without squeezing, and it gives a little splash of color right under the face.

Why does it work? The triangle shape creates a soft downward V on the chest, which elongates the neck visually and draws the eye toward the face. The loose knot to the side is relaxed and asymmetrical, which looks modern. And silk, as we said, brings that glow.

How do they wear it? With a crisp white shirt open at the collar, the bandana knot sitting inside the neckline. With a navy crewneck sweater, the triangle showing just above it. With a denim jacket and jeans. With a simple black dress.

Picture a woman waiting on a Métro platform at Saint-Germain-des-Prés, her hand resting on the strap of a leather tote. A white shirt, a silk square in red and ivory tied in that soft bandana knot to the side, a navy coat over her arm. The train rushes in, the wind from the tunnel lifts the ends of her scarf, and she steps inside without looking back.

And now, the fifth and final scarf. This one is all about the sparkle.

The fifth scarf they avoid is the glittery pashmina. The large wrap or shawl, often in a synthetic or a blend, woven with metallic thread or covered in sequins or little sparkles, usually saved for a party or a wedding.

The glittery pashmina is meant to be festive. But visually, metallic threads and sequins next to the face reflect light in hundreds of little harsh points. Close to the skin, that sharp, scattered sparkle can be unflattering, catching every line and every shadow. The fabric is often stiff and scratchy, so it does not drape well. And it looks a little like a costume, like something you put on to say I am dressed up, rather than something that is truly part of you. Elegance in the evening is usually quieter than we think.

What do French women wear instead? A wool scarf in a color pair. A fine wool or wool-and-silk scarf woven in two colors, sometimes in two solid halves, sometimes in a reversible weave with one color on each side, sometimes with a deep border in a second color. Navy and burgundy. Grey and camel. Forest green and mustard. Black and ivory. Plum and pink.

Why does it work? Color. Two colors, carefully paired, create depth and interest without any glitter. As you wrap and drape the scarf, both colors appear in different proportions, so the scarf looks like it was made just for this outfit. Fabric. Fine wool has a soft, matte texture and a beautiful drape. It warms the face and catches the light gently. And for the evening, a wool and silk blend adds a soft sheen without any sparkle at all. Sophisticated, not sequined.

How do they wear it? Draped over the shoulders like a shawl for the evening, over a black dress, one side showing burgundy, the other navy. Wrapped once around the neck with a camel coat. Knotted loosely with a grey sweater and jeans. Choose the color pair so that one color repeats something in your outfit, and the other adds a little surprise.

Picture a woman stepping out of the Théâtre du Châtelet after a performance, the lights of the square shining on the wet pavement. A black coat open over a black dress, and around her shoulders a fine wool scarf in deep plum with a soft pink border. She pauses to say goodnight to a friend, and the scarf slips a little off one shoulder, showing the pink side. No sparkle at all. And she is the most elegant woman on the steps.

So let us bring the five together.

Instead of the polyester printed scarf, a silk carré tied loose.

Instead of the giant blanket scarf, a long cashmere scarf in grey.

Instead of the infinity knit loop, a cotton-linen gauze scarf.

Instead of the tiny neckerchief tied tight, a bandana-knot silk.

And instead of the glittery pashmina, a wool scarf in a color pair.

Look at what all five replacements share. Every one of them is made of a natural fabric, silk, cashmere, cotton, linen, wool. These are fabrics that breathe, that drape, that glow softly rather than shine harshly. Every one of them is tied or worn loosely, never tight, never piled up, never choking. And every one of them brings light and color toward the face in a gentle, flattering way.

That is the principle. A scarf after sixty should frame the face, not hide the neck. It should float, not bundle. And it should be chosen by holding it up to your face in good daylight and asking one simple question: do my eyes look brighter?

A word about care, too, because natural fabrics reward a little attention. Silk squares can be hand washed in cool water with a gentle soap, rolled in a towel, and ironed on a low setting while still slightly damp. Cashmere should be folded, never hung, so it does not stretch, and rested for a day between wearings. Gauze can go in a cool gentle wash and dry crumpled, because that soft crinkle is part of its charm. A scarf that is cared for lasts for decades, and many of the most beautiful ones I see on the streets here are clearly older than the women's grandchildren.

Here is a little exercise for you this week. Open that scarf drawer, the one we all have. Take every scarf out. Hold each one under your chin, in front of a window, in natural light. Put them in two piles. The ones that light up your face, and the ones that do not. Be honest. Keep the first pile, and then practice. Practice the loose knot. Practice the long drape. Practice the bandana triangle. Do it every morning for a week, and by Friday, you will tie it in four seconds without a mirror, just like the woman on the Pont Neuf.

And remember that a scarf is one of the easiest ways to bring a new color into your wardrobe without buying anything else. If you are unsure about wearing red, or cobalt, or emerald, try it in a scarf first. Close to your face, a small amount of color can do a great deal of work.

When I think of that woman on the bridge, her silk scarf flying off in the March wind, I think of how relaxed she was about it. She did not panic. She did not tie it tighter afterward. She just laughed and let it be loose again. That is the spirit of the French scarf. It is not about control. It is about grace.

Now, tell me in the comments: which of these five scarves are you going to try? The silk carré? The long grey cashmere? Or the bandana knot? And if you try the knot this week, come back and tell me how it went. I would truly love to know.
