The fitting room curtain was a faded red velvet, and behind it a woman's voice said, to no one in particular, I think I am too old for these. I was waiting my turn in a little shop in the Haut Marais, holding a pair of jeans over my arm, and when the curtain opened, out stepped a woman of perhaps sixty-eight in a pair of straight, dark blue jeans that stopped just above her ankle bone. She looked at herself in the long mirror. She tucked in the front of her white silk blouse. She turned sideways. And she said, quietly, no, actually, I am not.

I have thought about that sentence many times. Because so many women I know have asked themselves the same question. Can I still wear jeans after sixty? And the answer, if you look at the women walking around Paris, is clearly yes. Almost every elegant woman I know over sixty owns at least one pair she adores. But there is a big difference between jeans that make you look relaxed and modern and jeans that make you look frumpy and tired. And that difference almost always comes down to a handful of small mistakes.

So today, I am going to walk you through seven jeans mistakes that French women over sixty avoid, and for each one, exactly what they do instead. And stay until the very end, because the seventh mistake is about color, and the solution is something many women think is forbidden after a certain date on the calendar. It is my favorite trick of all, and it makes winter so much brighter.

As always, these are observations and style principles, not laws. You know your body, your life and your comfort better than anyone. Take what helps you.

The first mistake is the mom jean worn too high and too baggy. The very high waist that comes up to the ribs, the wide, loose hips, the tapered, slouchy leg, often in a light, stiff denim.

Mom jeans were made fashionable again by young women who wear them with cropped tops and tiny waists. But on a mature body, the effect changes completely. The very high rise lengthens the torso below the bust and puts a lot of fabric over the stomach, so it can actually make the tummy look rounder. The baggy hips add volume where many of us already have some. And the tapered ankle makes the whole leg look like an upside-down triangle, wide at the top and narrow at the bottom, which makes the hips look even wider. It is not relaxed. It is lumpy.

What do French women wear instead? A straight-leg jean in a raw or rinsed denim that ends at the ankle. Straight means the leg falls in one even line from the hip to the hem, neither tight nor wide. Raw or rinsed denim is denim in a deep, even indigo, without fading, with a slightly firm, smooth surface.

Why does it work? Line. The straight leg creates two clean vertical columns, and the eye reads the whole leg as long and balanced. The hips look narrower because the leg does not taper away beneath them. Fabric. A firm, deep indigo denim holds its shape, smooths over the body rather than clinging to every curve, and its dark, even color is quietly slimming. And proportion. Ending at the ankle shows a little bit of skin or a fine sock, which lightens the whole silhouette.

The rise should be mid to high, coming to the natural waist or just below, not up to the ribs. Enough to sit comfortably over the stomach and keep a blouse tucked in, but not so high it swallows you.

How do they wear it? With a crisp white shirt, sleeves rolled. With a navy cashmere crewneck. With a camel coat and loafers. With a striped top and white sneakers.

Picture a woman walking out of the Luxembourg Gardens through the tall iron gates on a fresh spring morning. Straight dark indigo jeans to the ankle, a white shirt with the cuffs folded back, brown loafers, a small straw basket on her arm. Simple, clean, completely modern.

The second mistake is distressed jeans with holes. Ripped knees, shredded thighs, frayed patches, little tears made deliberately to look old.

Here is the truth. A hole in a pair of jeans on a twenty-year-old says rebellion. A hole in a pair of jeans on a woman of sixty-five can say, unfortunately, I have not noticed. It reads as worn out rather than worn in. And visually, the holes break up the line of the leg, so the eye jumps from tear to tear instead of moving smoothly from hip to ankle. If the holes are at the knee, they draw attention right to the knee, which, for many of us, is not the part of the leg we most want people to look at.

What do French women wear instead? A clean, mid-blue jean. A classic, slightly faded but perfectly intact denim, the color of a summer sky just after rain. No holes, no rips, no artificial fraying.

Why mid-blue? Light. A mid-blue denim is soft and luminous. It has a cool, gentle tone that flatters silver, grey and white hair beautifully. It is more relaxed than dark indigo, so it is perfect for weekends, for summer, for the countryside, but because the fabric is intact, it still looks polished. It says easy, not careless.

How do they wear it? With a navy and white striped top and espadrilles. With an ivory cable sweater and suede boots. With a crisp white T-shirt and a camel blazer. Mid-blue with white and navy is a perfect French palette.

Picture a woman on a bench in the Place Dauphine, that little triangle of chestnut trees on the Île de la Cité. Mid-blue straight jeans, a navy striped top, a white cotton jacket over her shoulders, white leather sneakers. She is reading a paperback with her sunglasses pushed up into her hair. Children are playing pétanque nearby.

The third mistake is the stretchy jegging. The jean that is really a legging: very thin, very stretchy, skin tight, often with fake pockets and an elastic waist.

I know why we love them. They are so comfortable. You can sit, bend, sleep in them. But visually, a jegging is merciless. The fabric is so thin and stretchy that it clings to every curve, every dimple, every line of underwear, and outlines the knees and the thighs exactly. It has a slightly shiny surface that catches light on every bump. And it rarely looks like denim at all. It looks like what it is, a legging pretending, and so the whole outfit looks pretending too.

What do French women wear instead? A straight jean in a denim with just a little stretch, worn with a tucked silk blouse. A denim that is mostly cotton, firm enough to hold its shape and smooth over the body, with just a touch of give for comfort.

And here, the silk blouse is part of the answer, because it is the styling that transforms a jean from frumpy to elegant. A blouse in a soft, fluid silk or a silky crêpe, in ivory, in navy, in a soft print, tucked loosely into the front of the jean, with the back left out or loosely bloused.

Why does it work? Fabric contrast. The fluid, slightly shiny silk against the matte, structured denim is a beautiful tension. One is soft, one is strong. The silk catches light and moves with you, the denim holds you. Together they look effortless. And the tuck, even just the front tuck, shows the waistline and gives the body a shape, without having to wear anything tight.

How do they wear it? Ivory silk blouse, dark straight jeans, kitten heels, small gold hoops for dinner. Navy silk blouse, mid-blue jeans, loafers for lunch. A printed silk blouse in a soft floral with a camel coat over the top.

Picture a woman sitting at a table in a restaurant in Saint-Germain, a white tablecloth, a glass of white wine. She is wearing an ivory silk blouse, the sleeves softly gathered at the wrist, tucked into straight dark jeans. A thin gold chain, small pearl studs. The candle flickers across the silk. She looks dressed for dinner, and she is wearing jeans.

Before we go to the fourth mistake, tell me in the comments: how many pairs of jeans do you own? And how many do you actually wear? I suspect many of us have a drawer full of jeans and one single pair we wear all the time. I would love to know if I am right.

The fourth mistake is the wrong hem that pools at the ankle. Jeans that are too long, bunching in folds over the shoe, sometimes dragging on the ground, sometimes stacked in thick creases at the ankle.

This one is so common and so easy to fix. When a hem pools, all that extra fabric collects at the very bottom of the leg, where it creates a heavy, wide, crumpled area. That bunching shortens the leg visually, because the eye stops where the folds begin. It also hides the shoe and the ankle, which are among the most delicate parts of the body at any age. The whole figure looks like it is sinking into its own trousers.

What do French women wear instead? A slightly cropped jean. A jean that ends just above the ankle bone, or that is shortened by a single neat cuff of a few centimeters, so the hem sits cleanly and shows a slim sliver of ankle or a fine sock.

Why does it work? Proportion. Showing the narrowest part of the leg, the ankle, makes the whole leg look slimmer and lighter. A clean hem creates a crisp horizontal line that ends the outfit with intention. And the shoe becomes visible, so a nice loafer or a ballet flat or a suede boot can add its own elegance.

Take your jeans to a tailor. It is one of the best small investments you can make in your wardrobe. Or, for a relaxed look, roll the hem once in a narrow, neat cuff. Not a wide, sloppy roll. One narrow fold.

How do they wear them? Cropped straight jeans with loafers and a fine contrasting sock. Cropped jeans with Mary Janes. Cropped jeans with ballet flats in summer. Cropped jeans with suede ankle boots that just meet the hem in winter.

Picture a woman walking up the steps of the Sacré-Cœur on a sunny afternoon, the whole city spread out behind her. Cropped mid-blue jeans with a neat narrow cuff, a navy sweater, a red silk scarf, and black loafers showing a bright little flash of a striped sock. Every step up the stone stairs, you see that clean line of ankle and shoe.

The fifth mistake is the acid wash. The bleached, mottled, marbled denim with white streaks and splotches, a very specific look from a very specific decade.

Acid wash makes an outfit feel dated instantly. It is one of those finishes that belongs to an era, and wearing it today can make you look as though you have been in the same jeans since then, which is exactly the impression we want to avoid. But beyond the nostalgia, the visual problem is light. The irregular white streaks scatter light unpredictably across the leg, so the eye sees patches, blotches and uneven areas. It breaks up the line and can make the thighs and hips look larger.

What do French women wear instead? This is the one I promised you at the beginning. White jeans in winter.

Yes, in winter. I know there is an old rule somewhere that says no white after a certain date at the end of summer. The women I admire in Paris ignore that rule completely. And once you see why, you may too.

Why does it work? Because white denim gives you all the brightness that acid wash was trying to have, but clean, even and intentional. On a grey winter day, white jeans are like a light switch. They reflect light upward, they look fresh and crisp, and they make every other color you wear look richer. And in winter, paired with heavy textures, they look especially luxurious and thoughtful.

The secret is to choose a winter white or an ecru, a slightly warmer, creamier white, in a thick, opaque denim so nothing shows through, and to pair them with winter fabrics: wool, cashmere, suede, leather.

How do they wear them? Ecru jeans with an ivory cable sweater and a camel coat, all in creams and caramels. White jeans with a navy peacoat and a grey scarf. White jeans with a black turtleneck, a black coat and black boots, which is very graphic and very chic. White jeans with chocolate suede ankle boots.

Picture a woman at the Christmas market in front of the Hôtel de Ville, holding a paper cup of hot spiced wine in both gloved hands. Ecru jeans, a long camel coat, an ivory cable knit, chocolate suede boots. The little wooden chalets glow with lights behind her. In a crowd of black and grey, she shines.

Now, we have two more mistakes, and the next one is about where the jean sits on your body.

The sixth mistake is the low-rise jean. The waistband that sits low on the hips, well below the belly button, sometimes showing a strip of skin or underwear when you sit down.

Low-rise jeans cut the body in an unflattering place. The waistband sits at the widest part of the hips, where it can create a little roll of softness above it, what some people call a muffin top. It also shortens the legs visually, because the leg seems to start lower. And when you sit or bend, it gaps at the back, which is uncomfortable and not something anyone wants to be worrying about at lunch.

What do French women wear instead? A mid-rise jean worn with a belt. A jean whose waistband sits at or just below the natural waist, comfortably over the stomach, and a simple leather belt threaded through the loops.

Why does the belt matter so much? Line. A belt draws a clean, defined horizontal line at the waist. It gives a shape to the body, even if your waist is not as narrow as it once was. It finishes the outfit, the way a frame finishes a painting. And when a shirt or blouse is tucked in, the belt makes the tuck look neat and deliberate instead of accidental.

Choose a belt in a smooth leather, about two or three centimeters wide, in brown, black or a cognac tan, with a simple buckle, gold or silver, nothing big or logoed. Match it more or less to your shoes for a polished look.

How do they wear it? Mid-rise dark jeans, a white shirt fully tucked, a cognac leather belt and cognac loafers. Mid-blue jeans, a striped top tucked in at the front, a black belt and black ballet flats. Jeans, a fine navy sweater, a brown belt peeking out at the front, and a camel blazer over it all.

Picture a woman standing at the counter of a cheese shop on rue de Seine, pointing at a round of Comté. A white shirt tucked into dark jeans, a slim cognac leather belt with a small gold buckle, cognac loafers. Her silver hair is pulled back in a low knot. The shopkeeper wraps the cheese in paper, and she tucks it into her basket.

And now, the seventh and last mistake. This one is very subtle, and it is about the color and finish of dark denim.

The seventh mistake is dark denim with whiskers and heavy fading. Dark jeans that have been artificially treated with those horizontal faded lines across the hips and the top of the thighs, called whiskers, and lighter patches on the front of the thighs and the seat.

Those whiskers and fades were designed to imitate years of wear. But they do something specific to the body. The faded lines sit right across the hips and the crotch, and the lighter patches sit on the front of the thighs, which are exactly the areas that the eye sees as widest. Lighter areas advance, darker areas recede. So the fading highlights the very areas many of us would prefer to soften. It is like putting a spotlight on the hips.

What do French women wear instead? A clean, even dark or mid denim, with no whiskers, no artificial fading, no contrast stitching that screams, worn with loafers.

The evenness is the secret. When a denim is one even color all over, the leg reads as one smooth shape, from the waist to the ankle. Nothing is highlighted, nothing is emphasized. It looks expensive, even if it is not. And because it is so plain, it becomes a perfect canvas for everything else.

And why the loafers? Because they are the shoe that most beautifully completes a pair of jeans after sixty. A leather loafer with a clean dark jean is the definition of relaxed Parisian elegance. It is polished but not formal, comfortable but not careless. The leather adds a soft gleam at the bottom of the leg, and the low profile shows a bit of ankle.

How do they wear it? Clean dark jeans, burgundy loafers, a grey cashmere sweater, a navy wool coat. Clean mid-blue jeans, brown suede loafers, a white shirt, a straw bag in summer. Clean dark jeans, black loafers, a black turtleneck and a bold gold chain for the evening.

Picture a woman walking along the Rue de Rivoli under the arcades at dusk, the shop windows lighting up one by one. Even, clean dark jeans just to the ankle, burgundy leather loafers, a grey sweater, a long navy coat open and flowing. She has a bouquet of ranunculus in her arm. She is walking home. She looks wonderful, and she looks like herself.

So let us gather all seven together, so you can see the whole picture.

Mistake one, the mom jean too high and too baggy. Instead, a straight-leg raw denim to the ankle.

Mistake two, distressed jeans with holes. Instead, a clean, mid-blue denim.

Mistake three, the stretchy jegging. Instead, a real denim with just a little stretch, with a tucked silk blouse.

Mistake four, the hem that pools at the ankle. Instead, a slightly cropped jean, tailored or with one neat cuff.

Mistake five, the acid wash. Instead, white or ecru jeans, even in winter, with warm, rich textures.

Mistake six, the low-rise jean. Instead, a mid-rise jean with a simple leather belt.

And mistake seven, dark denim with whiskers and heavy fading. Instead, a clean, even denim worn with leather loafers.

What do all these solutions have in common? First, they all respect the line of the leg: straight, even, clean, ending at the ankle. Second, they all avoid tricks. No holes, no bleach, no fake fading, no thin stretchy fabric pretending to be something it is not. Just good, honest denim. And third, they all depend on styling: the tuck, the belt, the hem, the shoe, the blouse. A jean on its own is just a jean. It is what you put around it that makes it elegant.

That is the principle I want you to keep. Jeans after sixty are not about trying to look young. They are about looking at ease. A woman in a well-fitting straight jean, a white shirt and a pair of loafers looks like she has nothing to prove and plenty to do.

If you are going to buy only one pair after this video, I would buy a straight-leg jean in a clean, deep indigo, with a mid to high rise, and take it to a tailor to hem it just above the ankle. That one pair can carry you through almost every season and almost every occasion, from the market to a dinner. And if you are feeling adventurous, add a pair of ecru jeans for the winter. You will be surprised how much joy a pair of white jeans can bring on a grey January morning.

When I think back to that woman in the little shop in the Haut Marais, looking at herself in the mirror and saying, no, actually, I am not too old, I think that is what all of this is really about. It is not about the jeans. It is about deciding that you still get to choose, that you still get to feel good, that nobody else gets to tell you what you are too old for.
