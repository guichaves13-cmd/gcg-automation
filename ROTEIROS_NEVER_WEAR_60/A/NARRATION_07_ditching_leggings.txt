I was folding laundry one Tuesday morning and I held up a pair of black leggings to the window. The knees had gone shiny and thin. The fabric had little grey pills all over the thighs. And when the sunlight came through, I could see straight through them, like looking through a dark curtain. I had worn those leggings to the market, to the pharmacy, to lunch with my sister. And I thought, if I can see through them standing at my window, what has everyone else been seeing at the bakery?

That was the beginning of something for me. And as I started paying attention, I realized I was not alone. Among the women I know here in Paris and the women who write to me, so many are quietly doing the same thing. Not with a big announcement. Not with a dramatic closet clear-out. They are simply reaching for the leggings less and less, and reaching for something else more and more. And when I look at the most elegant women over sixty walking around this city, I almost never see leggings worn as trousers. Under a dress for warmth, perhaps. For a yoga class, of course. But as pants on their own? Almost never.

So today, I want to show you the seven things French women over sixty wear instead of leggings. Seven real alternatives that are every bit as comfortable, I promise you, but that make you look polished, relaxed and entirely yourself. And stay with me for the fifth one, because it is not a pair of trousers at all, and it is the one that changed my winter wardrobe completely.

But first, let us be honest about why we love leggings so much. Because I do not think anyone should feel silly for wearing them.

Leggings are comfortable. They stretch, they move with us, they do not dig in at the waist. When our bodies change, when our weight shifts, when buttons and zips start to feel like enemies, leggings forgive everything. They are easy to wash, easy to pack, easy to pull on in the morning when we are tired. They feel like freedom.

But here is what they do visually, and I want to be gentle and clear.

Fabric. Most leggings are made of a thin, stretchy knit, often cotton and elastane or a synthetic blend. That fabric is designed to cling, and it does. It outlines everything, every curve of the thigh, the shape of the knee, the line of underwear, every little dimple and softness. As the fabric stretches over the body, it also thins out, and in bright light, it can become almost transparent, especially over the seat and the knees.

Light. That stretched fabric often has a slight sheen, and sheen catches light on every curve, so the eye sees highlights wherever the body is fullest. Shine makes things look larger.

Line. Leggings are skin-tight from the waist to the ankle. So unless you are wearing a long tunic, which many of us do, the hips and thighs are completely exposed in silhouette. And when we do wear a long tunic over them to cover up, we create a new problem: a big loose shape on top and two thin lines underneath, which makes the body look top-heavy and the legs look like sticks.

Proportion. The whole silhouette becomes very casual, very athletic. It reads as I am on my way to the gym, or I have just woken up. And when every other part of you is put together, the leggings undo it.

So what do French women wear instead? Here are the seven.

The first alternative is wide-leg trousers.

A pair of trousers that falls straight and wide from the hip, ending at the ankle or just above the shoe, in a fluid fabric like wool crêpe, a soft twill, or a heavy viscose. Often with a high waist and a flat front, sometimes with a single pleat.

Why does it work? Line, above all. A wide leg falls in a long, clean, vertical column from the hip to the floor. It does not reveal the shape of the leg at all. It skims past the hips and the thighs and simply drops. The eye sees one long line, which makes you look taller and slimmer. And the movement. A wide-leg trouser in a fluid fabric swings when you walk. It has life. It makes every step look graceful.

Comfort. And here is the secret that surprised me. Wide-leg trousers are just as comfortable as leggings. Nothing touches your legs. Air moves freely. Nothing pinches or rides up. Many are made with a soft elastic panel at the back of the waist, so you get the ease of a pull-on with the look of tailoring.

How do they wear them? With a fine fitted sweater tucked in at the front, so the waist shows and the wide leg looks deliberate. With a silk blouse and loafers. With a white T-shirt and white sneakers on a weekend. Navy, black, camel, grey flannel, ivory. A pair of camel wide-leg trousers with a navy crewneck is one of the most elegant everyday outfits I know.

Picture a woman walking across the Place Saint-Sulpice past the great stone fountain on a breezy autumn afternoon. Wide camel trousers swinging with every step, a navy cashmere sweater, a small leather bag over her shoulder, brown loafers. The wind catches the fabric and it flows around her legs like a skirt. She looks like she is gliding.

The second alternative is ponte pull-on trousers.

Ponte is a word I think every woman over sixty should know. It is a thick, double-knit fabric, firm and smooth, with a little stretch, almost like a heavy jersey with structure. It is what a legging would be if it grew up and went to a good school.

Ponte pull-on trousers have an elastic or flat stretch waistband, no zip, no buttons, and a slim or straight leg. They look like tailored trousers from the outside, but they feel like leggings on the inside.

Why does it work? Fabric. Unlike thin legging jersey, ponte is dense and opaque. It does not cling, it skims. It holds its shape, so it smooths over the body without outlining every detail. It has a matte surface that absorbs light softly rather than reflecting it. It does not go see-through. It does not bag at the knees. And because it is a knit, it stretches and moves exactly like your leggings did.

Line. A ponte trouser cut in a straight or slim straight leg, ending at the ankle, gives you the clean silhouette of a tailored pant. Many have a sewn crease down the front, which creates a vertical line that lengthens the leg.

How do they wear them? Black ponte trousers with a long fine knit and ballet flats. Navy ponte trousers with a striped top and a trench. Grey ponte trousers with a white shirt and a camel blazer. They are perfect for travel, for long days, for those mornings when you want comfort but you also want to look ready for anything.

Picture a woman at the Gare de Lyon, pulling a small suitcase along the platform toward a train to the south. Black ponte trousers, a long grey cashmere sweater, a trench over her arm, black leather sneakers. She has been up since six and she looks as fresh as if she had just stepped out of her apartment.

But if ponte trousers are the most practical alternative, the third one is the most charming. And it is a shape many women over sixty think they cannot wear.

The third alternative is culottes.

Culottes are wide, cropped trousers that end somewhere between the mid-calf and the ankle, with a wide leg that looks almost like a skirt when you stand still. Often in a soft wool, a crisp cotton, a linen, or a crêpe.

Many women tell me they are afraid of culottes because they think the cropped length will make them look short. But here is how French women make them work. Proportion. The trick is to show a little bit of ankle or leg between the hem and the shoe, and to wear a shoe that continues the line, a pointed flat, a low heel, a slim boot. That small gap of skin, or a tight boot meeting the hem, keeps the leg looking long and slender. And the high waist of most culottes lengthens the leg from the top.

Line. Because culottes are so wide, they swing and flow like a skirt, so they never cling. They give you all the movement and femininity of a skirt with all the practicality of trousers. You can sit on a low wall, climb onto a bus, bend over a flower bed, and never worry.

How do they wear them? In black wool with a black turtleneck and black ankle boots, all one color, which is incredibly elegant and lengthening. In navy linen with a white shirt and espadrilles in summer. In camel with an ivory cable knit. Always with the top tucked in or cropped at the waist, so the waistline shows.

Picture a woman cycling slowly along a quiet street in the seventh arrondissement on a warm September morning. Navy culottes fluttering in the breeze, a white cotton shirt with the sleeves rolled, a straw basket on the front of her bicycle full of flowers, and a pair of navy espadrilles on the pedals. The culottes look like a skirt when she rides, and like trousers when she stops. That is the magic.

Before we go to the fourth alternative, I want to ask you something. Tell me in the comments: are you a leggings woman? Do you wear them every day, sometimes, or never? And if you have already started moving away from them, what did you replace them with? I am so curious, because I think every one of us has a slightly different story about this.

The fourth alternative is straight knit pants.

This is the closest thing to leggings in comfort, and for many women, it is the gentlest step. Straight knit pants are trousers made of a soft, substantial knit fabric, a fine wool knit, a cotton-cashmere jersey, a thick ribbed knit, with an elastic or drawstring waist and a straight leg that falls without clinging.

Why does it work? Fabric. Unlike a thin legging, a good knit pant is made from a thicker, softer, more substantial yarn, so it falls away from the body rather than wrapping it. It has a lovely, gentle texture, especially in a fine rib, which catches light softly and creates little vertical lines that lengthen the leg. Line. The straight cut means the fabric drops from the hip without following every curve.

And comfort. They feel like pajamas. They feel like a cozy Sunday. But when you pair them with the right top and shoes, they look like a refined outfit.

The French way to wear them is often as a matching set. A knit pant and a knit sweater in the same color, grey with grey, navy with navy, oatmeal with oatmeal. That tonal look creates one long, unbroken column of color from the shoulders to the ankles, which looks deliberate, luxurious and very slimming.

How do they wear them? A grey ribbed knit pant with a matching grey sweater and white leather sneakers. A navy knit pant with a navy turtleneck, a camel coat and loafers. A cream knit pant and cream sweater under a long ivory coat for a soft winter look.

Picture a woman at a little café near the Jardin des Plantes on a Sunday morning, holding a bowl of café au lait in both hands. A matching set in soft heather grey, ribbed knit pant and fine sweater, a long camel coat over the back of her chair, white sneakers. She looks completely cozy and completely chic at the same time.

Now we come to the fifth alternative, the one I promised you. It is not a pair of trousers at all. And it is the one that changed my winter.

The fifth alternative is a midi skirt with boots.

I know. Many women stopped wearing skirts years ago, especially in winter. It seems cold, it seems complicated. But this combination is one of the most elegant and comfortable things you can wear, and it solves almost every problem leggings were trying to solve.

A midi skirt is a skirt that ends somewhere between just below the knee and the middle of the calf. For winter, think of a soft wool, a tweed, a corduroy, a heavy knit, or a fluid satin. Pleated, A-line, or slightly flared. And with it, a pair of boots, either knee-high leather boots that slide just under the hem, or ankle boots with opaque tights.

Why does it work? Proportion. The midi length hides the knees and thighs, which are often the areas we are most self-conscious about, while showing the slimmer part of the lower leg, or covering it completely with a boot. The boot and the skirt meet, or nearly meet, and create one continuous line. Line. A flared or pleated midi skirt moves when you walk. It swings and flows. It is the most feminine shape in the world, and on a woman over sixty, it looks gracious and confident.

And warmth. This is the secret. With thick opaque tights and a knee boot, you are just as warm as in trousers. Maybe warmer.

And here is where leggings come back, if you like. Many French women wear thin black tights or even fine leggings under a midi skirt and boots on very cold days. Hidden, they become a wonderful layer. It is only when they are worn alone that they become a problem.

How do they wear it? A grey wool pleated midi skirt, black opaque tights, black knee-high leather boots, a black turtleneck and a camel coat. A brown corduroy midi skirt, chocolate suede boots, an ivory cable knit. A navy satin midi skirt, black ankle boots with black tights, a grey sweater for an evening.

Picture a woman walking through the Tuileries on a crisp December afternoon, the bare chestnut trees in long rows, the gravel crunching under her steps. A charcoal wool midi skirt swinging just below her knees, tall black leather boots, a long camel coat open over a black sweater, a red scarf. She walks past the frozen fountain, and the skirt moves with every stride. She looks like winter itself, elegant and alive.

The sixth alternative is palazzo linen trousers.

Palazzo trousers are the widest of all wide trousers. They fall from a high waist in a very full, very fluid leg that reaches almost to the floor. In linen, they become the perfect summer garment.

Why does it work? Fabric. Linen is a natural fiber that breathes beautifully. It is cool on hot days. It has a lovely, slightly textured, matte surface that catches light softly. And it wrinkles, of course, but in a relaxed, natural way that looks lived-in and Mediterranean, never sloppy. Line. The palazzo shape creates such a long, full, vertical line that it makes you look tall and graceful, almost like wearing a long skirt, but you can still stride and sit cross-legged and climb stairs.

In summer, many women reach for leggings under a tunic or a long top. But in the heat, leggings are hot, sticky and clingy. Palazzo linen trousers are the opposite: airy, flowing and fresh.

How do they wear them? In white linen with a navy striped top and raffia espadrilles. In natural flax-colored linen with a white cotton shirt knotted at the waist. In black linen with a black sleeveless top, gold hoops and flat sandals for a summer evening. Always with a fitted or tucked top, so the volume of the trouser is balanced.

Picture a woman stepping off the little river boat at the Île Saint-Louis on a hot July evening, the sun low and golden on the water. White linen palazzo trousers moving in the breeze, a navy and white striped top, a straw hat in her hand, natural espadrilles. The trousers ripple like sails around her legs as she walks up the stone steps from the quai.

And the seventh and final alternative is the cigarette pant.

The cigarette pant is a slim, straight, tailored trouser that narrows slightly toward the ankle and ends just above it, showing a little sliver of skin. Usually in a firm wool, a stretch cotton twill or a crêpe, often in black, navy, grey or camel, with a flat front and a side zip or a discreet fastening.

Now, you may be thinking, but that is slim, isn't that close to a legging? Not at all, and here is the difference. Fabric. A cigarette pant is made of a woven fabric with structure. It does not cling. It is cut and sewn to follow the line of the leg from a slight distance, so it shapes the silhouette without exposing it. It has a pressed crease or a clean seam, which gives a sharp vertical line. Proportion. It ends at the ankle, showing the narrowest part of the leg, which makes the whole leg look slim and long.

The cigarette pant is perhaps the most Parisian trouser of all. It has a certain cool, intellectual elegance. It says I know exactly who I am.

How do they wear it? Black cigarette pants, a black turtleneck, black loafers and a bold gold chain, the classic chic. Navy cigarette pants with a striped top and a trench. Camel cigarette pants with a white shirt and nude ballerinas. With a slightly longer jacket or sweater that covers the hips, if you prefer, because the slim leg balances the length on top beautifully.

Picture a woman walking out of a gallery on the rue de Seine on a grey afternoon, a catalog tucked under her arm. Black cigarette pants ending just above the ankle, black suede loafers, a long camel blazer, a silk scarf knotted loosely at her neck. Her short silver hair catches the light. She stops to look in the window of a little antiques shop, one hand in her pocket, completely at ease.

So let us bring all seven together.

Instead of leggings worn as trousers, French women over sixty reach for wide-leg trousers, ponte pull-on trousers, culottes, straight knit pants, a midi skirt with boots, palazzo linen trousers in summer, and cigarette pants.

Look at what all seven have in common. Every one of them is made of a fabric with substance: wool, ponte, linen, a thick knit, a firm twill. Not a single one is thin and clingy. Every one of them creates a line of its own, a column, a swing, a crease, a gentle skim, rather than simply following the shape of your body. And every one of them is comfortable. That is so important. Not one of these alternatives asks you to give up comfort. Many have elastic waists, many are knits, many are wide and airy. They simply move the comfort to where no one can see it.

That is the principle. Comfort should be something you feel, not something people see.

If you want to start gently, I would suggest the ponte pull-on trousers or the straight knit pants. They feel the most like leggings, so the change is easy, but they look completely different. Then, when you feel ready, try the wide-leg trousers. They are the ones that give the biggest transformation, and the ones that most women tell me they never want to take off once they try them.

And do not throw your leggings away. Keep them for exercise, for long-haul flights under a dress, for layering under a midi skirt in January. They are a wonderful foundation. They just should not be the whole house.

When I think back to that Tuesday morning, standing at my window holding those old thin leggings up to the light, I remember feeling a little embarrassed, and then a little excited. Because it meant there was something new to discover. And there was. I now own two pairs of wide-leg trousers, one pair of ponte trousers and a grey wool midi skirt, and I have never been more comfortable or felt more like myself.
