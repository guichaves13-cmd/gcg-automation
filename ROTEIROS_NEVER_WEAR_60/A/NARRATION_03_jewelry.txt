On a cold Sunday morning at the flea market at Porte de Vanves, I watched a woman of about seventy open a little velvet tray of old jewelry at one of the stalls. She moved her fingers past the matching necklace and earring sets, past the strings of big plastic beads, past the tangled charm bracelets. And she stopped at one single thing. A plain gold signet ring, worn smooth on one side. She slipped it on her little finger, held her hand up to the grey light, and smiled. That was all she bought.

I have never forgotten that moment, because it taught me something I had been slowly understanding for years. Elegant women, as they get older, do not wear more jewelry. They wear better choices. Fewer pieces, with more meaning, placed exactly where they will catch the light.

So today I am going to share eight jewelry pieces that the French women I admire over sixty simply leave in the box, and for each one, exactly what they wear instead. And I want you to stay until the very last piece, because it is the one we look at most often in a day without ever thinking of it as jewelry at all. And once you change it, you will feel the difference every time you check the time.

Jewelry matters so much after sixty for a simple reason. It sits close to the face, the neck, the hands. These are the places people look at when they talk to us. And these are also the places where we often feel most self-conscious about age. The right piece draws the eye and lights you up. The wrong piece pulls you down, adds clutter, and sometimes even makes us look older than we are. So let us be thoughtful.

As always, this is not a list of rules. It is a set of observations and principles, about light, line, proportion and material, that you can take or leave. Let us begin.

The first piece they avoid is the matching jewelry set. The necklace, the earrings, the bracelet and sometimes the ring, all bought together, all in the same stone, the same metal, the same design.

There is something very understandable about a set. It feels safe. It feels complete. Often it was a gift, and it carries love with it. But visually, a matching set looks studied and a little old-fashioned. It looks like an outfit chosen for you by a department store display rather than by you. Everything is the same shape, the same sparkle, the same color, so the eye has nowhere to rest and nothing to discover. It is like a sentence where every word is the same.

So what do French women wear instead? Mixed metals. A gold chain with a silver watch. A silver cuff with a gold ring. Small gold hoops with a pair of steel-framed glasses. Pieces that were collected over a life rather than bought in one afternoon.

Why does this work? Because mixing metals feels personal and relaxed. It suggests that you have a history, that you have a grandmother's ring and a gift from a friend and something you bought yourself in a little shop on a trip. It also helps with color. As our hair turns grey and silver, a touch of silver near the face harmonizes with the hair, and a touch of gold near the hands warms the skin. Mixing lets you have both.

The trick is to repeat each metal at least twice so it looks intentional. Silver watch and silver earrings, gold ring and gold chain. Then it reads as a choice, not an accident.

Picture a woman at a café counter in the Latin Quarter, lifting a small espresso cup. A steel watch on her wrist, a thin gold band and a chunky silver ring on the same hand, small gold hoops at her ears. Her hair is pure white, pulled back. The metals catch the light from the window in two different colors, warm and cool, like she is wearing a little sunset.

The second piece they avoid is the chunky costume bead necklace. The big plastic or resin balls in bright colors, sometimes several strands twisted together, resting heavily on the chest.

Here is the problem. Light first. Plastic and resin reflect light in a flat, slightly cheap way, and that shine lands right under the chin. Line next. A heavy bead necklace sits at the base of the neck or the top of the chest and creates a thick horizontal band, which shortens the neck and widens the upper body. And proportion. The beads are often so big that they overwhelm the face. People see the necklace before they see you.

What do French women wear instead? A brooch on the lapel. I know. You might be thinking, a brooch, isn't that the most grandmotherly thing in the world? But in Paris, the brooch has come back in such a charming, modern way. A small vintage brooch, a little enamel bird, a gold bee, a cluster of tiny pearls, a flower, pinned not in the center of a blouse like a medal, but high on the lapel of a blazer, on the collar of a coat, on the shoulder of a simple black sweater.

Why does it work? Because it is placed high, near the face, and off-center. That asymmetry is modern and witty. It draws the eye up and toward the face, not down to the chest. It is small, so it never competes with you. And it tells a story. People ask about a brooch. Where did you find it? What does it mean? It becomes a conversation.

How do they wear it? On the lapel of a navy blazer. On the collar of a camel coat. On the knot of a silk scarf to hold it in place. Two or three small brooches clustered together on one shoulder of a black turtleneck, which is very chic and a little playful.

Picture a woman stepping out of the Musée d'Orsay into a bright winter afternoon. A charcoal wool coat, and on her lapel, a tiny gold dragonfly brooch catching the sun. That one small glint, right beside her face, is the thing you remember.

Now, if the bead necklace is too heavy, the third piece is the opposite problem. And this one fools many women, because it seems so discreet.

The third piece they avoid is the tiny thin chain that gets lost on the neck. A delicate little necklace, thin as a thread, sometimes with a minuscule pendant.

On a young woman's smooth neck, a thread of gold can be pretty. But as our skin softens and develops lines, a very fine chain tends to disappear into those lines. It sinks into the creases. It can even draw attention to them, because the eye follows the chain and finds the texture of the skin along the way. It is too small for the scale of a mature face, and it ends up looking like an afterthought, or like a necklace from a much younger life that we never updated.

So what do they wear instead? One bold gold chain. A substantial chain with visible links, sometimes a flat curb chain, sometimes a rounded rope, sometimes a slightly chunky paperclip shape. Worn alone, at the collarbone or a little below.

Why does it work? Scale. A stronger chain matches the presence of a woman who has lived a full life. It sits on top of the skin rather than sinking into it. The links catch the light along the whole length, so it glows rather than glints. And worn alone, it looks confident and modern, like a piece of sculpture.

How do they style it? Over a black turtleneck, so the gold stands out against the dark wool. With an open white shirt, the chain resting on the skin at the collarbone. Over a navy crewneck. With a striped top. It looks equally right at the market and at a dinner.

Picture a woman at a table on the terrace of a café on Boulevard Saint-Germain, wrapped in a black turtleneck, a bold gold chain resting on top, her silver hair cut short. The streetlights are just coming on. The chain catches that first evening light, and she looks like the most interesting woman on the street.

Before we go further, I want to hear from you. Tell me in the comments: what is the one piece of jewelry you wear every single day? Is it a wedding ring, a watch, a pair of earrings? And is there a story behind it? I read every comment, and some of your stories have truly moved me.

The fourth piece they avoid is too many rings. Rings on every finger, sometimes two or three on one finger, all different stones and styles competing for attention.

I understand the impulse. Our hands carry our memories. Each ring might be a gift, an anniversary, a loss. But visually, too many rings make the hands look busy and heavy. The eye jumps from stone to stone and never settles. And if our knuckles have grown a little larger with age, rings stacked over them draw attention there. The hands start to look cluttered rather than elegant.

What do French women wear instead? A signet ring. Just like the woman at the flea market. A simple flat-topped ring, in gold or silver, sometimes engraved with initials or a little crest, sometimes plain, worn on the little finger or the ring finger. Often alongside a wedding band, and nothing else on that hand.

Why does it work? Because a signet ring is quiet and strong at the same time. It has a solid, sculptural shape that suits a mature hand. It does not sparkle, it gleams. It has history. It was worn by men and women for centuries, and that gives it a kind of timeless authority. One beautiful ring says more than ten small ones.

If you have several rings you love, rotate them. Wear one or two at a time, not all of them. Let each one have its moment.

How do they style it? With a bare wrist or a single watch on the other hand. With short, clean nails, often unpolished or in a soft nude or a deep red. That combination of a plain signet ring and a red nail is one of the most elegant small details I know.

Picture a woman in a little bookshop on rue de l'Odéon, running her hand along a shelf of old books. A gold signet ring on her little finger, nails painted a deep red, a navy cashmere sleeve pushed back. Just the hand, in close-up, and you already know she has excellent taste.

Now, I mentioned at the beginning that the last piece is one we look at many times a day. We are getting there. But first, let us talk about the wrist.

The fifth piece they avoid is the charm bracelet. The chain full of little dangling pendants, hearts, keys, animals, letters, all jingling together.

Charm bracelets carry sentiment, and I would never tell anyone to throw one away. But worn every day, a charm bracelet looks youthful in a way that does not quite work on a mature wrist. It is busy, it is noisy, it catches on sleeves and sweaters, and all those little pieces break the line of the arm. Instead of a clean, graceful wrist, you get a cluster of clutter at the end of the arm.

What do they wear instead? A single cuff. A solid open bangle, in gold or silver, smooth or lightly hammered, sometimes a little wide, sometimes slim. Worn alone, on one wrist.

Why does it work? Line. A cuff wraps the wrist in one clean, continuous shape. It creates a strong graphic accent at the end of the sleeve, which draws the eye to the elegance of the hand and the gesture. It is silent. It does not jingle. It does not catch. And it looks modern and architectural at any age.

How do they style it? On a bare wrist with a short-sleeved black top in summer. Pushed over the cuff of a white shirt. Worn with a three-quarter sleeve sweater so the cuff sits right at the edge of the sleeve. A silver cuff with silver hair is especially striking.

Picture a woman at a gallery in the Marais, standing in front of a big abstract canvas, her arms crossed thoughtfully. A black dress with three-quarter sleeves, and on one wrist a wide hammered silver cuff. She lifts her hand to point at something in the painting, and the cuff catches the white gallery light. It is the most graceful gesture in the room.

The sixth piece is all about the ears, and this one is very personal for many of us.

The sixth piece they avoid is the large dangling earring that pulls the lobe. Heavy drop earrings, chandeliers, long hanging shapes that swing and tug.

Here is what happens. With time, our earlobes naturally soften and lengthen a little. A heavy earring pulls them further down, stretches the piercing into a slit, and draws attention to exactly that. The weight is uncomfortable, too, so we take them off by lunchtime. And visually, a long dangling earring pulls the eye downward, along the jaw and the neck, which are often the areas we feel less confident about. It drags the face down instead of lifting it.

What do French women wear instead? Small hoops. Little gold or silver hoops that sit close to the lobe, from the size of a fingertip up to about the size of a coin. Sometimes slightly chunky, sometimes thin, but always light.

Why do they work? Small hoops sit up high, close to the face, so they draw the eye up toward the cheekbones and the eyes. The curve of the hoop echoes the curve of the face and softens it. They catch the light right next to the skin, like a tiny reflector. And they are light, so they do not pull the lobe and you can wear them from morning until night.

How do they style them? With everything, honestly. With hair tucked behind the ear so they show. With a short haircut, where they become the only ornament on the face. With a turtleneck, with a silk scarf, with a simple T-shirt. Many women I know have not taken theirs off in years.

Picture a woman cycling slowly along the quai near Notre-Dame, a basket of flowers on the front of her bicycle. Her short grey hair is tucked behind her ears, and two little gold hoops flash in the sunlight as she turns her head. It is the smallest detail and it makes her look radiant.

The seventh piece they avoid is yellow-gold overload. A necklace, earrings, bracelets, rings and a watch, all in bright yellow gold, all worn at the same time.

Now, gold is beautiful, and I just told you how much I love a bold gold chain. But too much bright yellow gold at once becomes heavy and loud. The color is very warm and very reflective, and when it covers the neck, the ears, the wrists and the hands all together, it can make the skin look sallow by comparison, especially if your skin has cooled and your hair has turned silver. It looks like wealth on display rather than taste on display. And French women, as a rule, prefer to be quiet about their treasures.

So what do they wear instead? Pearl studs. A simple pair of pearl earrings, round, sitting right on the lobe, in white, in cream, or in a soft grey or pink tone.

Why pearls? Light. A pearl does not glitter. It glows. It has a soft, milky luster that reflects light gently onto the skin, almost like a little lamp beside the face. It brightens the complexion without any harshness. It works with silver hair, with white hair, with dyed hair, with every skin tone. And a small pearl stud, worn alone, balances any other gold you are wearing so that the whole look stays calm.

The modern way to wear them is with something casual. A white T-shirt. A denim shirt. A grey sweatshirt even. Pearls with a casual garment look effortless and French. Pearls with a formal twinset and a matching necklace look like a portrait in a bank lobby.

Picture a woman sitting on the stone edge of a fountain in the Luxembourg Gardens, a denim shirt open at the neck, sleeves rolled, and two small pearl studs at her ears. She tilts her face up to the sun. The pearls glow against her skin, and she looks fresh, rested and luminous.

And now, the eighth and final piece. The one I promised at the start. The one we look at all day long.

The eighth piece they avoid is the plastic watch. The chunky rubber or plastic watch with a big digital face, or a cheap fashion watch with a plastic strap and a shiny dial.

I understand the practical side. They are light, they are waterproof, some of them count your steps. And at the gym or on a long walk in the country, they are perfect. But in everyday life, a plastic watch makes the whole wrist look sporty and temporary. The plastic has that flat, slightly dull shine. The shape is often bulky. And it clashes with every other piece of jewelry you wear. You can wear the most beautiful cashmere and pearls in the world, but if your wrist says gym, the outfit says unfinished.

So what do French women wear instead? A vintage watch. A small, simple watch with a round or square face, hands instead of digits, and a leather strap or a metal bracelet. Often something inherited, or found secondhand, or simply a classic, plain design that could have been made fifty years ago.

Why does it work? Because a watch with a leather strap and a clean face looks like jewelry, not equipment. The leather softens with time and molds to your wrist. The small face suits the scale of a woman's wrist. And there is something deeply elegant about turning your wrist to read real hands on a real dial. It is a gesture that slows time down a little.

If you love your fitness watch, keep it for walks and wear the classic one for the rest of the day. Or choose a simple watch with a leather or metal band and keep it as your daily companion.

How do they style it? A small gold watch on a brown leather strap with a white shirt and a signet ring on the other hand. A steel watch with a navy sweater and small silver hoops. A watch with a worn black strap worn slightly loose, so it slides a little on the wrist, which is very Parisian.

Picture a woman waiting on a platform in the Métro at Saint-Paul, a camel coat over her shoulders. She pushes back her sleeve to check the time, and we see it: a small round gold watch on a softened brown leather strap. She smiles, because she is early, and she takes out a paperback book.

So let us gather all eight together.

Instead of the matching jewelry set, mixed metals collected over a life.

Instead of chunky costume beads, a brooch on the lapel.

Instead of the tiny thin chain lost on the neck, one bold gold chain.

Instead of too many rings, a single signet ring.

Instead of the charm bracelet, a single cuff.

Instead of large dangling earrings that pull the lobe, small hoops.

Instead of yellow-gold overload, pearl studs.

And instead of the plastic watch, a vintage watch with a leather strap.

Now, what do all of these replacements have in common? They are fewer. They are placed with intention, usually high, near the face, or at a clean edge like the wrist or the lapel. They are made of materials that glow rather than glitter: pearl, solid gold, silver, leather. And every single one of them has a story or the possibility of a story.

That is the principle I want you to take with you. Jewelry after sixty is not decoration. It is punctuation. It tells people where to look. And you want them to look at your eyes, your smile, your hands when you talk. Every piece should lead them there.

Here is a little exercise I do and I recommend to you. Get dressed completely, put on all the jewelry you would normally wear, and stand in front of a mirror. Then take off one piece. Then another. Stop when you feel a little twinge, the feeling that something is now missing. That is usually the perfect amount.

And do not forget your own jewelry box. You may already own a signet ring, a pearl stud, a small hoop, a watch that belonged to someone you loved. Sometimes the best piece is not the one you buy, but the one you rediscover in a drawer, polish, and finally decide to wear every day.

When I think about that woman at the market at Porte de Vanves, holding that little signet ring up to the light, I think about how much freedom there is in choosing just one thing. She did not need the trays and trays of sparkle. She knew exactly what she was looking for.

Now tell me in the comments: which of these eight are you going to change first? And which piece of jewelry from your own life do you treasure most? I would love to hear about your mother's pearls, your husband's watch, the ring you bought for yourself after a hard year. Those are the real stories behind elegance.

Next time, we are going to talk about the one garment that covers everything else when winter arrives, and what French women over sixty refuse to wear on cold days. Until then, choose fewer pieces, choose them with love, and let them glow. À bientôt.
