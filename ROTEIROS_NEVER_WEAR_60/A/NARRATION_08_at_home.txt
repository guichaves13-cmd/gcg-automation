The doorbell rang at ten past nine on a Saturday morning. I was not expecting anyone. I was standing in my kitchen in an enormous old T-shirt that had belonged to my son, with a faded coffee stain right over the heart, a pair of grey fleece sweatpants, and slippers so worn the backs had folded flat under my heels. I opened the door, and there was my neighbor from the fourth floor, a woman of about seventy-five, holding a parcel that had been delivered to her by mistake. She was wearing a navy and white striped top, wide cream cotton trousers, soft leather slippers, and her silver hair was pinned up with a tortoiseshell clip. She had clearly not left her apartment yet either. She was simply at home. And she looked lovely.

I thanked her, closed the door, and stood in my hallway for a long moment. Not because I was embarrassed in front of her. She was far too kind to notice. But because I realized I had been dressing for nobody. And the nobody I had been dressing for, every single morning, was me.

So today, I want to talk about something very intimate, something no one else usually sees. What French women over sixty never wear at home, and what they wear instead. Seven things. And I want you to stay until the sixth one, because it is a single garment that has completely changed my mornings, and I think it might change yours. It is the piece I reach for first now, before coffee, before anything.

Let me be clear about one thing first. Home is where we rest. Home is where we should be comfortable, soft, relaxed. I am not asking anyone to wear a blazer to water the plants. But there is a difference between comfortable and careless. And what I have noticed, in the homes of the elegant women I know here, is that they treat their own daily life as worthy of a little beauty. Not for visitors. For themselves.

Because here is the thing. If you are retired, or if you work from home, or if your children have grown and left, you may spend most of your hours at home now. That means what you wear at home is, in fact, what you wear most of your life. And how we dress affects how we move, how we stand, how we feel when we pass a mirror, even what we decide to do with our day.

So let us begin.

The first thing they avoid at home is the oversized stained T-shirt. The huge, shapeless old T-shirt, often borrowed from a husband or a child, stretched at the neck, faded, with a stain or two that never quite came out.

Fabric first. A cotton T-shirt that has been washed hundreds of times becomes thin, limp and slightly grey. It loses all its body. The neckline stretches into a wavy, drooping curve that sits low and loose, showing the neck and chest in the least flattering way. Line. Because it is oversized, it hangs from the shoulders like a tent, with no shape, so the body underneath looks larger and shapeless. Light. That faded grey-white tone drains the face, especially first thing in the morning, when we have no makeup and our complexion is at its palest. And the stains. Every time we pass a mirror, we see them. And they quietly tell us something unkind about ourselves.

What do French women wear instead? A striped marinière. Exactly what my neighbor was wearing. A breton striped top, in a substantial cotton jersey, with a boat neckline and long or three-quarter sleeves, in navy and white, or red and white, or black and cream.

Why does it work? Because it is just as comfortable as a T-shirt. It is soft cotton. You can garden in it, cook in it, read in it, nap in it. But the thicker cotton holds its shape, so it skims the body rather than drooping. The boat neckline sits neatly across the collarbones and frames the face. And the stripes do something magical. They bring energy and light. The white stripes reflect light onto the face, the navy ones give definition. Even with no makeup, even with messy hair, a striped top makes you look fresh and awake.

How do they wear it at home? With wide cotton trousers and leather slippers. With straight jeans when they might pop out to the bakery. Sleeves pushed up to the elbow while cooking. With hair pinned up with a clip. Some women own three or four, and wear them in rotation all week.

Picture a woman in a small apartment kitchen in the eleventh arrondissement, early morning light coming through tall windows, a pot of coffee on the stove. She is wearing a navy and white striped top, sleeves pushed up, slicing a peach on a wooden board. A pot of basil on the windowsill. She is alone. And she looks like a painting.

The second thing they avoid at home is fleece sweatpants. The thick, fuzzy jogging trousers with elastic at the ankles, often in grey or navy, often pilled and baggy at the knees.

I know. They are so warm. So soft. But look at the shape. The elastic cuff at the ankle gathers the fabric into a puff, which shortens the leg and makes the calves and the hips look wider by comparison. The fleece itself is thick and bulky, so it adds volume everywhere. And after a few washes, fleece pills and goes flat and dull. It becomes the uniform of giving up, the garment that says I am not going anywhere today, and I am not planning to do anything either.

What do French women wear instead? Wide-leg cotton trousers. Soft, wide trousers in a cotton twill, a brushed cotton, a cotton jersey or a light cotton canvas, with an elastic or drawstring waist, a straight wide leg that falls to the ankle, and no cuff.

Why does it work? Comfort, first, because this is what matters at home. They are just as easy as sweatpants, just as soft, with that same elastic waist. But line. The wide straight leg falls freely from the hip without gathering at the ankle, so it creates a long, clean, vertical line that makes you look taller and slimmer. And fabric. Cotton twill or brushed cotton has a soft matte texture that holds a little shape, so it looks like real clothing, not like gym wear.

What colors? Cream, ecru, navy, a soft stone grey, a washed khaki, a deep charcoal. Cream wide trousers with a striped top is one of the freshest home outfits you can imagine.

How do they wear them? With the marinière. With a fine knit. With a linen shirt. With leather slippers or barefoot. And the beauty is that if someone rings the doorbell, or if you suddenly decide to go out for a walk, you can simply add shoes and a coat and you are ready.

Picture a woman on a small wrought-iron balcony overlooking a quiet street in the Batignolles, watering a row of red geraniums with a little copper can. Wide cream cotton trousers moving in the breeze, a grey sweater, bare feet on the tiles. A church bell rings somewhere. She stops to listen.

The third thing they avoid is the old nightgown worn all day. The long, faded cotton or nylon nightdress, perhaps with a little lace at the neck, worn from bedtime until well into the afternoon.

There is something very sweet about a nightgown at night. But when it is worn all day, it changes our relationship with time. The day never begins. We drift. And visually, an old nightgown is often thin, crumpled from sleep, washed so many times it has become shapeless. The neckline may be loose, the hem uneven, the fabric slightly transparent in daylight. It is the garment of illness, of convalescence. And when we wear it all day, we feel, without even noticing, a little bit like a patient in our own home.

What do French women wear instead? A linen shirt. A loose, long, slightly oversized shirt in soft washed linen, in white, in sky blue, in natural flax, sometimes with a long length that covers the hips, worn open over a camisole or buttoned, with sleeves rolled to the elbow.

Why does it work? Fabric. Washed linen is one of the most beautiful fabrics in the world to live in. It is soft, it breathes, it is cool in summer and comfortable in winter over a knit. It has a lovely texture, slightly irregular, that catches light in a natural, gentle way. And its wrinkles look relaxed and charming, never sloppy. Line. A shirt has a collar, a placket, cuffs. It has structure, even when it is loose. It frames the face, and it says the day has begun.

And a long linen shirt has all the easy, floaty comfort of a nightgown, but it belongs to the day.

How do they wear it? Over wide cotton trousers. Over the straight knit pants of a knit set. Belted loosely at the waist with a thin leather belt over leggings, the one place leggings work well at home. Sleeves rolled, collar open, a little pair of gold hoops in the ears.

Picture a woman sitting at a small round table by an open window in the Marais on a warm June morning, a cup of tea and an open book in front of her. A long white linen shirt, sleeves rolled to the elbow, wide navy cotton trousers, her silver hair loose on her shoulders. The curtains move gently in the breeze. She turns a page.

Before we go to the fourth thing, I want to ask you something. Tell me in the comments: what are you wearing right now, as you watch this? Be honest. No judgment, I promise. I want to know how many of us are in an old T-shirt and slippers, and how many are already in a striped top. I have a feeling the answers will make us all smile.

The fourth thing they avoid is slipper scuffs. The old slippers with the backs folded down, the soles worn smooth, the fabric stained and flattened, making that sliding, scuffing sound on the floor.

Slippers seem like a tiny thing. But they change how we walk. When the back of the slipper is crushed, we drag our feet to keep them on. We shuffle instead of stepping. That shuffle changes the whole posture, the hips push forward, the shoulders round, the head drops. And shuffling, more than anything else, is a movement we associate with age. A woman who walks with a light step looks younger in her own hallway than a woman who scuffs. And there is a safety side too. Old slippers with smooth soles can slip on tiles and stairs.

What do French women wear instead? Leather slippers. Soft, real leather slippers with a closed back or a structured heel, a thin leather or suede sole, sometimes lined in wool or shearling for winter. Often in the shape of a little loafer or a moccasin, in brown, burgundy, navy or black.

Why does it work? Because a leather slipper holds the foot. The heel is supported, so you step rather than shuffle. Your posture straightens. Your walk becomes light. The leather molds to your foot over time and becomes more comfortable the more you wear it. And visually, a leather slipper looks like a shoe. It finishes the outfit. If you step out onto the landing to collect the mail, you look perfectly dressed.

How do they wear them? With everything at home. Brown leather slippers with cream trousers and a striped top. Burgundy velvet slippers with a navy knit set in winter. Black leather slippers with a black linen shirt and black trousers on a quiet evening.

Picture a woman walking down the old wooden staircase of a Haussmann building to collect her post, the morning light coming through a tall window on the landing. A grey knit set, soft brown leather slippers, a cup of coffee in one hand. Her steps are light and quick on the creaking stairs. She opens the little brass letterbox in the hall, smiles at a postcard, and goes back up.

The fifth thing they avoid at home is the hoodie. The thick cotton or fleece sweatshirt with a hood, a front pouch pocket, often a drawstring, sometimes a logo or a slogan across the chest.

The hoodie is comfortable, yes. But look at the line. The hood bunches up at the back of the neck and pulls the shoulders and head forward, which creates a slight hunch. The front pouch pocket adds bulk right over the stomach. The ribbed hem gathers at the hips, which makes them look wider. And the whole silhouette reads very young and very sporty, like a teenager on a phone, which on a woman of sixty or seventy can look a little lost, as if the clothes belong to someone else.

What do French women wear instead? A cashmere-blend knit set. A matching fine knit sweater and knit trousers, in a soft blend of cashmere, wool or cotton, in the same color. Grey with grey. Oatmeal with oatmeal. Navy with navy. Camel with camel.

Why does it work? Fabric. A fine cashmere or wool blend is wonderfully soft against the skin, just as cozy as a sweatshirt, but much lighter and warmer for its weight. It drapes rather than bulks. It has a soft, gentle luster that flatters the skin. Line. When the top and bottom are in the same color, they create one long unbroken column from the shoulders to the ankles, which looks calm, elegant and slimming. And there is something deeply luxurious about a matching set. It feels like a gift you give yourself.

You do not need pure cashmere. A blend with wool or cotton is softer on the budget and easier to wash. What matters is the feel and the matching color.

How do they wear it? With leather slippers at home. With white leather sneakers to go to the market. With a long camel coat over it for a walk. With small gold hoops and a little lipstick, so that the softness of the knit is balanced by a touch of polish.

Picture a woman curled up on a deep velvet sofa by a window in Montmartre on a rainy November afternoon, a cat beside her and a book on her knees. A heather grey cashmere-blend knit set, her feet tucked under her, a cup of tea steaming on the low table. Rain runs down the window. She looks warm, peaceful and quietly beautiful.

Now, the sixth thing. I told you it was the garment that changed my mornings. Here it is. But first, what it replaces.

The sixth thing they avoid is the bathrobe worn until noon. The thick terry cloth robe, often in white or pastel, a little pilled, the belt lost or knotted, worn from waking up until lunchtime or later.

A bathrobe is meant for the bathroom. It is wonderful when you step out of a hot shower. But worn all morning, it becomes heavy and bulky. The thick terry cloth adds volume everywhere and makes the body look like a soft, shapeless mass. The belt cinches the middle into a bunch. The big shawl collar sits heavily around the neck. And it carries a feeling of not quite being ready, of being in between. Every time you see yourself in the mirror, you think, I really should get dressed. And somehow, the morning slips away in that feeling.

What do French women wear instead? A kimono jacket. This is the garment I promised you. A light, open, wrap-style jacket inspired by the kimono shape, with wide sleeves, a straight cut, no buttons, sometimes a soft tie at the waist, in cotton, linen, silk or a light viscose. Sometimes printed with flowers, birds, little geometric patterns. Sometimes in a solid color, navy, deep green, ivory, rust.

Why does it change everything? Because the kimono jacket is the bridge between the bathrobe and real clothes. You put it on when you wake up, over a nightdress or over a camisole and soft trousers, and it immediately makes you feel dressed. It has that same easy wrap of a robe, but it is light, it drapes beautifully, it falls in long vertical lines from the shoulders, and it has a sense of style, of intention. You can make coffee in it, write letters in it, answer the door in it. And when you add trousers and a top underneath, it becomes a lovely light jacket for the whole day.

This is what I do now. I wake up, I wash my face, I put on a little moisturizer, and I put on my kimono jacket, a deep navy one with a soft white floral print, over whatever I slept in. And from that moment, the day has begun. I feel elegant. I feel awake. And I almost always get dressed properly within the hour, because the kimono has already put me in the right mood.

How do they wear it? Over a matching pajama set in the morning. Over a striped top and wide trousers in the afternoon. Over a simple black dress in the evening, almost like a light coat, especially in summer.

Picture a woman standing at an open French window on a sunny spring morning, looking out over the rooftops and chimneys of Paris. A long kimono jacket in soft green silk with a white bird print falling open over cream trousers, a cup of coffee in her hand. The sleeves move in the breeze. The pigeons coo on the zinc roof opposite. She closes her eyes and breathes in the morning.

The seventh and final thing they avoid is mismatched pajamas. The old flannel top with a different pair of cotton bottoms, faded, washed out, sometimes one with stripes and one with flowers, often one of them a little too small or a little too big.

Pajamas may seem like the most private thing of all. But they are the first and last thing we wear every day, and they set the tone. When they are mismatched and worn out, they feel like what is left over, the clothes we did not care enough to choose. Visually, two mismatched pieces cut the body in half at the waist, with two unrelated colors and patterns. And the faded fabrics make us look tired, at the very moments, morning and evening, when we already feel tired.

What do French women wear instead? A matching cotton pajama set. A classic button-front pajama, with a collar and piping, top and bottom in the same fabric. In a fine cotton poplin for summer, a soft brushed cotton or a cotton flannel for winter. In stripes, in a small print, in white, in pale blue, in navy.

Why does it work? Because a matching set is a complete, thought-through outfit. It has a collar that frames the face. It has buttons, a placket, piping, a neat structure. In one fabric and color, it creates a long, calm line. Fine cotton breathes and becomes softer with every wash. And there is something deeply restful about going to bed in something lovely and waking up in something lovely. It makes the night feel like an occasion.

You do not need many. Two or three sets in rotation are perfect. And when one wears out, replace it. You deserve to sleep in something that makes you feel cared for.

How do they wear it? With the kimono jacket over it in the morning. With leather slippers. With hair loosely braided at night. In blue and white stripes in summer, in navy with white piping in winter.

Picture a woman in a bedroom with tall windows and white curtains in the sixth arrondissement, on a Sunday morning. She is sitting on the edge of a neatly made bed in a matching pale blue and white striped cotton pajama set, pulling on a pair of soft leather slippers. A breakfast tray on the side table with a croissant and a little pot of jam. Sunlight on the parquet floor. It is the most peaceful image in the world.

So let us gather all seven together.

Instead of the oversized stained T-shirt, a striped marinière.

Instead of fleece sweatpants, wide-leg cotton trousers.

Instead of the old nightgown worn all day, a long linen shirt.

Instead of scuffed old slippers, real leather slippers.

Instead of the hoodie, a cashmere-blend knit set.

Instead of the bathrobe until noon, a kimono jacket.

And instead of mismatched pajamas, a matching cotton pajama set.

What do all seven have in common? They are all just as comfortable as what they replace. Every one of them is soft, easy, relaxed. But every one of them is also made of a fabric with a little dignity, good cotton, linen, cashmere blend, leather, silk. Every one of them holds a shape, a collar, a neckline, a straight leg, a clean line. And every one of them makes you feel, when you catch your reflection in a window or a mirror, that you are a woman who cares about her own life.

That is the principle I want you to take away. Home is not a place where elegance stops. Home is where it begins. The way you dress when no one is watching is the truest expression of how you feel about yourself.

If you want to start with just one thing, I would start with the kimono jacket or the striped top. They are the easiest to find, the easiest to wear, and they give the biggest change in how you feel in the mornings. Then, slowly, replace the other things as they wear out. There is no need to throw everything away at once.

When I think back to that Saturday morning, opening the door in my stained T-shirt to my neighbor in her striped top and leather slippers, I feel grateful to her. She never said a word. She did not have to. She simply showed me, without meaning to, that a woman can be completely at ease at home and still look like herself on a good day. Now, every time the doorbell rings unexpectedly, I am ready. And more importantly, every time I pass my own mirror, I smile.

Now tell me in the comments: which of these seven are you going to change first? And what is the one thing you love wearing at home most of all? Maybe it is a pair of slippers you have had for years, or a robe that belonged to your mother. I love hearing about those little treasures.
