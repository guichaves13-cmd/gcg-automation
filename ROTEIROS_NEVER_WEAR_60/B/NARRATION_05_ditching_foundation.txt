I want you to picture a small bathroom in an old apartment near the Place d'Italie. Black and white tiles, a little window looking onto a courtyard, a radiator that clanks every morning at seven. On the edge of the sink there used to be a heavy glass bottle of full-coverage foundation. I bought that same foundation for almost twenty years. Every morning, I would pump it onto a sponge and cover my entire face, ears to chin, like I was painting a fence.

One day, about four years ago, I ran out. The shop was closed for a holiday, and I had a lunch with an old friend. So I went with nothing but a little moisturizer, a dab of concealer on one spot near my nose, and some cream blush I found at the back of a drawer. And at lunch, my friend, who has never in her life paid me a compliment without a reason, looked at me across the table and asked if I had started doing something new for my skin.

I never bought that bottle again.

I am in my sixties now, and I have lived around Paris for most of my life. And I have noticed something happening quietly, among my friends, among women I see in the shops and on the metro. More and more women over sixty are simply putting the foundation away. Not dramatically, not as a statement. They just stop. And they replace it with a handful of lighter, smarter steps that make the skin look like skin again.

So today, I want to tell you why so many women over sixty are ditching foundation, and exactly what they are using instead. Six steps, each one simple, each one with a specific product type and technique. And stay with me until the fifth step, because it is the one that gives that lit-from-within glow everyone notices, and almost nobody talks about it.

Before we begin, tell me in the comments. Do you still wear foundation every day? Yes, no, or only for special occasions? I am curious where we all are.

First, let us talk about why full-coverage foundation works against a mature face.

When we are young, foundation sits on top of smooth, plump skin that has a natural bounce. As we age, skin changes. It becomes thinner and drier, it loses some of its elasticity, and fine lines appear, especially around the eyes and mouth. Pores can look a little larger on the cheeks. None of this is a problem. It is simply what skin does as it lives.

But full-coverage foundation, especially a thick or matte formula, interacts with these changes in a very unkind way. It is designed to sit on top of the skin and create an even, opaque layer. On mature skin, that layer does not stay smooth. It settles into fine lines and creases as the face moves through the day. It clings to dry patches. It can separate around the nose and gather in the pores. By the afternoon, the very lines we wanted to hide look deeper and more defined than they would on bare skin.

Then there is light. Healthy, youthful skin has a natural translucence. Light passes slightly into it and bounces back, which gives that soft glow. A thick foundation blocks that. It makes the skin look flat, like a matte painted surface, and under the cool light of a shop or a train, flat skin can look gray and tired.

And finally, there is the mask effect. Full coverage removes all the natural variation in the skin, the little bit of pink on the cheeks, the slight warmth on the nose. Without that variation, the face looks still and lifeless. Paradoxically, the more you cover, the older the skin can look.

So what are women using instead? Here are the six steps.

The first step is a tinted moisturizer.

This is the foundation replacement for most women I know. A tinted moisturizer is just what it sounds like. A moisturizer with a small amount of pigment. It gives sheer coverage, just enough to even out a little redness or unevenness, while keeping the skin hydrated. And hydration is half the battle for mature skin.

Why does it work? Because a sheer, moist texture moves with the skin rather than sitting on top of it. It does not crack or settle into lines because it is not thick enough to. The little bit of tint evens things out, and the moisture gives the skin a soft, healthy sheen that reflects light.

How do they apply it? With the fingers. A small amount, about the size of a pea, warmed between the fingertips and pressed into the center of the face, the cheeks, the nose, the chin, the forehead, then blended outward. The warmth of the fingers helps it melt into the skin. Most women choose a shade that matches their neck, so the face and neck look the same. And here is a tip I learned from a friend who worked behind a beauty counter for many years. Always check the shade by a window in daylight, never under a bathroom bulb.

Picture a woman stepping out of a café on the Rue des Martyrs on a spring morning, her face catching the sun, and her skin looks clear, fresh, and a little dewy. You could not tell she was wearing anything at all.

Now, a tinted moisturizer evens things out, but it does not hide everything. And that is exactly what the next step is for.

The second step is spot concealer.

Here is the shift in thinking. Instead of covering the whole face to hide a few small things, you cover only the few small things. A little redness by the nose, a dark spot on the cheek, a small blemish. Spot concealing means precision, not coverage.

Why does it work? Because most of the face does not need coverage at all. If you cover only where you need it, the rest of the skin can look natural and luminous, and the eye simply does not notice the spots anymore.

How do they do it? They choose a creamy concealer that matches the skin exactly, not lighter. A concealer that is too light draws a pale halo around whatever it is hiding. With a small, pointed brush or the tip of the ring finger, they dab a tiny amount directly onto the spot. Then they tap gently around the edges to blend, without rubbing over the center. That way the coverage stays where it is needed, and the edges disappear into the skin.

For under the eyes, they are very careful. Thick concealer under the eyes tends to settle into the fine lines there. So instead, they use a very small amount of a light, creamy concealer only in the inner corner, where shadows are usually darkest, and blend it outward softly. Not a big triangle. Just a little light in the shadow.

Imagine a woman at a bookstall along the Quai Voltaire, leaning in to look at an old print, and up close, her skin looks clear and soft, with no line of makeup anywhere. That is spot concealing.

Before we go on, I want to know. What is the one spot on your face you always want to cover? For me, it has always been the redness around my nose. Tell me yours in the comments.

The third step is an SPF tint.

This is one many women overlook, but in Paris I see it more and more. A tinted sunscreen. Sunscreen with a little sheer color in it. On days when they do not want even a tinted moisturizer, this is the only thing many women put on their face besides skincare.

Why does it work? First, because daily sun protection is one of the most important things we can do for our skin at any age. Your dermatologist will tell you that, and I would encourage you to ask them what kind of sun protection is right for your skin. Second, because a tinted sunscreen does double duty. It protects, and it gives a light wash of color that evens out the skin. And many tinted sunscreens, especially those with mineral filters, have a slight tint precisely to avoid the white cast that untinted mineral sunscreens can leave on the skin.

How do they wear it? As the last step of skincare in the morning, applied generously over the face, neck, and the backs of the hands. Because sunscreen needs to be applied in a fairly generous amount to work as intended, many women find that a tinted one gives them enough coverage that they need nothing else. On top, a dab of concealer where needed, and that is the base done.

Picture a woman walking through the Jardin des Plantes on a sunny afternoon, a straw hat on her head, her skin looking even and healthy, with a soft, natural finish. She has protected her skin and evened it in one step.

Now, we have the base. But a bare base can look a little flat on its own. And that is where the next step changes everything.

The fourth step is cream blush.

If there is one product that women who ditch foundation always keep, it is cream blush. Because when you stop covering the whole face, you suddenly need to bring back some life and color. And cream blush does that better than anything.

Why cream and not powder? Because cream melts into the skin. It does not sit on top, it does not cling to dry patches, and it does not settle into lines. It gives a soft, translucent flush that looks like your own color, like you just came in from a cold walk. Powder blush on mature skin can look dusty and sit visibly on the surface, especially without foundation underneath to grip it.

How do they apply it? With the fingertips. A small dab on the top of the cheekbone, slightly toward the temple, then tapped and blended upward and outward. Not on the apples of the cheeks, which can drag the face down. High and lifted. Many women also tap the same color on the lips, so everything matches. Shades are soft and natural. Rosy pinks for cooler skin, warm apricots and peaches for warmer skin, a soft berry for deeper skin tones.

I remember an older woman I used to see in the mornings at the bakery near my building. She always looked so well. One day I asked her what her secret was. She opened her handbag and showed me a little pot of cream blush. She said, this is the only makeup I own. Then she winked and took her baguette.

Picture a woman at a flower market near the Île de la Cité, holding a bunch of peonies close to her face, and her cheeks are almost the same soft pink as the petals. That is cream blush.

Before the next step, the one I promised you at the beginning, I want to ask. Do you use blush? Cream or powder? Tell me in the comments. I find that so many women over sixty stopped using blush at some point, and it is one of the best things you can bring back.

The fifth step is facial oil glow.

This is the step that gives that lit-from-within look. The one that makes people ask you what you have been doing. And it is simply a tiny amount of facial oil, used strategically.

Why does it work? Because glow is about light. Mature skin often loses some of its natural radiance as it becomes drier. A very small amount of facial oil adds a soft, healthy sheen to the skin that catches the light. Not greasy, not shiny like a forehead at the end of a long day. Just a gentle luminosity on the high points of the face.

How do they use it? There are two ways I see French women do it. The first is to mix a single drop of facial oil into the tinted moisturizer before applying it. This makes the base even sheerer and gives the whole face a dewy finish. The second, and this is the trick I love, is to press a tiny amount of oil onto the high points of the face after the makeup is done. The tops of the cheekbones, the bridge of the nose, the center of the forehead, the little point above the lip. Just pressed in with a fingertip, not rubbed. It acts like a natural highlighter, without any glitter or shimmer.

A word of caution. Facial oils are not for everyone. If you have very oily skin, or skin that is prone to breakouts, a little goes a very long way, or you may want to skip this step entirely. And if you have sensitive skin, try any new oil on a small area first. Your dermatologist can tell you what is likely to suit your skin.

I think of a friend who lives in an old apartment overlooking the Canal Saint-Martin. She is seventy and her skin glows. Every morning, she presses one drop of oil into her cheekbones by the window, looking at the water. She says it is her little moment of light before the day begins.

Picture a woman sitting at a café table by the window in the late afternoon, the low sun coming through the glass, and her cheekbones catch the light with a soft, pearly sheen. Not shimmer. Just glow.

We are almost at the end. One last step, and it is the one that makes the whole routine last all day.

The sixth step is setting only the T-zone.

Many of us were taught to powder the entire face after foundation to set it. When you stop wearing foundation, you might wonder if you still need powder at all. Most women I know use very little, and only in one place.

Why? Because powder all over the face takes away the glow we just worked so hard to create. It sits in the fine lines and can make the skin look dry. But many of us do get a little shine on the forehead, nose, and chin through the day. That area, the T-zone, is the only place most women set.

How do they do it? With a small, fluffy brush and a finely milled translucent powder. A tiny amount, tapped off the brush first, then swept lightly over the sides of the nose, the center of the forehead, and the chin. Never under the eyes. Never on the cheeks. The cheeks keep their cream blush and their glow. During the day, if shine appears, they use a blotting paper instead of adding more powder. A single press, and the oil is gone without building up any layers.

Picture a woman stepping out of the metro at the Opéra station into the evening, ready for a concert, and her skin looks exactly as fresh as it did that morning. Soft on the cheeks, matte on the nose, glowing where the light hits. That is a T-zone-only set.

So let us bring it all together. Instead of full-coverage foundation, a tinted moisturizer pressed in with warm fingertips. Concealer only on the spots that need it, tapped at the edges. A tinted sunscreen on days when you want even less, and for daily protection. Cream blush high on the cheekbones, blended upward. A drop of facial oil, mixed into the base or pressed onto the high points. And powder only on the T-zone, with blotting papers in your bag.

It takes less than ten minutes. It costs less, because you use smaller amounts of fewer things. And it lets your skin look like your skin. Even, rested, and alive.

Let me walk you through what the whole routine actually looks like on an ordinary morning, because it is easier than it sounds when you see it all together.

I wash my face, put on my moisturizer, and while it sinks in, I make my coffee. Then I come back to the window, because I always do my face by the window now. A tinted sunscreen, or a tinted moisturizer if I have already put on a separate sunscreen. Warmed between the fingers, pressed into the center of the face. Then I look closely. Is there redness by the nose today? A little spot on the chin? A dab of concealer, tapped at the edges. A tiny bit in the inner corners of the eyes. Then the cream blush, high on the cheekbones, blended upward with two fingers. A little on the lips. Sometimes one drop of facial oil pressed onto the cheekbones. Then a few seconds with the powder brush on my nose and forehead. And I am done. I look at the clock, and it has been about seven minutes.

Compare that to my old routine. Primer, foundation with a sponge, concealer over the foundation, powder all over, bronzer, powder blush, setting spray. Twenty-five minutes on a good day, and I looked older at the end of it than when I started. I sometimes think about how many hours of my life went into that bottle.

Now, a word for those of you with particular concerns. If you have a lot of redness, or pigmentation, or scarring that you feel more comfortable covering, there is no rule that says you must go bare. You can use a slightly more pigmented tinted moisturizer, or a skin tint with a little more coverage, and keep everything else the same. You can also use a color-correcting product under the concealer, a soft green for redness or a peachy tone for darker spots, in a tiny amount just where you need it. The idea is still the same. Cover what you need to cover, and let the rest of your skin show.

And for those of you with darker skin tones, the principles are exactly the same, but the shade matters even more. Many women find that tinted products can look ashy if the shade is not right, so it is worth testing a few in daylight. Cream blush in a deep berry, a warm terracotta, or a rich plum can be extraordinary on deeper skin, and a drop of oil on the cheekbones gives the most beautiful glow.

There is one more thing that changed for me when I stopped wearing foundation, and I did not expect it. I started looking at my own face more kindly. When I wore a full mask every day, I only ever saw my bare skin in the evening, tired and red from taking off all that makeup, and I disliked it. Once I started letting my skin show, I got used to it. I began to see that the little freckles across my nose were part of me, and that the lines at the corners of my eyes came from years of laughing. That is not a small thing, at our age.

I want to say something important here. Ditching foundation does not mean you can never wear it again. Some days, for a wedding, an evening out, a photograph, you may want a little more coverage, and that is perfectly fine. The point is that it is no longer the default. It is a choice for a special day, not a mask for every day. And many women find that when they do wear foundation on those days, they choose a lighter, more luminous one than they used to, because they have learned to love the look of their own skin.

And one more thing. When you wear less makeup, skincare becomes more important. Not complicated, just consistent. A gentle cleanser, a good moisturizer, sunscreen in the daytime. And if you have particular concerns, like redness that does not go away, or changes in a spot on your skin, please see your dermatologist. Less makeup also means you will notice your skin more, which is a good thing.

I want to leave you with a little story. A few months after I stopped wearing foundation, I was sitting on a bench in the Parc Montsouris, and a little girl, maybe five years old, sat down next to me with her grandmother. She looked at me for a long time, the way children do, and then she said to her grandmother, she has pink cheeks like you. Her grandmother laughed, and so did I. Pink cheeks. That is what I had been hiding under all that foundation for twenty years.
