I am in my sixties now, and I have lived around Paris most of my life. And I have noticed that many French women my age shop for skincare this way. Not by the name on the jar, but by the ingredients in it. The pharmacy is as much a part of their beauty routine as the department store. Not because luxury creams are bad. Many women love them, and if you do, enjoy them. The first ingredient is niacinamide.

Niacinamide is a form of vitamin B3. It is one of the most common ingredients in serums today, and you will find it in many drugstore products.

Why is it popular for mature skin? It is often described as a multitasker. Niacinamide is commonly used to help support the skin's barrier, which is the outer layer that keeps moisture in and irritants out. As we age, that barrier can become weaker, which makes the skin drier and more sensitive. Niacinamide is also often chosen to help with the look of uneven tone, redness, and the appearance of enlarged pores. And it is generally considered gentle and well tolerated by many skin types, which makes it a common starting point.

How do French women use it? Usually in a light serum, applied morning or evening, or both, after cleansing and before moisturizer. A few drops pressed into the skin. Because it plays well with many other ingredients, it is often used as a base layer in a routine. As always, try it on a small area first, and if you notice irritation, stop and check with your dermatologist.

Now, if niacinamide is about supporting the skin, the second ingredient is about the thing mature skin craves most. And I think you will recognize it.

The second ingredient is hyaluronic acid.

Despite the word acid, hyaluronic acid is not an exfoliating acid. It does not peel or sting. It is a substance the body produces naturally, found in the skin, and it is known for its ability to attract and hold water.

Why is it popular for mature skin? Because as we age, skin tends to become drier, and the natural amount of hyaluronic acid in the skin decreases. Dehydrated skin looks dull, and fine lines appear more visible. Hyaluronic acid in skincare acts as a humectant. It draws moisture to the surface of the skin, which helps it look plumper, smoother, and more dewy. That plump, hydrated look reflects light beautifully, which is one of the things that makes skin look youthful.

How do French women use it? Here is a little trick my friend from the pharmacy taught me. Apply hyaluronic acid serum to slightly damp skin. Right after cleansing, pat the face mostly dry, but leave it a little moist. Then press in a few drops of the serum. Then, very importantly, follow with a moisturizer to seal it in. If you live in a very dry climate or a heated apartment in winter, sealing it with a cream is especially important, so the moisture stays in the skin.

The third ingredient is ceramides.

Ceramides are lipids, fats, that occur naturally in the skin. They are a key part of the skin's barrier. You can think of the skin's outer layer as a brick wall, with the skin cells as the bricks and lipids like ceramides as the mortar holding them together.

Why are they popular for mature skin? Because as we get older, the skin's natural ceramide levels can decline. When the mortar thins, the wall weakens. Moisture escapes more easily, and irritants get in. The result is skin that feels dry, tight, rough, and sensitive. Skincare with ceramides is often recommended to help replenish and support that barrier, so the skin holds on to moisture and feels more comfortable.

How do French women use them? Ceramides are very often found in moisturizers and creams, rather than serums, though some serums contain them too. Many drugstore moisturizers designed for dry or sensitive skin feature ceramides. French women often use a ceramide cream at night, as the last step, sealing in everything underneath. It is especially comforting in winter, when the radiators are on and the air is dry.

I remember the woman from the department store pointing at a plain white tube on a pharmacy shelf and saying, this is the mortar. Without the mortar, nothing else stays in.

Now, the fourth ingredient is probably the one you have heard the most about. And it is the one that requires the most care.

The fourth ingredient is retinol, at a low strength.

Retinol is a form of vitamin A. It belongs to a family of ingredients called retinoids. And it is one of the most researched ingredients in skincare, which is why dermatologists so often bring it up when people ask about aging skin.

Why is it popular for mature skin? Retinoids are widely used to help with the look of fine lines, uneven texture, and uneven tone, by encouraging the skin's natural renewal process. Many dermatologists consider retinoids one of the cornerstones of a routine for aging skin. Some stronger retinoids are available only by prescription, while retinol in lower strengths is commonly found in drugstore products.

But here is the important part. Retinol can be irritating, especially when you first start using it, and especially on mature or sensitive skin. It can cause dryness, redness, flaking, and sensitivity. It can make the skin more sensitive to the sun. And it is not suitable for everyone. That is why French women I know who use it are very careful.

How do they use it? They start with a low strength. They use it only at night. They begin slowly, perhaps two or three nights a week, and increase only if the skin is comfortable. They apply a small amount, about the size of a pea for the whole face, to dry skin, avoiding the area very close to the eyes and the corners of the nose and mouth. Then they follow with a moisturizer. Some women apply moisturizer first and retinol on top, to soften the effect. They never combine it with lots of other strong actives on the same night. And during the day, they always wear sunscreen.

Please, if you are thinking about starting retinol, talk to your dermatologist first. They can tell you whether it is right for your skin, which strength to start with, and whether a different type of retinoid might suit you better.

The fifth ingredient is vitamin C.

Vitamin C in skincare usually comes as ascorbic acid or one of its gentler derivatives. It is an antioxidant, which means it helps protect the skin from some of the everyday stress caused by things like sun exposure and pollution.

Why is it popular for mature skin? Vitamin C is commonly used to help brighten the look of dull skin and to help with the appearance of uneven tone and dark spots. Many people describe their skin as looking more radiant after using it regularly. Its antioxidant properties also make it a popular morning step, used under sunscreen, as an extra layer of support during the day.

How do French women use it? Usually in the morning, a few drops of a vitamin C serum after cleansing, then moisturizer, then sunscreen. A few things to know. Pure vitamin C can be unstable. It can break down when exposed to light and air, and it may turn darker in color as it does. So it is often packaged in dark or opaque bottles, and it is best stored away from heat and sunlight, with the cap closed tightly. Some forms of vitamin C can tingle or irritate sensitive skin. Gentler derivatives are available, and your dermatologist can help you choose.

Now we come to the sixth ingredient. The one I promised you at the beginning. The one women over sixty most often overlook.

The sixth ingredient is peptides.

Peptides are short chains of amino acids, which are the building blocks of proteins in the skin, such as collagen. In skincare, there are many different types of peptides, and they are often included in serums and creams designed for mature skin.

Why are they popular for mature skin? Peptides are often chosen because they are generally considered gentle, and they are commonly included in products aimed at supporting the skin's look of firmness and smoothness. For women who find retinol too irritating, or who have sensitive skin, peptides are often suggested as a gentler option to include in a routine. They tend to play well with many other ingredients and are suitable for morning or evening use.

Now, I want to be honest. Peptides are a broad family, and the research varies depending on the type of peptide. They are not miracle workers. But many women find them a comfortable, pleasant addition to a routine, especially in a nourishing serum or cream for the evening, when the skin is resting.

How do French women use them? Usually in a serum or a cream, applied after cleansing and any lighter serums, and before or as the moisturizer. They are often a good companion to hyaluronic acid and ceramides, all supporting the skin from different angles. Ask your dermatologist if you are not sure where peptides fit in your routine.

The seventh ingredient is not a serum at all. It is mineral SPF.

I saved this for last because dermatologists very often say that if you do only one thing for your skin, it should be daily sun protection. Every serum, every cream, every expensive or inexpensive product, works within the context of how much sun the skin is getting. Without sun protection, it is very hard for any other product to do its job.

Mineral sunscreens use ingredients such as zinc oxide or titanium dioxide. These are often described as sitting on the skin and reflecting or scattering the sun's rays, though they also absorb some of them. Many people with sensitive skin find mineral sunscreens gentler, which is one reason they are popular among mature women.

Why is it so important for mature skin? Because sun exposure is widely understood to contribute to many of the visible changes we associate with aging skin, such as dark spots, uneven tone, and a loss of firmness. And if you use retinol or other actives, your skin may be more sensitive to the sun, which makes protection even more important.

How do French women use it? Every morning, as the last step of skincare, applied generously to the face, the neck, and the backs of the hands. Many choose a tinted mineral sunscreen, because a slight tint helps counteract the white cast that untinted mineral sunscreens can leave, and it doubles as a light base for the skin. They reapply when they spend time outside. And they add a wide-brimmed hat and sunglasses on bright days, because sunscreen is only one part of protecting the skin.

Ask your dermatologist which type and strength of sunscreen is best for your skin and your lifestyle.

So let us bring it all together. Niacinamide, to help support the skin barrier and even out the look of tone. Hyaluronic acid on damp skin, sealed with moisturizer, for hydration and plumpness. Ceramides in a rich cream, the mortar that holds the barrier together. Retinol, at a low strength, introduced slowly, at night, with your dermatologist's guidance. Vitamin C in the morning, stored away from light, for brightness. Peptides, a gentle companion for mature and sensitive skin. And mineral SPF every single morning, the foundation of everything.

Now, please do not use all seven at once. That would be the quickest way to irritate your skin. French women build routines slowly. Here is a simple way many women start. In the morning, a gentle cleanser, a vitamin C or niacinamide serum, a moisturizer, and mineral sunscreen. In the evening, a gentle cleanser, a hyaluronic acid serum on damp skin, and a ceramide or peptide cream. Then, if your dermatologist agrees, you might add retinol a few nights a week. Introduce one new product at a time, wait a few weeks to see how your skin responds, then decide whether to add another.

Let me also share a few things about reading labels, because that is really what this whole video is about. On most skincare products, the ingredients are listed in order of concentration, from the most to the least, at least for the ingredients present in larger amounts. So if the ingredient you are looking for appears very near the end of a long list, there may be only a small amount of it. That does not always mean it will not help, because some ingredients work at low levels, but it is useful to know. Some products also state the percentage of a key ingredient on the front, which can help you compare.

The ingredient names on the back are often in their scientific form. Niacinamide is usually listed simply as niacinamide. Hyaluronic acid may appear as sodium hyaluronate, which is a related form. Ceramides often appear with letters and numbers after the word ceramide. Retinol is usually listed as retinol, though there are other retinoids with different names. Vitamin C may appear as ascorbic acid or under the name of one of its derivatives. Peptides often have long names that include the word peptide. And mineral sunscreens list zinc oxide or titanium dioxide. If you are unsure, the pharmacist is a wonderful resource. In France, pharmacists are very used to answering these questions, and I am sure many pharmacists where you live are too.

Look also at the packaging. Ingredients like vitamin C and retinol are sensitive to light and air, so opaque bottles, tubes, and pumps tend to protect them better than clear jars that you dip your fingers into. And pay attention to fragrance. Many people with sensitive mature skin find they are more comfortable with fragrance-free products, especially for anything left on the skin all night.

And remember, how you use a product matters as much as what is in it. Gentle cleansing, lukewarm water, patting instead of rubbing, applying to the neck as well as the face, consistency every day. Those habits cost nothing, and they make every ingredient work better.
