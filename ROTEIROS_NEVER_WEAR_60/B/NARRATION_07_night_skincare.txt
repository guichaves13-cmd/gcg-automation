It was nearly midnight, and I had just come home from a long dinner in a little restaurant behind the Place de la Bastille. Too much wine, too much laughing, my feet aching in my good shoes. I remember standing in my bathroom, looking at my face in the mirror under that harsh little light, mascara smudged, lipstick faded, and I thought, I will just take it all off in the morning. I pulled a makeup wipe from the packet on the shelf, swiped it once across my eyes, and went to bed.

The next morning, my skin looked like a crumpled paper bag. Dull, puffy, a red patch on my cheek, my eyes stinging. And it was that morning, oddly enough, that my neighbor across the landing, a retired nurse of about seventy with the clearest skin I have ever seen, knocked on my door to borrow some sugar. She took one look at me and said, gently, my dear, what did you do to your face last night? And then she sat at my kitchen table and told me, over coffee, exactly what she does every single night. I have followed her routine ever since.

I am in my sixties, and I have lived around Paris most of my life. And I have come to believe that what French women do at night matters far more than anything they do in the morning. The evening is when the skin rests. When it is cared for, or neglected. And there are a few habits French women over sixty simply never fall into at night.

So today I want to walk you through seven skincare mistakes French women over sixty never make at night, and exactly what they do instead. Simple steps, the type of product, and the technique. And stay with me until the sixth mistake, because it is the one I see women make most often when they start taking their skin seriously, and it can undo all the good work of the others.

Before we begin, tell me in the comments. How long does your nighttime routine take? Thirty seconds? Twenty minutes? I am curious, because I think we are all very different.

The first mistake French women never make is sleeping in makeup.

I know. We have all done it. A late night, a tired evening, and the bed seems so much more appealing than the sink. But French women over sixty are almost religious about this. No matter how late, the face gets cleaned.

Why does it matter so much? Because makeup, along with the dust, pollution, and oil the skin collects through the day, sits on the surface of the skin overnight. That layer can clog pores, irritate the skin, and leave it looking dull and congested in the morning. Mascara left on the lashes overnight can make them brittle and irritate the eyes. And for mature skin, which tends to be drier and more sensitive, that irritation can show up as redness and rough patches. Night is when the skin is meant to rest and recover, and it cannot do that well under a layer of foundation.

What do French women do instead? They cleanse in two steps. First, micellar water. This is very common in France. A gentle, water-based cleanser that you put on a soft cotton pad and press onto the skin, especially the eyes, holding it there for a few seconds so it dissolves the makeup, then gently sweeping it away. No rubbing. Then, a cleansing balm. A rich, oily balm that you massage into dry skin with your fingertips. It melts makeup, sunscreen, and the day's grime. Then you add a little water, and it turns milky, and you rinse it away. The skin feels clean but soft, never tight.

My neighbor does this in about two minutes. She says the trick is to keep the products right by the sink so there is no excuse.

Picture a softly lit bathroom in an old Parisian apartment, a window open onto the courtyard, a woman in a cotton robe gently pressing a cotton pad to her closed eyes. A moment of quiet at the end of the day.

Now, cleansing gently is one thing. But some of us, in our desire to be thorough, go much too far. And that is the second mistake.

The second mistake is harsh scrubs.

Many of us grew up believing that clean skin should feel squeaky. We scrubbed with gritty creams, rough washcloths, brushes. And we kept doing it as we got older, thinking we were removing dead skin and keeping the face fresh.

Why does it age? Because mature skin is thinner and more delicate than it used to be. Harsh scrubs with rough particles can create tiny irritations on the surface of the skin, strip away the natural oils that protect it, and weaken the skin's barrier, the outer layer that keeps moisture in and irritants out. When that barrier is damaged, the skin becomes dry, red, and sensitive. And dry, irritated skin looks older. Fine lines are more visible, the texture is rougher, and the skin loses its glow.

What French women do instead is be gentle. The cleansing balm I just mentioned does most of the work, softening and lifting away the day without any friction. If they want to exfoliate, they do it rarely and gently. Perhaps once a week or less, with a mild product, never with rough grains. And many skip it altogether, because a good cleanser and a soft washcloth, pressed rather than rubbed, is enough. If you are not sure what kind of exfoliation suits your skin, if any, this is a good question for your dermatologist.

I remember my neighbor holding up her washcloth, a very soft, old cotton one, and saying, I press, I never scrub. She had been a nurse for forty years, and she said she treated her own face the way she treated a patient's skin. With respect.

Picture a woman at her bathroom sink, pressing a warm, soft washcloth to her face and holding it there for a moment, breathing in the steam, eyes closed.

The third mistake is hot water.

This one sounds so small, but it makes a real difference. After a long day, a very hot shower or splashing the face with hot water feels wonderful. Many of us do it without thinking.

Why does it age? Because very hot water strips away the skin's natural oils very quickly. Mature skin already produces less oil than it used to, so it has less to lose. After a hot rinse, the skin can feel tight and dry, and over time, this can lead to flaking, redness, and a dull, rough texture. Very hot water can also make redness and visible little blood vessels more noticeable for people who are prone to them.

What French women do instead is a lukewarm rinse. Water that feels comfortable, neither hot nor cold, roughly the temperature of the skin itself. They rinse the cleansing balm away with lukewarm water and a soft cloth, then pat the face dry with a clean towel. Not rubbing. Patting. And here is a little detail many French women follow. They do not dry the face completely. They leave it very slightly damp, then apply their next step right away, so it helps seal in that moisture.

Imagine a woman bending over a white porcelain sink in a little bathroom with blue and white tiles, gently splashing lukewarm water on her face, then patting it with a soft folded towel. Simple, unhurried.

Now, the fourth mistake is one I think many of you will recognize, because it is the easiest habit in the world. And it is the one I made that night after the dinner.

The fourth mistake is makeup wipes.

Makeup wipes are so convenient. You keep a packet by the bed, you swipe, you are done. They are especially tempting late at night. But French women over sixty rarely use them as their only cleanser.

Why does it age? Because wipes rely on friction. You rub the wipe across the face, often several times, to remove the makeup. That rubbing pulls and drags at the skin, especially around the delicate eyes. Mature skin, which has lost some of its elasticity, does not need that pulling night after night. And wipes often do not remove everything. They move the makeup around, leaving a thin film behind that can irritate and dull the skin by morning. Some wipes also contain fragrance or other ingredients that sensitive skin may not love, and since you do not rinse, those ingredients stay on the skin all night.

What French women do instead is go back to that two-step cleanse. Micellar water on a cotton pad, held and pressed rather than rubbed, and then a cleansing balm massaged in and rinsed off. If they are traveling or truly exhausted, they might use a wipe as an emergency, but they always follow with a rinse or at least a swipe of micellar water, and never make it a habit.

That morning, sitting at my kitchen table, my neighbor said to me, a wipe is a picnic napkin. It is fine for a picnic. It is not how you wash your face.

Picture a small round marble table by a window, a cup of herbal tea, a cotton pad and a bottle of micellar water next to a little jar of balm. The ritual of a woman who takes a few minutes for herself.

The fifth mistake is skipping the neck.

So many of us take wonderful care of our faces and then stop at the jawline, as if the face ended there. Cleanser, serum, cream, all on the face. The neck gets nothing.

Why does it age? Because the skin of the neck is thinner than the skin of the face and has fewer oil glands. It is often one of the first places to show dryness and fine lines. And it is right next to the face. If your face is well moisturized and glowing, and your neck is dry and crepey, the contrast makes both look older. It is also often exposed to the sun without protection.

What French women do instead is treat the neck as part of the face. Everything that goes on the face, the cleanser and the cream, goes on the neck too. And they apply it in a particular way. Upward strokes. They take the cream in their palms and sweep it gently up the neck, from the collarbone toward the jaw, one hand after the other. Never pulling downward. It is a gentle, almost meditative motion, and it takes only a few seconds. They also apply it to the décolleté, the upper chest, which is often exposed in summer.

My neighbor showed me this right at the kitchen table, sweeping her hands up her neck like a dancer warming up. She said her mother had taught her, and her grandmother had taught her mother.

Picture a woman sitting at a small vanity by a tall window, the lights of Paris outside, sweeping cream up her neck with slow, upward strokes, the lamplight catching her face.

Now we come to the sixth mistake, the one I promised you at the beginning. The one that undoes the rest.

The sixth mistake is too many actives.

When we start taking our skin seriously, especially after sixty, it is very tempting to do everything at once. We read about ingredients like retinoids, vitamin C, exfoliating acids. We buy a serum with one, a cream with another, a toner with a third, and we layer them all every night. More, we think, must be better.

Why does it age? Because many of these active ingredients are powerful, and mature skin is often more sensitive. Using several strong actives together, or using them too often, can overwhelm the skin. Some combinations are particularly likely to cause irritation. The result can be redness, peeling, a stinging feeling, a damaged skin barrier. The skin becomes dry and inflamed, and the very lines and dullness we wanted to improve can look worse.

What French women do instead is one active at a time. They choose one, perhaps a gentle retinoid, which many dermatologists recommend for mature skin, and they introduce it slowly. A few nights a week at first, then more as the skin gets used to it. On the other nights, they simply moisturize. If they want to add another active later, they wait until the skin is completely comfortable, and they ask their dermatologist which combinations make sense. Some women use different actives on different nights, rather than layering them. The point is restraint. Let the skin adjust. Let it tell you how it feels.

And please, if you are thinking about starting a retinoid or any strong active, talk to your dermatologist first, especially if you have sensitive skin, rosacea, or take any medication that might affect your skin.

Then, after the active, or on nights without one, the most important step of all. A rich cream. Mature skin, especially at night, loves a rich, nourishing moisturizer. Something with a thicker, creamier texture than the light lotions we might use in the morning. It helps support the skin's barrier, keeps it hydrated through the night, and softens the look of fine lines by morning. French women apply it generously, warming it between the palms first and pressing it into the skin rather than rubbing.

Imagine a woman sitting on the edge of her bed in a quiet apartment, the shutters half open onto a moonlit street, warming a spoonful of rich cream between her palms and pressing it gently onto her cheeks.

The seventh mistake is a cotton pillowcase.

This one surprised me when I first heard it. Of all the things to worry about, a pillowcase? But French women I know are very particular about it.

Why does it matter? Because we spend many hours each night with our face pressed against the pillow. Cotton is a slightly rough, absorbent fabric. It creates friction against the skin as we turn in our sleep, which can leave creases on the face in the morning, especially on mature skin, which bounces back more slowly. Some people find that these sleep creases become more noticeable over time. Cotton can also absorb some of the cream we so carefully applied, pulling it away from the skin and into the fabric.

What French women do instead is sleep on a silk pillowcase. Silk is smooth and slippery, so the face glides over it rather than being creased by it. It is gentler on the skin and on the hair too, which is why I mentioned it in another video about gray hair. Some women prefer satin, which has a similar smooth feel. The face wakes up looking a little smoother, the hair a little less tangled, and the cream stays on the skin where it belongs.

And here is the final part of my neighbor's ritual, the one she does just before the pillowcase. The eye cream, tapped. She takes a tiny amount of eye cream, about the size of a grain of rice, and dots it under each eye along the bone, never too close to the lashes. Then, with the ring finger, the weakest finger, she taps it in gently, like little raindrops. Never rubbing, never pulling. The skin around the eyes is the thinnest on the face, and it deserves the lightest touch.

Picture a woman lying back on a silk pillowcase in a soft gray bedroom, the window open to the sound of the street below, her face calm and clean, her eyes closed. That is the end of a French evening routine.

So let us bring it all together. Instead of sleeping in makeup, micellar water and a cleansing balm. Instead of harsh scrubs, gentle pressing with a soft cloth, and exfoliation rarely, if at all. Instead of hot water, a lukewarm rinse, patting dry, and leaving the skin slightly damp. Instead of makeup wipes, that two-step cleanse, every night. Instead of skipping the neck, upward strokes from the collarbone to the jaw. Instead of too many actives, one at a time, introduced slowly, followed by a rich cream. And instead of a cotton pillowcase, silk, with eye cream tapped in gently before sleep.

Now, let me show you how this looks on an ordinary evening, because I promise it is not as long as it sounds. Micellar water on the eyes and lips, about thirty seconds. Cleansing balm, massaged in for a minute, rinsed with lukewarm water. Pat dry, leaving the skin slightly damp. On retinoid nights, a small amount of the active, waiting a moment. Then rich cream on the face and neck, upward strokes. Eye cream, tapped. Silk pillowcase. Lights out. About five minutes in all, and every minute of it is time for yourself.

Let me add a few small details that make this routine easier to keep. First, set the scene. French women I know treat the evening routine as a little ritual, not a chore. A soft lamp instead of the harsh overhead light. A clean, folded towel. The products lined up in the order you use them. My neighbor keeps a small radio on the shelf and listens to the classical station while she cleanses. It sounds like nothing, but when the routine feels pleasant, you are far more likely to do it every night.

Second, do it earlier. Many women do their skincare right after dinner, rather than at the very end of the evening when they are exhausted. Then the skin is clean and the cream has time to sink in before the face touches the pillow. And on the nights when you are out late, you have no excuse to skip it, because you know it only takes five minutes.

Third, wash your pillowcase often. Silk or cotton, a pillowcase collects oil, cream, and hair products, and a fresh one is kinder to the skin. Many French women I know change theirs twice a week.

Fourth, keep a glass of water by the bed. It will not replace a good cream, but staying comfortably hydrated is part of feeling well, and a dry bedroom, especially in winter with the radiators on, can make the skin feel tight by morning. Some women also put a small bowl of water near the radiator, an old trick to add a little moisture to the air.

And fifth, listen to your skin. Some nights it wants more cream. Some nights, after a day in the cold wind, it wants nothing but a gentle cleanse and a rich moisturizer, and no active at all. French women adjust their routine by feel, not by a rigid schedule.

What strikes me about the French approach to night skincare is how calm it is. It is not about fighting the skin or stripping it or attacking every line. It is about protecting it, nourishing it, and letting it rest. The skin does a great deal of its repair work while we sleep. Our job is simply not to get in the way, and to give it a little comfort.

I want to say a word about consistency. None of these steps work miracles in one night. But done every evening, for weeks and months, they add up. My neighbor has done this routine for decades, and she says the secret is not any particular product. The secret is doing it every night, even when you are tired, even after a dinner with too much wine. Especially then.

And a word about care. Everyone's skin is different. If you have a skin condition, very sensitive skin, or you are not sure which products suit you, a dermatologist can help you build a routine that works for you. And if you notice any change in a mole or a spot, or a patch that will not heal, please see a doctor. Taking care of your skin at night is a wonderful time to notice those things.
