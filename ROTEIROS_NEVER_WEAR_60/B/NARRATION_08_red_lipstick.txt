The first time I saw a woman over seventy wear red lipstick perfectly, I was twenty-three and working as a shop girl in a small glove boutique off the Rue Saint-Honoré. She came in every Thursday at eleven. Silver hair in a low twist, a gray wool coat, a pair of tortoiseshell glasses on a chain. Almost no makeup at all. And a red mouth. Not a dark, heavy red, not a glossy one. A clear, bright, soft red, slightly blurred at the edges, like a poppy. She would try on gloves, choose a pair, pay in cash, and leave. And every Thursday, I would stand by the window and watch her walk away down the street, because I wanted to be her when I grew old.

Now I am in my sixties, and I have lived around Paris most of my life. And I have finally learned what that woman knew. Red lipstick after sixty is not difficult. It is not too much, not too bold, not too young. It only looks harsh when we make a few particular mistakes. Fix those, and red becomes the most elegant thing you can wear.

So today, I want to show you how French women over sixty wear red lipstick, and the five mistakes that make it look harsh. For each one, I will tell you exactly what they do instead. The shade, the formula, the technique. And stay with me until the fourth mistake, because it is the one that turns red lipstick from elegant to overdone in seconds, and the fix is something you take away, not something you add.

Before we start, tell me in the comments. Do you wear red lipstick? Always, sometimes, never, or you would love to but you are afraid? I would really like to know.

First, a quick word about why red is worth the trouble. A red lip brings color to the whole face. As we get older, the skin and lips naturally lose some of their color, and a well-chosen red puts it back. It makes the eyes look brighter and the skin look clearer. It draws attention to the mouth, which is where we smile and speak, and that makes a face look lively and engaged. And there is something about red that changes how you carry yourself. You stand a little straighter. That is not a fact you will find in any study. It is just something every woman who wears red seems to know.

But red is also unforgiving. When it is wrong, it is very wrong. So let us go through the mistakes.

The first mistake is a red that is too dark.

Many women, when they decide to try red after sixty, choose a deep red. A wine, a burgundy, an oxblood, a very dark brick. It feels safer, more sophisticated, more appropriate for our age.

Why does it look harsh? Because dark colors make the lips look smaller. As we age, the lips naturally become thinner, and a very dark red shrinks them further. It also creates a strong contrast against mature skin, which tends to be lighter and softer in tone than it once was. That contrast draws a hard outline around the mouth, emphasizing every fine line and any unevenness. And dark reds show wear very clearly. As the color fades in the center through the day, the edges stay dark, and you end up with a ring.

What do French women wear instead? A blue-red in a satin finish. A true, clear red, with a slightly cool, blue undertone, rather than a dark red or an orange red. Blue-based reds tend to make the teeth look whiter, which is a lovely bonus after sixty. They brighten the skin and make the face look fresh. And a satin finish, with its soft sheen, reflects light, which makes the lips look fuller and smoother.

How do they find the right shade? They try it in daylight. A blue-red often looks quite bright in the tube, almost alarmingly so. But on the lips, in natural light, it softens beautifully. If a clear blue-red feels like too much, many women choose a slightly softer version, a raspberry red or a cherry red, still cool in tone, but a little gentler.

Picture a woman walking out of the doors of the Comédie-Française into the evening, the lights of the theater behind her, and her lips are a clear, bright red that seems to make her whole face glow.

Now, getting the shade right is half the battle. But even the perfect red will look harsh if the formula is wrong. And that is the second mistake.

The second mistake is a dry matte red.

Matte red lipstick is everywhere. Liquid mattes, long-wear mattes, velvet mattes that promise to last all day. And on a younger face, a matte red can look striking.

Why does it look harsh on a mature face? Because of texture. Matte formulas dry down to a completely flat finish, and many of them are drying on the lips. Mature lips are often already drier and have more fine vertical lines. A dry matte red settles into those lines and emphasizes them. By afternoon, it can look cracked, flaky, and patchy. And because a matte surface reflects no light, the lips look flatter and thinner. The overall effect is a red that looks hard and painted, rather than soft and lived in.

What French women do instead starts before the lipstick. A lip balm base. They apply a thin layer of a good lip balm and let it sink in for a minute or two while they do something else. Put on their earrings, find their keys. Then they blot away any excess with a tissue, so the lips are smooth and hydrated but not slippery. Then they apply the satin red on top. The balm fills in the fine lines a little and gives the lipstick a smooth surface to sit on. It glides on more evenly, it looks more comfortable, and it wears off gracefully.

If they love the look of a softer finish, they press a tissue lightly over the lipstick afterward. That takes away a little of the shine and makes it look almost velvety, without the dryness of a true matte. More on that technique in a moment, because it is the last secret.

I remember a woman at the counter of a pharmacy near the Place des Ternes, applying her balm while she waited for her prescription, then her red, all without a mirror. She caught me watching and said, with a little smile, the balm does all the work. The red just comes along.

Picture a woman sitting at a café table near the Opéra Garnier, unscrewing a little tin of balm, pressing it onto her lips with her fingertip, then turning her face to the window as she applies her red.

The third mistake is no liner, and bleeding.

This is the mistake that worries most women about red. And it is a real concern. You put on a beautiful red, and within an hour, it has started to travel. Tiny threads of red have crept into the fine lines above and below the lips. The edge of the mouth looks blurry and smudged.

Why does it happen? As we age, those small vertical lines around the mouth can act like little channels. Creamy lipsticks, especially richly pigmented ones like red, can slowly move into them as the day goes on. Because red is such a strong color, even a tiny amount of bleeding is very visible. And a smudged red edge looks careless and harsh.

What French women do instead is use a lip liner. But not a red one, and not a dark one. A nude liner. Or a liner that closely matches the natural color of their lips. This is the trick that surprises most people. You might think you need a red liner to go with a red lipstick. But a red liner, if it is even slightly off, creates a visible line. A nude liner, close to the color of your lips, is almost invisible. It creates a soft barrier at the edge of the lips that helps keep the red from traveling, without showing.

How do they apply it? Very lightly, along the natural lip line, not outside it. They pay special attention to the cupid's bow, the dip in the center of the upper lip, and the outer corners, which are where red tends to travel first. Then they soften the line with a fingertip, so there is no hard edge. Some women also fill in the lips lightly with the liner, which gives the lipstick something to grip and helps it last.

There are also clear or invisible lip liners that create a barrier without any color, and some women love those. Either way, the goal is the same. A clean edge that holds the red in place.

Picture a woman at a small mirror in the hallway of her apartment in the sixteenth arrondissement, gloves and handbag on the console table, carefully drawing a fine nude line along her lips before she goes out. A small, precise ritual.

Now we come to the fourth mistake. The one I promised you at the beginning. The one where the fix is taking something away.

The fourth mistake is red lips with heavy eyes.

Many of us, when we decide to wear red lipstick, think it is a special occasion. So we do everything. Dark eyeliner, smoky shadow, heavy mascara, maybe some shimmer. A full face.

Why does it look harsh? Because red lips are already a statement. When you pair a strong lip with strong eyes, the two compete for attention, and the face looks heavily made up. On a mature face, heavy eye makeup can also emphasize hooded lids and fine lines around the eyes, and it can make the eyes look smaller and more tired. Combined with a red lip, the overall effect can look dated and theatrical, rather than elegant. It is too much makeup, and too much makeup is one of the things that makes a mature woman look older.

What French women do instead is bare eyes. Or almost bare. When the lips are red, the eyes are quiet. A little mascara on the upper lashes, perhaps a soft brown pencil pressed into the lash line and smudged. A brush of brow gel. That is all. No dark liner on the lower lash line, no smoky shadow, no shimmer. The skin is kept simple too. A light tinted moisturizer or skin tint, a little concealer where needed, perhaps a touch of cream blush. The red lip becomes the only accent on the face.

The effect is completely different. The face looks clean and fresh, and the red lip looks confident and intentional, like a single red flower in a vase. That woman in the glove shop, now that I think back, wore exactly this. Almost nothing on her eyes, and a perfect red mouth.

Picture a woman sitting on a bench in the Square du Temple on a crisp autumn morning, a camel coat, a gray cashmere scarf, eyes nearly bare behind her glasses, and a red lip that is the only splash of color in the whole scene, besides the falling leaves.

The fifth mistake is the wrong undertone.

Red is not one color. There are hundreds of reds, and they lean in different directions. Some are warm, leaning orange, tomato, or brick. Some are cool, leaning blue or pink. And some are in between.

Why does the wrong undertone look harsh? Because when the undertone of the red fights the undertone of your skin, it can make the skin look sallow, gray, or red and blotchy. An orange-red on very cool-toned skin can look brassy and make the skin look yellowish. A very cool blue-red on very warm, golden skin can sometimes look slightly purple or harsh. And if your hair has gone silver or white, the undertone matters even more, because your whole coloring has shifted.

What French women do instead is choose carefully, and then soften. For many women over sixty, especially those with silver hair, a blue-red is the most flattering, as I mentioned at the start. But for women with warmer skin, a true red, balanced between warm and cool, or a slightly warmer red can be lovely. The best way to find yours is to try a few in daylight, side by side, and see which makes your teeth look whiter and your skin look brighter.

And then comes the final technique, the one that turns any red into something soft and wearable. The blot technique. After applying the lipstick over your balm base and nude liner, take a single tissue. Separate it into its thin layers, and place one layer over your lips. Press your lips together gently into the tissue. Then lift it away. This removes a little of the excess color and shine. Then, if you want, apply one more thin layer of lipstick, and blot once more. Some women even dust a tiny amount of translucent powder through the tissue before the second layer, to help the color last longer.

The result is a red that looks like it has sunk into the lips. It is softer, more natural, and lasts much longer. It looks lived in, not freshly painted. And this is how French women seem to wear red. Never perfect, never glossy, always looking as if they put it on an hour ago and forgot about it.

Picture a woman at a corner table of a small bistro in the Latin Quarter, pressing a folded tissue gently between her lips, then tucking it back into her handbag. She looks up and smiles, and her red is soft, blurred, and perfect.

So let us bring it all together. Instead of a red that is too dark, a blue-red in a satin finish. Instead of a dry matte red, a lip balm base, then satin red on top. Instead of no liner and bleeding, a nude liner along the natural lip line, softened with a fingertip. Instead of red lips with heavy eyes, bare or nearly bare eyes, with just mascara and brow gel. And instead of the wrong undertone, a red tested in daylight, finished with the blot technique so it looks soft and lived in.

Here is the whole routine, start to finish. Balm on the lips, left for a minute, then blotted. A light skin tint and a dab of concealer. Brow gel. A coat of mascara. Nude liner along the lip line and the corners, softened. Satin red, applied from the center outward. A tissue pressed between the lips. Perhaps a second thin layer, and another blot. That is it. Five minutes, maybe less. And you will look like that woman in the glove shop, walking down the Rue Saint-Honoré on a Thursday morning.

Now, some practical notes. Carry the red with you. Even with a liner and the blot technique, red wears off when you eat and drink. French women reapply discreetly, usually after a meal, with a quick blot first. And they carry a small mirror, because red needs a little precision.

Keep your lips healthy. A balm at night and in the morning makes every red look better. If your lips are very dry or cracked, red will highlight that, so give them a few days of care before a big occasion. And if your lips spend time in the sun, think about protection for them too, and ask your dermatologist or pharmacist what they recommend.

Think about your teeth. Red draws attention to the mouth, and to the smile. Many people find a blue-red makes the teeth look brighter. And here is a small trick. After applying red, put your index finger in your mouth, close your lips around it, and pull it out. Any lipstick on the inside of the lips, the part that might transfer to your teeth, comes off on your finger. It looks a little silly, but it works, and I have seen elegant Parisian women do it in café restrooms more than once.

Let me add a word about wearing red every day versus for occasions. Many of us keep red for weddings and dinners, and that is lovely. But some of the most elegant women I know in Paris wear red on an ordinary Tuesday, to the market, to the post office, with jeans and a striped sweater. It becomes part of who they are, rather than a costume. If you feel shy about it, try wearing your red to somewhere small first. The bakery. The pharmacy. Let people see it on a normal day. You will find that nobody stares. They just smile a little more.

Let me also talk about what to wear with red, because the lipstick does not live alone on the face. French women tend to pair red lips with very simple clothes. A navy sweater. A white shirt. A black dress. A camel coat. A striped top. Neutral colors let the red be the accent. When the outfit is very busy, with prints and bright colors, a red lip can start to compete, and the whole look becomes noisy. If you love color in your clothes, you can still wear red lipstick, but choose one thing to be the star. On the days you wear a bright scarf, perhaps a softer lip. On the days the lips are red, perhaps a quieter scarf.

Jewelry matters too. Gold earrings with a warm red, silver or pearls with a blue-red. A pair of simple gold hoops and a red lip is one of the most classic French combinations I know. It frames the face and pulls everything together.

And glasses. Many of us wear glasses now, and a red lip is a wonderful balance to a strong frame. Tortoiseshell, black, or a deep navy frame with a red lip looks extraordinarily chic. The glasses frame the eyes, so the eyes need even less makeup, and the red lip balances the face below.

Finally, think about the season. In summer, a slightly brighter, fresher red, perhaps a cherry or a poppy, with a lighter balm base, looks lovely with linen and a little tan. In winter, a classic blue-red with a richer balm underneath stands up beautifully against gray skies, dark wool, and cold cheeks. Some women keep two reds, one for each half of the year. That is all you need.

And if you are still nervous, here is my gentlest suggestion. Start with a sheer red. A red tinted balm or a sheer red lipstick, tapped on with a finger. It gives you the color and the feeling of red, but in a soft, forgiving way. Wear it for a few weeks. Get used to seeing yourself with a red mouth. Then, when you are ready, move to a satin red with a liner. Many women who say they cannot wear red simply have never let themselves get used to it.

And finally, a word about confidence. The thing that makes red lipstick look elegant, more than any shade or technique, is that you wear it as if it belongs to you. Not as a disguise or a special effect, but as part of who you are. That woman in the glove shop never once looked in the mirror after trying on gloves. She did not need to. She knew exactly how she looked.
