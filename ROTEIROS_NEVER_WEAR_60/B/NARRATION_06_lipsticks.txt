My mother kept her lipsticks in a little blue enamel dish on the shelf above the sink in our apartment in Vincennes. When I was a girl, I would sneak in and line them all up on the edge of the bath, opening them one by one. And I remember one in particular. A deep, frosted, pearly brown, with a shimmer like the inside of a seashell. She wore it for years. When I was about sixty, cleaning out a drawer, I found an almost identical shade I had bought myself, without even realizing I was copying her. I put it on, looked in the mirror, and for a moment I did not see myself. I saw my mother in her seventies, looking tired and a little sad.

I put it in the bin. Then I went to a little perfumery near the Rue de Rennes and asked the woman behind the counter, who must have been sixty herself, what she would wear. She handed me a sheer rosewood balm. I put it on in front of her mirror, and I looked like I had slept for a week.

I am in my sixties, and I have lived around Paris most of my life. And lipstick, more than any other product, has taught me how much a small choice can change a face. French women over sixty have very particular feelings about lipstick. There are a handful of formulas and shades they almost never wear, and a few they reach for again and again.

So today, I want to walk you through five lipsticks French women over sixty never wear, and exactly what they wear instead. The formula, the shade, the way they put it on. And stay with me until the fourth one, because it is the one almost every woman thinks is the safe choice, and it is actually one of the most aging lipsticks you can wear.

Before we start, tell me in the comments. What is the lipstick shade you have worn the longest? I suspect some of you have been loyal to one color for decades. I would love to hear it.

First, a quick word about why lips change as we age, because it explains everything that follows.

As we get older, the lips naturally lose a little volume and become thinner. The natural color of the lips often fades too, becoming paler. The edges of the lips, the little border we call the lip line, can become less defined. And fine vertical lines often appear around the mouth. None of this is a flaw. It is simply what happens. But it means that a lipstick that looked beautiful on us at thirty can behave very differently at sixty-five. Some formulas fight these changes. Others work with them.

The first lipstick French women never wear is matte long-wear.

You know these. The liquid lipsticks or very matte formulas that promise to stay on all day, through coffee and lunch and kisses. They dry down to a completely flat finish, and they often feel tight on the lips.

Why does it age? Because of texture and light. Matte long-wear formulas are designed to dry and set. On mature lips, which are often drier and have more fine lines, that drying finish emphasizes every crease. The color settles into the vertical lines and looks cracked by the afternoon. And because a completely matte surface reflects no light, the lips look flat, which makes them seem thinner. Then there is the feather effect. Some long-wear formulas can travel into the fine lines around the mouth, especially as they wear off, leaving a blurry edge.

What do French women wear instead? A satin red, or a satin finish in general. Satin lipstick has a soft, creamy texture with a gentle sheen. Not glossy, not matte, somewhere in between, like the surface of a silk ribbon. It glides onto the lips, it stays comfortable, and that slight sheen reflects light, which makes the lips look fuller and smoother. And when French women want something bold, they often choose a satin red with a slightly blue undertone. Blue-based reds tend to make the teeth look whiter and the skin brighter, and the satin finish keeps it soft rather than harsh.

How do they apply it? Over a thin layer of lip balm, blotted for a minute so it is not greasy. Then the satin lipstick, straight from the bullet, pressed on rather than dragged. Then a tissue, pressed lightly between the lips, to remove any excess. The color sinks in and looks part of the lips, not painted on top.

Picture a woman at the bar of a small bistro near the Place Saint-Michel, raising a glass of red wine, and her lips are a soft, satin red that seems to glow under the warm lights. Elegant without trying.

Now, if matte makes the lips look flat, the second one does the opposite. It makes them look too much. And I know many of you loved this one, because I did too.

The second lipstick French women never wear is frosted pearl.

This was my mother's shade. Frosted, pearlescent lipsticks with a shimmering metallic finish. Pale pinks, peachy beiges, shimmering browns. They were enormously popular for a long time, and they still turn up.

Why does it age? Because frost and lines are not friends. The large shimmering particles in a frosted lipstick sit on the surface of the lips and catch the light. Wherever there is a fine line or a dry patch, the frost catches it and highlights it. So instead of looking soft and full, the lips look dry, cracked, and textured. And frosted shades are often pale, cool, and slightly gray in tone, which can make mature skin look washed out and the teeth look more yellow by contrast. There is also something immediately dated about frost. The eye reads it as a look from another decade, and that brings the whole face along with it.

What French women wear instead is a rosewood tinted balm. Rosewood is a soft, muted pink-brown, the color of old roses, or a dusty mauve. It is incredibly flattering on mature women, because it is close to the natural color of the lips, just a little deeper and warmer. And in a tinted balm formula, it is sheer and moisturizing. It gives the lips a soft, healthy color with a gentle natural shine, no shimmer at all.

How do they apply it? Straight from the tube, or with the fingertip, pressed onto the lips. Because it is sheer, it is forgiving. No mirror needed. You can put it on in the street. And a tinted balm keeps the lips hydrated all day, which is exactly what mature lips need.

Imagine a woman walking past the stalls at the Marché des Enfants Rouges, stopping to taste a piece of fruit, and her lips have that soft rosewood color that looks completely natural, as if she had just bitten into a raspberry.

Before we go on, I would love to know. Did you wear frosted lipstick back in the day? Tell me in the comments. I have a feeling many of us did, and I would love to hear your favorite shade from those years.

The third lipstick French women never wear is a dark brown-plum.

Deep, dark lipsticks in brown, plum, aubergine, or a very deep wine color. Some women wear these because they feel sophisticated, or because they were fashionable at some point, or because they seem to match a darker hair color.

Why does it age? Because of contrast and size. As the lips get thinner, a very dark color makes them look even smaller. Dark colors recede visually, so the mouth seems to shrink. And the high contrast between a very dark lip and the lighter mature skin around it draws a hard outline around the mouth, highlighting any fine lines and any unevenness in the lip line. A dark lipstick also shows every imperfection in application. A little smudge, a slightly uneven edge, and it is visible from across the room. And as the day goes on, dark colors tend to wear off in the center first and leave a darker ring at the edges, which is very unflattering.

What French women wear instead is a berry stain. A sheer, juicy berry color. Think raspberry, blackberry, a soft cranberry. It gives the depth and richness of a darker lip without the heaviness. A stain sinks into the lips rather than sitting on top, so it wears off evenly and naturally, leaving a soft wash of color all day.

How do they apply it? With a fingertip, tapped onto the center of the lips and blended outward toward the edges. Building up the color slowly, one layer at a time, until it is as deep as they want. The edges stay soft and blurred. That softness is what makes it so flattering. Then a touch of balm on top if the lips feel dry.

Picture a woman stepping out of a cinema on the Champs-Élysées on an autumn evening, the lights of the avenue behind her, and her lips are a soft, deep berry, like she has just had a glass of wine. Rich, but soft.

I will tell you a small secret about berry. It is the shade I reach for when I feel a little gray myself. A rainy November day, a doctor's appointment, a morning when I did not sleep well. A berry stain, tapped on in thirty seconds, and suddenly the face looks warm and awake again. A friend of mine who lives near the Gare de Lyon calls it her courage color. She keeps one in every coat pocket, and I have never once seen her without it in winter. And because a stain fades so evenly, she never has to worry about checking a mirror. It simply softens through the day, like the color of the lips after a cup of tea.

If you have always loved dark lipsticks and feel naked without them, a berry stain is the perfect bridge. You keep the depth and the drama you love, but you lose the hard edge and the shrinking effect. Try it for a week, and look at your face in photographs, especially the ones taken outdoors in natural light. I think you will be surprised by how much softer and warmer you look.

Now we come to the fourth one, the one I promised you at the beginning. The lipstick almost everyone thinks is the safe choice.

The fourth lipstick French women never wear is a nude that matches the skin.

This is so common. Many women, as they get older, decide to tone things down. They choose a nude lipstick. Beige, pale peach, sand, the color of the skin itself. It feels safe, natural, appropriate.

Why does it age? Because remember what happens to the lips as we get older. They lose color and become paler. If you put on a nude lipstick that is the same color as your skin, you erase the lips entirely. The mouth disappears into the face. And a face without a defined mouth looks tired, pale, and even unwell. Very pale nudes, especially the ones with a slightly gray or chalky tone, can also make the teeth look yellow and the skin look sallow. It is the lipstick equivalent of a beige cardigan that washes you out. Safe, but at a cost.

What French women wear instead is what some people call an MLBB pink. That stands for my lips but better. It is a pink or rosy shade that matches the natural color of your lips when they are at their best. When you are young, healthy, maybe just after a brisk walk in the cold. It is usually one or two shades deeper and rosier than your lips are now. Not a nude, not a bright pink. Your own color, restored.

How do they find it? Here is a little trick a friend taught me. Gently press your lips together, or press a finger on your lower lip for a moment and release. The flush of color you see as the blood comes back is close to your MLBB shade. Look for a lipstick, balm, or tint that matches that. For fair skin, it is often a soft rose pink. For medium skin, a warmer rosy mauve. For deeper skin, a deep rose or berry brown. A satin or sheer formula, never matte.

The effect is extraordinary. People will not notice that you are wearing lipstick. They will just think you look healthy and well.

Imagine a woman standing in front of a mirror in a small boutique on the Rue de Grenelle, trying on a coat, and she smiles at herself, and her lips are a soft, natural rose. She looks like the best version of herself.

Before the last one, I want to ask. Do you wear nude lipstick? Have you noticed how it makes your face look? Tell me in the comments. I am curious how many of you have been trapped in the safe nude, the way I was for a while.

The fifth lipstick French women never wear is glitter gloss.

Glossy lip products full of glitter and shimmer, often in pale pinks or clear finishes. Sticky, sparkly, very shiny.

Why does it age? Because glitter on mature lips does the same thing frost does. The sparkles catch every fine line and dry patch. And thick, sticky gloss tends to travel, especially into the vertical lines around the mouth, so within an hour the gloss is outside the lips, making the edge look blurry and messy. Glitter also reads as very young. On a teenager, it looks playful. On a mature woman, the contrast can look like trying too hard, which makes everything else look older by comparison.

What French women wear instead is a coral sheer. A soft, sheer coral or peachy-coral, in a balm or a light lipstick formula. Coral is a warm, bright color that brings life to the face, especially in spring and summer. A sheer formula gives a fresh, slightly glossy finish without the stickiness or sparkle. It brightens the skin and makes the face look sunny and rested.

Who does it suit? Coral is wonderful on women with warm undertones, but a soft, pinkish coral works on almost everyone. If you are cooler toned, choose a coral that leans more pink. If you are warmer, a coral that leans more peach.

How do they apply it? Straight from the bullet or with a fingertip, one layer, pressed on. Because it is sheer, it is easy and forgiving. If they want more shine, a tiny dab of clear balm in the center of the lower lip only, which catches the light and makes the lips look fuller without any glitter.

Picture a woman on a terrace at a café near the Jardin du Luxembourg in early summer, wearing a white linen shirt, laughing in the sun, and her lips are a fresh, soft coral that matches the geraniums on the windowsill behind her.

So let us bring it all together. Instead of matte long-wear, a satin finish, and for a bold moment, a satin blue-red. Instead of frosted pearl, a sheer rosewood tinted balm. Instead of a dark brown-plum, a soft berry stain, tapped on and blurred at the edges. Instead of a nude that matches your skin, your MLBB pink, the color of your own lips at their best. And instead of glitter gloss, a fresh coral sheer, with a dab of balm in the center for shine.

What is the common thread? Every one of the lipsticks French women avoid either flattens the lips, highlights the lines, or creates harsh contrast. Every one they choose instead is soft, moisturizing, and brings a little warmth and life back to the face. The goal is never to look painted. It is to look like you have color in your cheeks and lips because you are well and happy and just came in from a walk.

Let me tell you how these five lipsticks fit into a real week, because French women rarely own dozens. Most of the women I know in Paris have perhaps four or five lip products in their handbag drawer, and they rotate them according to the day, the season, and their mood.

On an ordinary weekday, going to the market or meeting a friend for coffee, it is usually the MLBB pink or the rosewood balm. Easy, sheer, no mirror needed. In summer, the coral sheer comes out, with linen and straw and bare shoulders. In autumn and winter, the berry stain, which looks so lovely with camel coats and gray scarves and the first cold mornings. And for an evening, a dinner, the opera, a wedding, the satin red. That is the whole wardrobe. Five products, each one doing a very particular job.

And here is something else I have noticed. French women match their lipstick to their whole face, not to their clothes. Many of us were taught to match the lipstick to a blouse or a dress. But what really matters is how the color sits with your skin, your hair, and your eyes. If your hair has gone silver, cooler roses and blue-reds often come alive. If your hair is a warm honey or bronde, corals and warm berries can be beautiful. When your hair color changes, it is worth trying your lipsticks again, because the ones that suited you before may not be the ones that suit you now.

Then there is the question of the eyes and the cheeks. A soft lip goes with almost anything. But the stronger the lip, the quieter everything else should be. With a berry stain or a satin red, French women usually keep the eyes very simple. A little mascara, perhaps a soft brown pencil smudged at the lash line, and nothing else. And they often use a touch of the same lip color on the cheeks, tapped on with a finger, so the face looks harmonious. That is one of my favorite little tricks. One color, two places, and the face looks finished.

Finally, a word about how to wear lipstick through the day. Mature lips can get dry, and lipstick that was perfect at nine in the morning can look tired by three. Rather than adding a fresh thick layer on top of what is left, French women usually blot off the old color with a tissue, smooth on a little balm, wait a moment, and then reapply. It takes thirty seconds in a café restroom, and the lips look freshly done instead of layered.

A few practical notes before I go. First, take care of the lips underneath. A good balm at night, and in the morning before lipstick, makes every color look better. If the lips are very dry, gently remove flakes with a soft washcloth after a shower, never with a harsh scrub.

Second, think about protection. Lips can burn in the sun like the rest of the skin. Some balms include sun protection, and it is worth asking your dermatologist or pharmacist what is suitable for you, especially if you spend a lot of time outdoors.

Third, a lip liner can help, but choose wisely. A liner that matches your lip color, not darker, used to softly define the edge and fill in the corners, can keep lipstick from traveling into fine lines. Draw it lightly, then blend it inward with a fingertip so there is no visible line.

And finally, try colors in daylight. Lipstick looks completely different under shop lights. If you can, put a little on the back of your hand or your fingertip and look at it by a window before you decide.
