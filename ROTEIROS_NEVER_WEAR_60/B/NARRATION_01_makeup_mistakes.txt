A few winters ago, I was sitting in a little café near the Luxembourg Gardens, the kind with the round marble tables and the waiter who pretends not to see you for the first ten minutes. Across from me sat a woman I guessed was about seventy. Silver hair pulled back loosely, a camel coat, a cup of black coffee. And her face. Her face looked rested, fresh, almost lit from inside. I leaned in, as discreetly as I could, trying to work out what she had on. A little cream blush high on the cheeks. A soft brown pencil smudged at the lash line. A lip balm with just a hint of rose. That was it. No heavy foundation. No powder sitting in the lines. And she looked ten years younger than the woman at the next table, who had clearly spent forty minutes on her makeup that morning.

I have lived around Paris for most of my adult life, and I am in my sixties now, so I have watched this little miracle happen over and over again. The women who look the freshest are almost never the ones wearing the most makeup. They are the ones who quietly stopped making a handful of mistakes that the rest of us were taught were the rules.

So today I want to walk you through seven makeup mistakes French women over sixty simply do not make, and for each one, exactly what they do instead. The product type, the shade, the little technique. And stay with me until the sixth mistake, because it is one almost every woman I know was making without realizing it, and the fix takes about four seconds and changes the whole look of the eyes.

Before we start, tell me in the comments where you are watching from. I love knowing whether you are in Ohio or Ontario or somewhere in the south of France with your coffee. Now, let us begin.

The first mistake is heavy, matte, full-coverage foundation.

I understand why we reach for it. When we start to see spots, a little redness, some uneven tone, the instinct is to cover everything, one smooth mask from forehead to chin. For years I did exactly that. I had a bottle of thick matte foundation, one shade, and I applied it with a sponge every morning like I was plastering a wall.

Here is why it ages us. Mature skin has texture. Fine lines, a few larger pores, softness around the jaw. A thick matte layer does not hide texture. It settles into it. By lunchtime, that foundation has gathered in every crease and the lines look deeper and more defined than they would on bare skin. And because matte formulas absorb light rather than reflect it, the face loses that soft glow that reads as youth and health. Under the flat light of an office or a restaurant, the skin looks dull and almost gray. Then there is the jawline. Heavy foundation always seems to stop somewhere, and that edge becomes a line of its own.

What do French women do instead? They use a skin tint or a sheer tinted moisturizer, something with a thin, fluid texture and a natural or dewy finish. They choose a shade that matches the neck, not the face, because the face is often a touch redder. And here is the technique I learned from a woman who did makeup for theater for thirty years. Warm a small amount between your fingertips, and press it into the center of the face only. Around the nose, the chin, the middle of the cheeks. Then blend outward with whatever is left on your fingers. The edges of the face get almost nothing. The result is skin that looks like skin, just evened out. Where you need more coverage, a small dab of concealer on a spot, patted with the ring finger. Not a layer over everything.

Picture a woman stepping out of a boulangerie on a gray morning in the Marais, a baguette under her arm, the light from the shop window catching her cheek. That is the finish we are after. Skin that moves and catches light.

Now, if foundation was the first problem, the second one is what most of us do right after it. And this one surprised me more than any other.

The second mistake is powder, lots of it, pressed into the wrinkles.

So many of us were taught to set everything with powder. All over the face, generously, with a big puff. It feels tidy, it feels finished, it feels like it will hold. But think about what powder is. Tiny dry particles. When you press those particles over fine lines, they collect in the creases. Then the face moves. You smile, you talk, you squint into the sun. And every movement pushes the powder deeper into those lines. Within an hour, the powder outlines every wrinkle on your face in a fine, pale stripe. Around the eyes it is especially unkind, because the skin there is thin and very mobile. You end up looking dry and tired, even when you are not.

What French women over sixty do is far more selective. They set only the T-zone, if they set anything at all. A tiny amount of finely milled translucent powder on a small fluffy brush, swept lightly over the sides of the nose, the center of the forehead, maybe the chin. Never under the eyes, never on the cheeks where you want that glow. And the brush, not a puff. A puff presses. A brush floats. If you have an oily day, they reach for blotting papers in their handbag instead of adding more powder. One press, and the shine is gone without adding a single particle.

I remember watching a friend of mine do this in the mirror of a tiny restroom at a brasserie near Saint-Germain. She took a blotting paper out of a little leather case, pressed it once on her nose, folded it, and put it away. The whole thing took five seconds and she walked back to the table looking exactly as fresh as she did at breakfast.

Here is a little secret before we move on. Once you stop over-powdering, your skin starts showing a natural sheen. And that sheen is exactly what the third and fourth fixes are designed to work with, so keep it in mind.

The third mistake is dark lip liner.

This was a trend many of us lived through. A brown or deep plum liner, drawn just outside the natural lip line, filled with a lighter color or a gloss. It looked sharp in photographs from a certain decade. But on a mature face, it creates a problem. As we age, the edges of the lips naturally soften and lose some definition. A dark outline around a softer center draws a ring around the mouth. It makes the lips look smaller, harder, and it highlights every vertical line above the lip. Then, as the day goes on and the lipstick in the middle fades, the dark ring stays behind. There is nothing more aging than a lip liner that has outlasted the lipstick.

French women take a much gentler approach. They reach for a tinted balm, something with a sheer wash of color in a rose, a soft berry, or a warm nude that is one or two shades deeper than their natural lip. They apply it straight from the tube or with a fingertip, pressing it into the lips so the edges blur softly. If they want definition, they choose a liner that matches the lip itself, or a neutral shade almost invisible, and they use it only to fill the very corners and the outer edge, then soften it with the fingertip. The goal is a mouth that looks naturally flushed. Like you just came in from a walk.

Imagine a woman crossing the Pont des Arts in the late afternoon, the wind lifting her scarf, and she turns to laugh at something her companion said. Her lips look soft and rosy and real. That is the look of a tinted balm.

And I will tell you right now, there is one exception to this rule. One lip color French women do love to wear boldly. I am saving it for the very end of the video, because it deserves its own moment.

The fourth mistake is thin, over-drawn brows.

So many of us plucked our brows to a thin line years ago, because that was the fashion. And then the brows never quite came back. As we get older, the brow hairs can also lighten and thin naturally. So what do we do? We draw them in. Often with a pencil that is too dark, in a shape that is too thin and too arched, with a hard edge. The trouble is that a thin, sharp brow makes the face look surprised, severe, or tired. The brows frame the eyes. When they are thin and harsh, the whole upper face looks drawn and older. And a solid pencil line in a single color looks flat because real brows are made of individual hairs with a little variation in shade.

Here is the French approach. Brow gel. A tinted brow gel, in a shade one step lighter than your natural hair color if you are dark, or a soft taupe if you are gray or silver. The little brush coats the hairs you have, adds a touch of color, and gives the impression of fullness. Brush up and slightly outward. If there are gaps, they use a very fine pencil to draw tiny feathery strokes, in the direction the hair grows, never a solid line. And they keep the shape close to their natural brow, a little fuller and straighter rather than thin and highly arched. A softer, slightly straighter brow lifts the face without looking drawn on.

I picture a woman reading in the window of a bookshop near the Seine, glasses pushed up on her head, and her brows look soft and present, natural. You do not notice them. You just notice that she looks awake.

Now, I would love to hear from you on this one. Did you over-pluck your brows back in the day? I certainly did, and I regret it every morning. Tell me in the comments, and let us all feel a little less alone about it.

The fifth mistake is the one I promised you earlier. Black eyeliner all the way around the eye.

I know. Many of us wore it this way for decades. Black kohl on the top lash line and the bottom, sometimes even inside the waterline. It felt dramatic, it felt like Paris in the movies. But on a mature eye, it does something unkind. When you ring the entire eye in black, you close the eye. It looks smaller. As the eyelids soften and hood a little with age, that black line can disappear into the fold on top and then sit heavily on the bottom, which pulls the whole eye downward. And black on the lower lash line tends to smudge into the fine lines underneath, creating shadows exactly where we already have them. It can make you look exhausted.

What French women over sixty do instead is wonderfully simple. They switch to a brown pencil. A soft, warm brown, or a deep taupe, or even a charcoal gray if they have very dark coloring. They draw it only along the upper lash line, as close to the lashes as possible, pressing it into the roots of the lashes rather than drawing a line above them. Then, and this is the four-second trick I mentioned at the start, they take a cotton swab or a small smudge brush and gently blur that line outward. The color sits at the roots of the lashes, so the lashes look thicker. No harsh edge. No line on the bottom. If they want a little definition below, they smudge the tiniest amount of the same brown only at the outer corner, just the last third of the eye, and blend it out until it is almost invisible.

The difference is remarkable. The eye looks open, lifted, and soft. People will tell you that you look well rested and they will not be able to say why.

Picture a woman stepping out of the metro at Saint-Paul, the morning light hitting her face as she climbs the stairs, and her eyes look clear and open. That is a brown smudged liner.

And now we come to the sixth mistake. This is the one almost every woman I know was making without realizing it.

The sixth mistake is frosted eyeshadow.

You know the kind. Shimmering, icy, frosted, pearly. Pale blue, silver, frosty pink. Many of us loved it, and it still sells well. But frost on a mature eyelid is a little like holding a flashlight sideways to a piece of crumpled paper. It highlights every ridge. Those big reflective particles sit on top of the skin, and wherever there is a fine line or a crease, the frost catches the light and makes it more visible. The lid can look crepey even when it is not. And icy shades in particular can make the skin around the eyes look gray and cool.

French women do not give up on light, though. They just choose a different kind. They wear satin eyeshadow. Not completely matte, which can look flat and dry, and not frosted. A satin finish has a soft, low sheen, like the surface of a silk blouse. It reflects light gently, which brightens the lid without exaggerating texture. And the shades they choose are soft and close to skin tone. Taupe, soft bronze, warm mushroom, a muted rose-brown. Swept over the lid with a fingertip or a fluffy brush, and blended up toward the crease. One color, maybe two. Nothing complicated. Cream shadows in a satin finish are especially lovely on mature lids because they melt into the skin. Just set a light layer, let it dry for a moment, and blend the edges with a clean finger.

Here is the visual I always see in my mind. A woman sitting on a green metal chair in the Tuileries in autumn, eyes closed, face turned toward the sun. Her eyelids have the faintest bronze glow, soft as the light itself. That is satin.

We are almost there. Before I tell you the last mistake, and that one lip color French women wear without apology, I want to ask you something. Which of these mistakes have you been making? Be honest, I promise no one here is judging. Write the number in the comments. One, two, three, four, five, six. I am curious which one is the most common.

The seventh mistake is contour stripes.

Over the past several years, contouring became very fashionable. Dark brown stripes under the cheekbones, along the sides of the nose, under the jaw, blended out to sculpt the face. On a camera with professional lighting, it can look striking. In daylight on a mature face, it usually does the opposite of what we hope. As we age, the face naturally loses a little volume in the cheeks and the temples. Adding dark shadows under the cheekbones does not sculpt. It hollows. It makes the face look gaunt, tired, and older. And in natural daylight, a brown stripe is visible for exactly what it is. A stripe of makeup. The cooler, grayish contour shades many of us were sold make it even worse, because they look like bruising rather than shadow.

French women over sixty do the opposite. Instead of taking away, they add warmth and life. They use cream blush. A soft, sheer cream in a rosy pink, a warm apricot, or a gentle berry, depending on their coloring. And here is the placement that changes everything. Not on the apples of the cheeks, where it can pull the face down when you stop smiling. Instead, they place a small amount on the top of the cheekbone, slightly toward the temple, and blend upward and outward with the fingertips. The color sits high, which lifts the whole face. Because it is a cream, it melts into the skin and reflects light softly rather than sitting on top. Some women also tap the same cream lightly on the lips and even the eyelids, so everything matches and looks like a natural flush. That is a very Parisian trick. One color, three places, done in a minute.

If they want a little shape, a touch of bronzer, very sheer, very warm, on the high points of the face where the sun would naturally touch. Forehead, the top of the cheekbones, the bridge of the nose. A sun-kissed look, not a shadow.

Picture an older woman at a flower stall on the Rue Cler, choosing peonies, her cheeks pink with color, laughing with the vendor. She looks alive. That is cream blush, high and warm.

And now, as promised, the one exception. The bold choice French women over sixty make without hesitation.

The red lip.

Remember what I said about dark liner, about keeping the mouth soft and natural. That is the everyday rule. But French women have one bold move they keep for themselves, and that is a true red lip. When they wear it, they wear it with confidence, and here is the important part. Everything else on the face goes quiet. A sheer skin tint. A little brow gel. A brown smudge at the lash line, or nothing at all on the eyes. And then a beautiful red. Not a heavy dark matte, which can dry out and settle into lines, but a satin red, often with a slightly blue undertone, which makes the teeth look whiter and the skin look brighter. Applied over a thin layer of balm so it glides. Blotted once with a tissue so it looks lived in, not painted.

The rule is simple. Red lip, or strong eyes. Never both. When the lip is the statement, the face looks elegant and intentional, never overdone.

I will never forget an older woman I saw at the Palais Garnier one evening, at intermission. Silver hair in a low bun, a simple black dress, almost no makeup except a perfect red mouth. She was the most elegant person in the room. Everyone noticed her, and no one could quite say why.

So let us bring it all together. Instead of heavy matte foundation, a sheer skin tint pressed into the center of the face. Instead of powder in the wrinkles, a light touch on the T-zone only, and blotting papers in your bag. Instead of dark lip liner, a tinted balm pressed in with your finger. Instead of thin, over-drawn brows, a tinted brow gel and soft feathery strokes. Instead of black liner all around, a brown pencil pressed into the upper lashes and smudged. Instead of frosted shadow, satin in soft, warm neutrals. Instead of contour stripes, a cream blush placed high and blended upward. And when you want a statement, a satin red lip with everything else quiet.

What strikes me, every time, is that none of this is about hiding our age. The French women I have known over the years were never trying to look thirty-five. They were trying to look like themselves, on a good day. Rested, a little rosy, interested in the world. The makeup supports the face rather than covering it. And it is quicker, too. Most of these women spend ten minutes in the morning, not forty. That leaves a lot more time for coffee.

I want to share one more little story before I let you go. A neighbor of mine, a retired schoolteacher who must be close to seventy-five now, once told me something while we were waiting for the bus outside the market. I had complimented her on how lovely she looked, and she just shrugged in that way the French do, and she said, the older I get, the less I put on, and the more people tell me I look well. I laughed, but it stayed with me. She was right. Every year I take one more thing out of my makeup bag, and every year I like my face a little more.

Let me give you a few practical notes on how to make these changes without throwing everything away at once. Start with one. Just one. If I had to choose, I would start with the eyeliner, because it is the fastest and the most dramatic difference. Put away the black kohl for a week. Use a soft brown on the upper lash line only, smudge it, and look in the mirror in natural light by a window. You will see it immediately. Then, the next week, try the skin tint instead of your usual foundation. The week after, the cream blush. By the end of a month, you will have a whole new routine and it will feel like your own.

And always check your makeup in daylight, not under the bathroom bulb. The light by a window is the light people see you in on the street. French women almost always do a last check by the window before they leave the apartment, and now I do too. It is the most honest mirror there is.

One last thing about skin care, because makeup only sits as well as the skin underneath. A good moisturizer under the skin tint makes all of these techniques work better, especially on drier mature skin. If you have particular skin concerns, sensitivity or redness, it is always worth checking with your dermatologist about what suits you. Every face is different, and a little professional advice goes a long way.
