Last spring, I was waiting for a friend outside the church of Saint-Sulpice, sitting on the edge of the fountain with the pigeons, when a woman walked across the square who made me stop mid-sip of my coffee. She was perhaps sixty-eight. Her hair was pure silver, cut just below the jaw, and it shone. I mean truly shone, like a piece of polished pewter in the sun. Smooth, soft at the ends, lifted gently at the roots. She had a navy silk scarf knotted at her neck and a little canvas bag over her shoulder, and when the breeze moved her hair, it moved all in one silky piece.

That same morning, I had looked at my own gray hair in the bathroom mirror and sighed. It was a little yellow at the front, frizzy at the ends, and flat as a pancake on top. Same color as hers, more or less. Completely different result.

I am in my sixties, and I have lived around Paris for most of my life. Since I stopped coloring, I have become a little obsessed with how French women care for their gray hair. Because here is what I learned. Going gray is only the first step. What makes gray hair look elegant, or tired, is what you do to it afterward.

So today, I want to walk you through seven things French women over sixty never do to gray hair, and exactly what they do instead. Simple habits, a few products, a couple of little tricks. And stay with me until the sixth one, because it is the one almost everybody skips, and it is the single biggest reason gray hair looks dull instead of bright.

Before we start, tell me in the comments. How long have you been gray? A few months? Twenty years? I always love seeing the range in this community.

The first thing French women never do is let their gray turn yellow.

If you have gray or white hair, you have probably noticed it. Over time, especially at the front and around the face, a yellowish or brassy tone creeps in. It can come from the sun, from pollution, from minerals in the water, from heat styling, even from some hair products. Gray hair has less pigment, so it takes on those tints more easily than colored hair would.

Why does yellow age us? Because it dulls everything. Bright silver reflects cool, clean light onto the face, which makes the skin look fresh and the eyes look clear. Yellowed gray reflects a muddy, warm light that can make the skin look sallow and the teeth look less white. It also makes the hair look neglected, even if you take good care of it. People see yellow and think tired.

What French women do instead is simple. They use a purple shampoo, once a week. Purple sits opposite yellow on the color wheel, so a shampoo with a violet pigment gently cancels out the yellow tone. You lather it in, leave it a few minutes, and rinse. The trick is not to overdo it. Used every day, or left on too long, a purple shampoo can leave a faint lilac tint on very light hair, and it can be a little drying. Once a week is usually enough. On the other days, a gentle, moisturizing shampoo.

And here is a small detail I learned from a colorist in the Batignolles. Concentrate the purple shampoo on the areas that yellow the most. Usually the front hairline and the pieces around the face, where the sun hits. The back often needs much less.

Picture a woman walking along the Seine on a bright winter morning, her silver hair catching that cold, clear light, and it looks almost white-blue. That is the effect of a toning shampoo used well.

Now, if yellow is the problem of color, the second mistake is the problem of texture. And it is the one I fought with the longest.

The second thing French women never do is ignore frizzy, dry ends.

Gray hair often has a different texture from the hair we had before. Many women notice it is coarser, wirier, or drier. The scalp produces a little less oil as we age, so the hair, especially the ends, can become dry. And dry hair frizzes. The cuticle, the outer layer of each strand, lifts up instead of lying flat.

Why does frizz age us? Because of light. Smooth hair has a flat surface that reflects light like a mirror, which is what we see as shine. Frizzy hair has a rough, lifted surface that scatters light in every direction. Instead of shining, it looks fuzzy and matte. And frizz around the outline of the head, especially at the ends, creates a kind of halo that looks unkempt. It makes the whole style look older, even with a good cut.

What French women do instead is wonderfully simple. A few drops of oil on the ends. A light hair oil, rubbed between the palms and smoothed over the last few inches of the hair, the ends only, never the roots. They do it on damp hair after washing, and sometimes a tiny bit more on dry hair before going out. It smooths the cuticle, tames the frizz, and adds that lovely soft shine. Lighter oils work well for fine hair, richer ones for thick, coarse hair. The key is to use very little. Two or three drops for short hair, a few more for longer. Too much, and the hair goes greasy.

I watched an older woman do this once on the platform of the metro at Odéon, waiting for her train. She took a tiny bottle out of her bag, put one drop on her fingertips, and smoothed it over the ends of her bob, quick as anything, then put the bottle away. Nobody noticed but me.

Before we go on, I would love to ask. Has your hair texture changed since you went gray? Tell me in the comments. Mine certainly did. Coarser and much drier.

The third thing French women never do is accept flat roots.

This is a very common complaint. As we get older, hair often becomes finer and less dense, and it tends to lie flat against the scalp, especially at the crown. With gray hair, flat roots can show more of the scalp, which can make the hair look thinner than it is.

Why does it age? Because volume at the roots lifts the whole face. When the hair sits flat on top, the shape of the head looks smaller and the face looks longer and heavier by comparison. Flat hair also falls into straight, still lines around the face, and those straight lines draw the eye downward. Lift at the crown, on the other hand, creates a soft, rounded outline that frames the face and makes it look rested.

What French women do instead is use a root lift spray. A light spray or mousse applied right at the roots on damp hair, especially at the crown and the part. Then they rough-dry the hair, lifting it at the roots with their fingers, or flip their head upside down for a moment while drying. Some use a round brush to lift the crown section. And here is a lovely trick. They change their part. If you have parted your hair in exactly the same place for twenty years, the hair is trained to lie flat there. Moving the part even half an inch to one side gives instant lift, because the hair is falling against its habit.

Imagine a woman stepping out of a doorway on the Rue du Bac, pausing to pull on her gloves, and her silver hair has a soft, natural lift at the crown, like it was styled without effort. That is the result.

Now, the fourth thing is one I feel very strongly about, because I made this mistake myself, and I would hate for you to make it too.

The fourth thing French women never do is harsh home bleaching.

Many women, during the transition to gray, try to speed things up by bleaching their dark hair at home to match the silver coming in. Or they try to lighten yellowed gray with a strong bleach kit from the pharmacy. I did this once, in my kitchen, with the window open and a towel around my neck, and I will never do it again.

Why does it age? Because strong bleach is very hard on hair, and gray hair is often already more fragile and drier. Harsh bleaching at home can leave the hair brittle, straw-like, and uneven in color. Patchy yellow and orange areas, breakage, and a texture that feels like cotton candy. And once the hair is damaged that badly, the only real fix is to cut it off. Damaged hair does not reflect light. It looks dry, dull, and frayed at the ends, which is exactly the opposite of the silky silver we want.

What French women do instead is ask a professional for a gloss treatment. A gloss is a sheer, shiny treatment, usually done in the salon, that coats the hair, smooths the surface, and adds a beautiful shine. Some glosses are clear. Some have a very soft tint to correct yellowing or to blend gray more evenly. They are far gentler than bleach. If someone wants a more dramatic change in color, they see a colorist who can lighten gradually and carefully over several appointments, using products designed to protect the hair. And they always do a patch test if they are trying something new, and talk to their dermatologist if their scalp has ever reacted to a product.

I remember after my kitchen disaster, I went to a small salon near the Rue de Charonne, and the colorist looked at my hair with a pained expression, and said, very kindly, that we would cut it short and start again. She gave me a gloss every few weeks while it grew out, and my hair was shinier than it had been in years.

Before we move on, I want to know. Have you ever had a hair disaster at home? A color gone wrong, a cut you did yourself? Share it in the comments. We have all been there, and it helps to laugh about it.

The fifth thing French women never do is buzz it too short.

When women go gray, some decide to cut it all off. A very short, buzzed cut, almost shaved. It is a quick way to get rid of old color, and I deeply respect the courage. On some women, with the right bone structure and the confidence to carry it, it looks fantastic.

But for many women, a buzz cut that is too short creates a few problems. Very short gray hair can show a lot of scalp, which reads as thinning. It removes all the softness around the face, so every angle of the jaw and every line of the neck is exposed. And gray hair cut extremely short can look coarse and bristly, catching the light in a harsh, scattered way.

What French women tend to do instead is keep enough length to have some softness. A short cut, yes, but with a little length on top and around the face, so the hair can fall forward and frame the eyes. Something like a soft pixie or a short bob. And they keep it in shape with a trim every eight weeks. That is the real secret. Not too short, but never shaggy either. A regular trim keeps the ends fresh and healthy, keeps the shape crisp, and removes any dry or yellowed ends before they become a problem.

I think every eight weeks is the magic number for most gray haircuts. Long enough to let the hair grow in, short enough that the cut never loses its shape.

Picture a woman sitting at a sidewalk table in front of a café on the Place de la Contrescarpe, her short silver pixie soft over her forehead, her cheekbones framed. It looks deliberate, polished, and very chic.

Now we come to the sixth one, the one I promised you at the beginning. The one almost everyone skips.

The sixth thing French women never do is ignore gloss.

I mentioned gloss a moment ago as an alternative to bleach. But here I want to talk about it as a habit, because this is truly the secret of that woman I saw at Saint-Sulpice. Gray hair, by its nature, tends to look duller than pigmented hair. Pigment gives hair depth, and depth helps shine show up. Without it, gray can look flat, chalky, or matte, even when it is healthy.

Why does it matter so much? Because shine is the single quality we read as healthy, youthful hair. A head of gray hair that shines looks vibrant and elegant. A head of gray hair that is dull looks tired, no matter how good the cut. The difference between them is often not the hair itself, but whether the surface has been smoothed and sealed.

What French women do instead is make gloss a regular part of their routine. Some go to the salon every few weeks for a gloss treatment. Others use an at-home gloss or a shine treatment once a week in the shower. These products coat the hair with a thin, smooth layer that reflects light. Some are clear, some slightly tinted. And between treatments, they protect the shine. They rinse with cool or lukewarm water rather than very hot, they use heat protection if they use a hair dryer, and they wear a hat in strong sun.

There is a little ritual I learned from an older friend who lives near the Parc Monceau. Once a week, on Sunday evening, she does what she calls her silver night. Purple shampoo, then a gloss, then a few drops of oil on the ends. She says it takes fifteen minutes, and her hair looks freshly done all week. I have stolen her ritual completely, and I recommend you steal it too.

Picture a woman stepping out of a Haussmann building into a sunny street, and her silver hair catches the sun so brightly it almost looks like it is lit from inside. That is gloss.

We are nearly at the end. Before the last one, I want to ask you. Which of these habits are you already doing? And which one are you going to try this week? Tell me in the comments. I love hearing your plans.

The seventh thing French women never do is go six months without a cut.

I know how it happens. Life gets busy. The hair looks fine, more or less. And then suddenly it has been half a year. With colored hair, the root regrowth forces us to go back to the salon. With gray hair, there is no root to remind us, so it is easy to put it off.

Why does it age? Because over six months the ends become dry, thin, and split. The shape of the cut grows out and loses its structure. And with gray hair, the oldest ends are often the ones that have yellowed the most from sun and water. So a long gap between cuts gives you a head of hair that is shapeless, dull at the ends, and slightly yellow. Exactly what we have spent this whole video trying to avoid.

What French women do instead is keep a regular rhythm. A trim every eight weeks or so, even if it is just to clean up the ends. Some book their next appointment as they leave the salon so they never forget. And between cuts, on days when the hair is not cooperating, they have a beautiful backup plan. The silk hair scarf.

This is one of the most Parisian things I know. A silk scarf, folded into a band and tied around the head, or knotted at the nape, or wrapped around a low ponytail. It hides a bad hair day in seconds. And because it is silk, it is smooth and gentle on the hair, and it protects it from wind and sun. It also brings color right up next to the face, which is so flattering with gray hair. A navy, a rust, a soft red, a print with a little gold. Gray hair is a perfect canvas for color.

And speaking of silk, there is one more habit I want to share. Many French women sleep on a silk or satin pillowcase. Cotton creates friction against the hair while you turn in your sleep, which can cause frizz, tangling, and breakage, especially for drier gray hair. Silk lets the hair glide, so you wake up with smoother hair, and fewer flyaways around the face. It is such a small change, and so many women I know swear by it.

Picture a woman walking past the green bookstalls along the Quai de Montebello, a printed silk scarf tied loosely in her silver hair, the ends fluttering in the breeze. Effortless, colorful, and completely elegant.

So let us bring it all together. Instead of letting gray turn yellow, a purple shampoo once a week. Instead of frizzy, dry ends, a few drops of oil on the ends only. Instead of flat roots, a root lift spray and a new part. Instead of harsh home bleach, a gentle gloss from a professional. Instead of a buzz cut that is too short, a soft short cut with a trim every eight weeks. Instead of ignoring shine, a regular gloss ritual. And instead of going six months without a cut, a steady rhythm of trims, with a silk scarf for the in-between days and a silk pillowcase at night.

What I love about all of this is that none of it is complicated or expensive. It is not about fighting gray hair. It is about caring for it. Gray hair is a different material from the hair we had before, and it simply asks for different attention. Once you give it that attention, it becomes one of the most beautiful things about you.

Let me give you a simple weekly plan, because I know it helps to see how this fits into real life. On wash days, use a gentle, moisturizing shampoo and conditioner, and rinse with lukewarm water. Once a week, swap in the purple shampoo, concentrating on the front. On damp hair, a little root lift spray at the crown and a few drops of oil on the ends. Rough-dry with your fingers. Every few weeks, a gloss, at home or in the salon. Every eight weeks, a trim. Every night, a silk pillowcase. And in your handbag, a silk scarf for windy days and the moments when your hair simply refuses to behave.

That is it. No complicated rituals, no cabinet full of bottles. A handful of habits that, together, make an enormous difference.

I also want to say something about heat. Many of us grew up blow-drying our hair hot every single day, and curling it, and setting it. Gray hair is often less forgiving of high heat. It can scorch, it can yellow, and it can become brittle. French women I know tend to let their hair air-dry most of the way, then finish with a few minutes of the dryer on a medium setting, just to smooth the front and lift the roots. If you do use heat, a protective spray beforehand is a kind gift to your hair.

And one note about water. In some places, including parts of Paris, the tap water is quite hard, full of minerals. Over time those minerals can leave a dull film on gray hair and add to that yellowish tint. Some women do a final rinse with filtered water, or use a clarifying shampoo now and then to lift the buildup. If your gray seems to go dull no matter what you do, it is worth thinking about your water.

A few practical notes before I go. If you notice sudden changes in your hair, a lot of shedding, or changes in your scalp, itchiness, flaking, or redness, please talk to your doctor or a dermatologist. Hair changes for many reasons as we age, and some of those reasons are worth checking. And when you try any new product, especially on your scalp, try a small amount first to see how your skin reacts.

I want to leave you with a little memory. When I finally caught up with my friend that morning at Saint-Sulpice, I told her about the woman with the shining silver hair. And my friend, who has known me for forty years, laughed and said, you know, your hair could look like that too. You just have to treat it like it matters. It took me another year to really listen. But she was right. It does matter. And so do you.
