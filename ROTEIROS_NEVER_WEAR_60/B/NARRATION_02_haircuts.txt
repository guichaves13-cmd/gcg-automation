It was a rainy Tuesday in the seventeenth arrondissement, and I was sitting in a small salon with a towel around my shoulders, staring at a photograph I had brought with me. A short, tight, very neat cut I had worn for nearly fifteen years. My hairdresser, a woman in her fifties with a gray streak she wore like a ribbon, picked up the photo, looked at it, looked at me, and set it face down on the counter. Then she said, very kindly, that she would rather not. She said that cut was holding my face still, and that I deserved a haircut that moved.

I was a little offended, I will admit. But I let her do what she wanted. And when I walked out onto the wet pavement an hour later, catching my reflection in the window of a pharmacy, I barely recognized myself. I looked softer. Lighter. Younger, honestly, though she never once used that word.

I am in my sixties now, and I have spent most of my life around Paris, watching how the women here wear their hair as they get older. There are a handful of haircuts they almost never choose. Not because of some rule, but because they understand what those cuts do to a mature face. So today I am going to walk you through six haircuts French women over sixty never wear, and for each one, exactly what they wear instead, with the details you can take straight to your own hairdresser.

And stay with me until the fifth one, because it is a cut so many women ask for, thinking it hides the forehead and takes years off, and in fact it does almost the opposite. The fix is something you can try without cutting anything at all.

Before we begin, tell me in the comments how long you have had your current haircut. I suspect some of you, like me, will say more than ten years. Let us see.

The first haircut is the tight, helmet perm.

Many of us grew up watching our mothers and grandmothers sit under the dryer every week with a set of small rollers, and then later the tight perm became the easy answer. Curls close to the head, set firm, needing very little work. I understand the appeal. When hair thins and loses body, the perm gives the impression of volume.

But here is why it ages us. A tight, uniform curl creates a solid shape around the head. It does not move. It does not catch light in soft waves. Instead it forms a rounded, dense outline, and that outline reads immediately as a style from another era. Our eyes associate it with age before they even look at the face. And tight curls tend to frizz at the surface, which scatters light and makes the hair look dry and dull, especially if it is gray. The shape also sits wide on the sides and close on top, which widens the face exactly where we would rather not.

What do French women do instead? They wear a soft French bob. Somewhere between the chin and the jaw, cut with a few invisible internal layers so the hair has movement but no obvious steps. The ends are cut softly, often with a point-cutting technique, where the hairdresser snips into the ends vertically rather than straight across, so the edge looks feathered and natural. If they need volume, the hairdresser leaves a little more length at the crown, and they rough-dry it with their fingers, lifting at the roots. The bob swings when they turn their head. That movement alone takes years off.

Picture a woman stepping off a bus near the Place des Vosges, the wind catching her chin-length bob so it lifts and falls back into place. That swing is what we are after.

Now, if the perm is the cut that holds too still, the next one is the cut that tries too hard. And I think many of you will recognize it.

The second haircut is the spiky short cut.

At some point, many women decide to go very short. Easy to manage, practical, maybe a little bold. Nothing wrong with short hair at all. The problem is how it is often styled. Spiked up with gel or wax, short and choppy all over, with hard, pointed ends sticking out from the crown.

Here is why it ages. Spikes create a lot of sharp, harsh lines around the face. On mature skin, which has softened, those sharp edges create a contrast that makes the face look tired, almost brittle. The heavy styling products also catch the light in a stiff, shiny way, or they make the hair look stuck together in clumps, which shows the scalp underneath. And if the hair is gray or white, spikes can look thin and wiry. The overall effect is a style that seems to be working too hard to look young, and that is precisely what makes it look older.

French women who love short hair go for something softer. They wear what some hairdressers call a bixie, which is a cross between a bob and a pixie. Short at the back and the nape, but longer and softer on top and around the face, with a little length at the front that can be swept to one side. The layers are long enough to fall rather than stand up. They style it with a light cream or a soft mousse, worked through with the fingers, and they push it forward and to the side rather than up. The result is short, easy hair that frames the face with softness. It shows off the neck and cheekbones, which is one of the great advantages of short hair, without any harshness.

Imagine a woman in a navy trench walking along the quai of the Canal Saint-Martin, her short silver hair falling softly over one eyebrow as she leans to look at the water. That is a bixie.

Before we go on, tell me, are you a short hair person or a long hair person? Write short or long in the comments. I am curious how this community divides, and I have a feeling it is closer than we think.

The third haircut is the severe, one-length bob.

This one surprises people, because I just told you French women love a bob. They do. But there is a world of difference between a soft bob and a severe one. The severe bob is cut completely blunt, all one length, very straight, often sharp at the chin, and blow-dried flat and sleek with no movement at all.

Here is the trouble. A heavy, blunt line at the jaw draws a hard horizontal line across the face, exactly where many of us are starting to see some softness in the jawline. Your eye goes straight to that line, and then straight to whatever is happening around it. Flat, straight hair against the face also removes all the light and shadow that hair creates when it moves. Instead it frames the face like a pair of curtains, very dark and heavy on the sides. If the hair is fine, a blunt one-length cut can also look thin at the ends because there is nothing to support it.

French women keep the bob but soften it in a few specific ways. First, they choose a length just slightly below the jaw or at the chin, not exactly at the jaw. Second, the hairdresser adds subtle layers around the face, so the front pieces fall a touch shorter and frame the cheekbones. Third, the ends are textured, not blunt. And finally, they style it with a gentle bend, a soft wave or a slight curve under, using a large round brush or simply twisting the hair while it dries. The bob looks relaxed and easy, like it was done without trying.

Picture a woman at a café terrace on the Rue Montorgueil, tucking one side of her bob behind her ear while she reads the menu. The other side falls forward softly. That is the effect. Effortless, but finished.

Now, if the first three were about hair that is too short or too set, the next one goes completely the other way. And I know some of you hold on to this one with a lot of love.

The fourth haircut is waist-long, thin hair.

I want to be gentle here, because long hair can be beautiful at any age, and I have seen some marvelous older women with long hair worn in a braid or a bun. But there is a particular version that French women over sixty tend to avoid. Very long hair, past the shoulder blades or down to the waist, that has become fine and thin at the ends, worn loose with no shape.

Here is why it ages. As hair gets longer, the ends are older. They have been washed and brushed and exposed to sun for years, so they become thinner, drier, and more transparent. When long hair is worn loose, those thin ends hang down and draw the eye downward, which makes the whole face seem to drag. And because the weight of long hair pulls it flat at the roots, there is no volume on top, which can make the face look longer and more tired. The see-through ends also catch the light in a wispy, uneven way that reads as fragile.

What French women do instead, if they love length, is a collarbone lob with layers. That is a long bob that sits right at the collarbone or just above it. Long enough to tie back, long enough to feel feminine and flowing, but short enough that the ends stay thick and healthy. The hairdresser adds long layers through the lengths so the hair has movement, and a few face-framing layers starting around the cheekbones. The weight comes off, so the roots lift naturally. The ends look full. And it is wonderfully versatile. You can wear it straight, with a soft wave, or pull it into a low ponytail.

Imagine a woman crossing the Jardin du Palais Royal under the rows of trees, her collarbone-length hair moving in the breeze with a soft wave at the ends. It looks generous and healthy, and it frames her face beautifully.

The fifth haircut is the one I mentioned at the beginning. The heavy, blunt fringe.

So many women ask for bangs as they get older. The thinking makes sense. Bangs cover the forehead lines, they feel youthful, they draw attention to the eyes. And bangs can be lovely. But the heavy, thick, blunt fringe, cut straight across at the eyebrows, often does the opposite of what we hope.

Here is why. A thick, solid fringe creates a strong horizontal line right above the eyes. If the eyelids are starting to hood, or the brows have thinned, that heavy line sits directly on top of them and makes the eyes look smaller and heavier. It casts a shadow over the upper face. Blunt fringes also need constant styling. They separate, they lift in humidity, they go flat with a hat. And when they are too heavy, they look like a helmet sitting on the forehead. It is a sharp, young look, and on a mature face, the contrast makes everything around it look older.

What French women wear instead are curtain bangs. Longer, softer bangs that part in the center and sweep out to the sides, falling around the cheekbones like a pair of parted curtains, which is where the name comes from. They are cut longest at the outer edges and a little shorter in the middle, so they frame the eyes rather than cover them. Because they open up in the center, they show a little forehead, which lifts the face. And they blend beautifully into face-framing layers.

Now here is the part I promised you. You can try this without cutting a thing. Take the front section of your hair, part it in the center, and while it is damp, wrap each side around a large round brush, or simply twist it back away from the face and pin it for a few minutes while you have your coffee. When you take it out, you will see the shape that curtain bangs would give you. If you like it, take that image to your hairdresser.

Picture a woman at a window table in a tearoom on the Île Saint-Louis, her soft curtain bangs framing her eyes as she looks up at you and smiles. Open, warm, lifted. That is the magic of a fringe that parts.

Before the sixth one, I would love to know. Have you ever cut bangs and regretted it? I certainly have, twice. Tell me your bang story in the comments. I promise to read them.

The sixth haircut is the stacked bob.

You will know it immediately. Short in the back, with layers stacked up high at the nape, often shaved or cut close at the neck, and then longer angled pieces in the front. The back has a rounded, built-up shape, sometimes called a wedge. It was enormously popular for a while, and many hairdressers still offer it as the go-to cut for women over fifty.

Here is why French women avoid it. The stacked bob depends on a lot of volume built up in a very specific spot at the back of the head. That shape is very recognizable, and it immediately dates the haircut. And it requires a lot of maintenance. Round brushing, teasing, hairspray, regular trims every few weeks to keep the stacking sharp. When it is not perfectly styled, the back looks flat and the angled front pieces look stringy. The sharp angle from back to front also creates a strong diagonal line that pulls the eye down along the jaw.

What French women do instead is the very opposite of high maintenance. Wash-and-go texture. A cut designed to work with the natural texture of the hair, whatever it is. If the hair has a little natural wave, the cut encourages it. If it is straight, the cut uses soft layers so it falls into a relaxed shape. The hairdresser studies how the hair behaves when it dries naturally and cuts accordingly. Then all you need is a little texturizing spray or a light cream, scrunched into damp hair, and either a few minutes with a diffuser or simply letting it air dry. It never looks overdone, because it is not done. It just looks like you.

This is perhaps the most French idea of all. The haircut is the style. You should not have to work hard every morning to recreate something the hairdresser did. A good cut falls into place by itself.

Imagine a woman at the Marché d'Aligre on a Saturday morning, choosing tomatoes, her slightly wavy gray hair a little tousled from the wind. It looks relaxed, intentional, and completely modern.

Now, as promised, my solution for those of you who love your long hair. The low chignon.

French women over sixty who keep their hair long almost always wear it up, at least part of the time. And not in a tight, high bun pulled back hard from the face, which can look severe. They wear a low chignon. Soft, loose, gathered at the nape of the neck, with a few pieces left out around the face. You twist the hair loosely, wrap it around itself, and secure it with a few pins or a simple clip. It does not need to be perfect. In fact, it looks better a little imperfect. The weight of the hair sits low, which shows off the neck and the earrings, and the loose pieces around the face soften the whole look.

It is the easiest thing in the world, and it turns long hair from something that drags the face down into something elegant and graceful. And the chignon also protects the ends of long hair from rubbing against collars and scarves, which keeps them healthier.

I think of a woman I used to see at the Sunday concerts in a church in the fifth arrondissement. She must have been eighty. Long white hair, always in a soft low chignon with a tortoiseshell pin. She was the picture of elegance, every single week.

So let us bring it all together. Instead of a tight helmet perm, a soft French bob that swings. Instead of a spiky short cut, a bixie with soft length on top. Instead of a severe one-length bob, a softly layered bob with textured ends and a gentle bend. Instead of waist-long thin hair, a collarbone lob with long layers. Instead of a heavy blunt fringe, curtain bangs that part in the middle. Instead of a stacked bob, a wash-and-go cut that works with your natural texture. And if you keep it long, a soft low chignon.

There is a common thread through all of these, and it is movement. Every haircut French women choose after sixty moves. It catches light. It falls naturally. It frames the face without boxing it in. The cuts they avoid, on the other hand, are almost all fixed. Set, stacked, spiked, blunt. Hair that holds still makes the face look still, and stillness reads as age. Hair that moves makes the face look alive.

Let me give you a few practical tips for your next appointment. First, bring photos, but bring them as a conversation, not an order. Show your hairdresser what you like about the image. The movement, the length, the softness. Then ask what would work with your hair texture. Second, ask your hairdresser to look at your hair dry before cutting, so they see how it really falls. Third, if you are nervous about a big change, go in stages. Take off a few inches, add a few face-framing layers, and see how you feel. You can always go shorter next time.

Fourth, talk about your routine honestly. If you will never pick up a round brush, say so. If you swim three times a week, or wear a hat in winter, or wake up with your hair flat on one side, tell them. The best hairdressers I have known in Paris always asked me how much time I spend on my hair in the morning before they picked up the scissors. A beautiful cut that you cannot recreate at home is not a beautiful cut. It is a beautiful afternoon, and then a frustrating month.

Fifth, think about the shape of your face and your glasses, if you wear them. Frames sit right where face-framing layers fall, so ask your hairdresser to look at you with your glasses on. A curtain bang that skims the top of a frame can look lovely, while one that tangles with it every time you move will drive you mad within a week.

And finally, keep up with trims. Softer cuts with textured ends look their best when the ends are fresh. Every six to ten weeks is a good rhythm for most of these styles. It does not need to be a big appointment. Ten minutes to clean up the shape is often enough, and it keeps the movement in the cut.

I once asked a hairdresser in the Batignolles, a very elegant older man who had been cutting hair for decades, what the single most common mistake was that he saw on women my age. He thought for a moment and said, they come in asking me to make them look younger, and what they really need is for me to make them look lighter. Lighter in weight, lighter in shape, lighter around the face. I have never forgotten it.

And one more thing. If you have noticed your hair thinning a lot, or changes in your scalp, it is always worth mentioning to your doctor or a dermatologist. Hair can change for many reasons as we age, and it is good to know what is going on.

I want to leave you with a little memory. After that first appointment I told you about, the one where my hairdresser turned my photo face down, I went to a dinner with friends that same evening. Nobody said, oh, you cut your hair. What they said was, you look so well, have you been on holiday? That is when I understood. A good haircut does not announce itself. It just makes you look like the best version of yourself.
