The first time I really noticed it, I was standing in the queue at the post office on the Rue du Louvre. In front of me was a woman about my age, perhaps a little older, with hair dyed a deep, flat, almost blue-black. The overhead light hit it and it looked like a helmet of shoe polish. And underneath it, her face looked pale, a little gray, every line sharpened. Then she turned to leave and I saw her friend waiting for her by the door. Same age, same height. But her hair was a soft, cool silver, cut to the chin, with a few slightly deeper strands underneath. Her skin looked luminous. Her eyes looked bright. She looked like the younger sister, though I would guess they had been born the same year.

I have lived around Paris for most of my life, and now that I am in my sixties, I have gone through every hair color decision you can imagine. Dark brown from a box for years. Then highlights. Then the slow, terrifying grow-out. And along the way I have watched how French women my age handle this question, because they handle it very differently from what I was taught.

So today I want to talk about why so many French women over sixty stop dyeing their hair dark, and the seven shades they wear instead. I will tell you what each one looks like, who it suits, and how to ask for it. And stay with me until the sixth shade, because it is one I had never heard of until a colorist explained it to me, and it is the gentlest way I know to transition from dark hair without that awful line of regrowth.

Before we start, tell me in the comments. Are you coloring your hair right now, or have you gone natural? I would love to know where everyone is on this journey.

First, let us talk honestly about why dark dye ages a mature face.

When we are young, our hair color and our skin, eyebrows, and eyes are in harmony. Nature matched them. As we age, the skin naturally becomes a little lighter and loses some of its warmth and color. The eyebrows and lashes lighten too. But if we keep dyeing the hair the same dark shade we had at twenty-five, or a shade even darker, there is suddenly a very strong contrast between the hair and the face. That contrast is what does the damage.

Dark hair right next to the face casts a shadow. It drains color from the cheeks and lips and makes the skin look sallow or gray. It emphasizes every line and every dark circle under the eyes, because dark frames make everything inside them look more defined. Then there is the texture of box dye. Flat, single-tone dark color has no variation, no dimension. Real hair, at any age, has many shades in it. Lighter on the surface where the sun hits, deeper underneath. A single flat color looks like a wig, and it reflects light in a hard, uniform way.

And finally there is the regrowth. Every three or four weeks, a stripe of silver or white appears at the part, and against very dark dye it is extremely visible. So we dye again, and again, and the color builds up on the lengths and becomes even darker and flatter.

French women over sixty have mostly stepped away from this. Not all at once, and not all in the same direction. Here are the seven shades they choose instead.

The first shade is soft silver.

This is the one that most people think of, and it is absolutely beautiful when it is done well. Soft silver is natural gray hair that has been cared for and enhanced so it looks bright and cool, almost like polished metal. Not yellowed, not dull. Silver hair reflects light, which is exactly what dark dye cannot do. That reflection bounces light up onto the face, which softens lines and brightens the skin.

Who does it suit? Most women whose natural gray has come in evenly and fairly light. It is especially lovely on women with cool or neutral skin, and with blue, gray, or green eyes, though I have seen it look wonderful on almost everyone.

How do French women get there? Usually with patience, and often with the help of a colorist. Some let the dye grow out with a short cut. Others ask for gradual lightening so the transition is less dramatic. Once they are silver, they keep it bright with a toning shampoo now and then, and they wear it with intention. A good cut, a little lipstick, a pair of earrings. Silver hair with a careless cut can look tired. Silver hair with a sharp, modern cut looks magnificent.

Picture a woman walking out of the Musée d'Orsay into the afternoon light, her short silver hair practically glowing against her navy coat. That is soft silver.

Now, if your gray has not come in evenly, and for many of us it does not, the next shade might be exactly what you need.

The second shade is the salt-and-pepper blend.

Many women find that their gray grows in unevenly. Silver at the temples, darker at the back, a white streak at the front. Rather than covering that, salt-and-pepper embraces it. It is the natural mix of dark and gray strands, sometimes softly blended by a colorist so it looks balanced and intentional.

Why does it work? Because it keeps some depth, which many women with naturally dark coloring need to avoid looking washed out, while adding enough light to soften the face. The variation in the strands gives the hair dimension and movement, exactly what flat dye lacks. And regrowth is almost invisible, because the hair is already a mix.

How do they ask for it? Usually a colorist will add very fine lowlights, slightly darker strands, in the areas that have gone very gray, and maybe fine highlights in the areas that are still very dark, so the two blend evenly. Over time, as more gray comes in, the lowlights are reduced. It is a lovely, gradual approach.

Imagine a woman sitting on a bench in the Square du Vert-Galant at the tip of the Île de la Cité, the river all around, her salt-and-pepper hair catching flecks of light as she reads. Natural and distinguished.

The third shade is bronde.

Bronde is just what it sounds like. A mix of brown and blonde. It is a soft, light brown base with blonde pieces woven through it, so the hair looks sun-touched and dimensional. For women who were naturally brunette and are not ready to go silver, this is often the first step French women take away from dark dye.

Why does it work? Because it lifts the color overall, which reduces that harsh contrast between hair and face. The blonde pieces reflect light and brighten the skin, while the brown keeps enough warmth that the face does not look washed out. And here is the clever part. As gray grows in at the roots, it blends into the lighter pieces. Regrowth becomes far less obvious, so you can go much longer between appointments.

How do you ask for it? Tell your colorist you want to move from dark brown to a softer, lighter brown with blended blonde pieces, especially around the face. If your hair has years of dark dye on it, be prepared for this to take a few sessions. Lifting old dark dye takes patience, and a good colorist will not rush it.

One more thing about bronde. It is very forgiving. If you are not sure yet whether you want to go all the way to silver someday, bronde keeps every door open. You can go a little lighter each visit, a little more blonde around the face, and let the gray do some of the work for you. Or you can stay right there for years. Many of the women I know in Paris have worn some version of bronde through their entire sixties, adjusting it gently as their natural color changes, and it has never once looked dated.

Picture a woman pausing at a newsstand on the Boulevard Saint-Michel, the morning sun catching the light strands in her shoulder-length bronde hair. She looks warm, relaxed, healthy.

Now, if bronde is a little too blonde for you, or if you love warmth, the fourth shade is going to be your favorite.

The fourth shade is honey highlights.

Honey is warm, golden, a soft amber shade. Honey highlights are fine pieces of this warm color placed through the hair, often on a light brown or dark blonde base. This is a lovely option for women with warm skin, golden or olive undertones, or brown or hazel eyes.

Why does it work after sixty? Because warmth gives life to the face. As skin loses some of its natural color with age, cool tones can sometimes make it look pale. Honey brings a glow back. Placed around the face, those warm highlights act a little like a reflector, bouncing a golden light onto the cheeks. It is incredibly flattering.

How do you ask for it? Fine, soft highlights in a warm honey or caramel tone, concentrated around the face and on the top layer, where the sun would naturally lighten the hair. Not chunky stripes. Not bright yellow. The goal is that people cannot quite tell where the highlights start and stop.

Imagine a woman at the counter of a fromagerie on the Rue Mouffetard, laughing with the shopkeeper, her honey-toned hair glowing under the warm shop lights. It looks like she spent the summer in Provence.

Now, we have covered three warm shades. The next one goes lighter, and it is one of the most elegant colors I know.

The fifth shade is champagne blonde.

Champagne is a pale, soft blonde, neither too golden nor too icy. Think of the color of the wine in the glass, a light gold with a hint of beige. On a mature woman, it is very elegant. It is lighter than bronde, softer than platinum, and it blends beautifully with gray.

Why does it work? Because a pale blonde close to the color of gray is the easiest way to make regrowth almost disappear. As the silver comes in at the roots, it simply melts into the champagne. And because it is soft rather than bright, it does not look harsh against mature skin. It brightens the face without fighting it.

Who does it suit? Women with light to medium skin, and those who had lighter hair when they were young. It can work on darker skin too, but your colorist may suggest a slightly deeper version.

How do you ask for it? A soft champagne blonde, with a warm beige tone rather than a yellow or a white. Ask for a slightly deeper root, called a root shadow or a smudged root, so the grow-out is soft and the color looks natural.

Picture a woman stepping out of a taxi in front of the Hôtel de Ville on a winter evening, the lights of the Christmas market behind her, her champagne bob shining softly under her black beret.

Now we come to the sixth shade, the one I promised you at the beginning. The gentlest way I know to leave dark dye behind.

The sixth shade is ash-beige lowlights.

Here is the problem with growing out dark dye. You have three, four, five inches of very dark color on the lengths, and silver or white coming in at the root. The contrast between them is enormous. Many women give up at this stage because it looks so harsh.

Ash-beige lowlights are a technique a colorist uses to bridge that gap. Instead of adding light to the dark hair, which can take many sessions and be hard on the hair, the colorist first lightens the overall dark color gently, then adds fine strands of an ash-beige shade. That is a cool, slightly gray-toned light brown, almost the color of driftwood or a mushroom. Those ash-beige strands sit between the dark and the silver. They soften the line where the regrowth meets the old color, and they make the whole head look blended rather than divided in two.

Why does it work? Because ash tones neutralize warmth, which keeps the lifted dark hair from turning orange or brassy. And beige keeps it soft enough not to look gray and flat. It is a peacemaker shade, if you like. It makes the transition feel calm.

How do you ask for it? Tell your colorist you are growing out dark dye and want to blend the regrowth gently. Ask about ash-beige lowlights or a blending technique, and be honest about how much time and money you want to spend. A good colorist will plan a path over several appointments.

I remember a friend of mine doing exactly this. She sat in a salon chair near the Place de Clichy every eight weeks for about a year. Each time she came out, the dark had receded a little and the silver had come forward. By the end, she was a beautiful soft gray, and there was never a single week she looked like she was in between.

Picture a woman walking along the arcades of the Rue de Rivoli in the rain, under the stone arches, her hair a soft mix of beige and silver that seems to shimmer in the gray light.

Before the last shade, I want to ask you a question. If you could choose one of these shades right now, without worrying about the process, which would it be? Silver, salt-and-pepper, bronde, honey, champagne, or ash-beige? Tell me in the comments.

The seventh shade is embraced white with gloss.

This is the shade that many French women eventually arrive at, after all the transitions. Pure white hair, or very pale silver-white, worn completely natural. But here is the key. They do not just leave it alone. They care for it. And the most important part of that care is a gloss.

White hair is beautiful, but without care it can look dull or take on a yellowish tint from sun, pollution, hard water, or even some styling products. A gloss is a sheer, shiny treatment that a salon can apply, or that you can find in at-home versions, that seals the surface of the hair and adds shine. Some glosses are clear, some have a slight tint, often a very soft violet or blue, to counteract any yellow. The result is white hair that shines like silk and reflects light all over the face.

Why does it work? Because shine is youth. Healthy young hair reflects light. When white hair is glossy and smooth, it looks vibrant and modern, never tired. And white hair, worn with confidence, is one of the most striking looks a woman can have. It makes eyes look brighter, it makes a red lip look extraordinary, and it pairs with almost every color you could wear.

How do they keep it up? A gloss every few weeks or months, depending on the product and your hair. A toning shampoo once a week if yellow creeps in. A good cut. And often, a little lipstick and a pair of earrings, because white hair loves contrast in the accessories.

I think of a woman I used to see at the Saturday market on the Boulevard Raspail. Snow-white hair in a sharp chin-length bob, always shining, a silk scarf in a bright color, a basket of vegetables on her arm. She was perhaps seventy-five. She stopped people in their tracks.

So let us bring it all together. Instead of flat jet black or box dark brown, French women over sixty choose soft silver, salt-and-pepper blended with lowlights, bronde with soft blonde pieces, warm honey highlights, elegant champagne blonde, ash-beige lowlights to bridge the transition, or embraced white with a glossy finish.

The common thread is light. Every one of these shades lets more light into the hair and onto the face. Every one has dimension, more than one tone, so it looks like real hair. And every one makes regrowth softer and less of a weekly worry. Dark dye fights against what our bodies are doing. These shades work with it.

A few practical notes before I let you go.

First, if you have been dyeing your hair dark for years, please do not try to lift it at home with a strong bleach. Old dark dye does not lighten evenly, and it can turn orange, patchy, and badly damaged. A colorist can lift it gradually and protect the hair in the process. This is one place where a professional is worth it.

Second, if you have a sensitive scalp, or have ever had a reaction to hair dye, mention it to your colorist and talk with your dermatologist or doctor. Many people develop sensitivities over time, and a patch test before any new color is always a good idea.

Third, think about your eyebrows. When your hair gets lighter, your brows may need to soften a little too. A tinted brow gel in a taupe or soft gray can bring them into harmony with the new color.

Fourth, think about the clothes you wear near your face. This surprised me. When my hair was dark, I wore a lot of black and burgundy, and they looked fine. When I went lighter, those same colors suddenly looked heavy, almost severe, right under my chin. Soft navy, camel, ivory, a dusty rose, a soft gray-blue, all of these came alive next to my lighter hair. You do not need to change your whole wardrobe. But try holding a few of your scarves up to your face by the window, and you will see which ones your new hair loves.

Fifth, be patient with yourself during the transition. There will be a week, maybe two, when you look in the mirror and wonder what you have done. Every woman I know who went lighter had that moment. My friend from the Place de Clichy called me once, almost in tears, because her hair looked, in her words, like a tabby cat. Three weeks later, after her next appointment, she sent me a photo from a terrace in the Marais and she looked radiant. The middle is the hardest part. It passes.

Sixth, protect your new color from the sun. Light hair, and especially gray and white hair, can turn brassy or yellow from strong sun exposure. A hat in summer, a scarf on windy days, and a leave-in product with some protection will help it stay bright. French women seem to have a hat or a silk scarf for every season, and I used to think it was only for elegance. Now I understand it is also very practical.

And one word about cost and time. The very dark dye we buy in a box is cheap and quick in the short term, but it needs doing so often. Many of these softer shades need fewer appointments once they are established, because regrowth is so much less visible. Over a year, many women find they actually spend less time in the salon chair, not more. That freedom is part of what makes these shades feel so liberating.

And finally, adjust your makeup to your new shade. Lighter hair often looks wonderful with a little more color on the cheeks and lips. A cream blush, a rosy balm, or a red lip. It is like redecorating a room. When the walls change color, the furniture may need to move a little too.

I want to leave you with a little thought. When I finally stopped dyeing my hair dark, I was terrified. I thought I would look older, that I would look like I had given up. Instead, the first thing my granddaughter said when she saw me, with that brutal honesty children have, was, Grandma, your face looks brighter. And she was right. My face had been there all along, it was just sitting in the shadow of my hair.
