The first time I truly understood Italian style, I was sitting at a small café table in a piazza in Rome, holding a cup of espresso that had gone cold because I had forgotten to drink it. I was watching a woman cross the square. She was perhaps seventy. Silver hair, cut just below the ear. A navy linen suit, the jacket unbuttoned, the trousers falling straight to a pair of brown leather loafers. A white shirt open at the collar. One gold bracelet. That was all. And yet every head at every table turned to look at her, and not one of us could have said exactly why.

I looked down at myself that afternoon. I was wearing a baggy T-shirt, a pair of white sneakers with a cotton sundress, and a little nylon crossbody bag bouncing against my hip. I was comfortable. I was practical. And next to her, I looked like I had given up.

That was many seasons ago. Since then I have spent long stretches of my life in Italy, in Rome, in Milan, in Florence, along the Amalfi coast, and I have spent an embarrassing number of hours watching women over sixty walk, shop, eat, and argue with their grocers. And I have noticed that there are certain things these women simply do not wear. Not because a rule tells them so, but because they understand, almost in their bones, what flatters a woman who has lived a little.

So today I am going to share ten things Italian women over sixty never wear, and exactly what they wear instead. Stay with me until the very end, because the last one, number ten, is the piece I see American women reach for most often, and it is the one that changes everything the moment you replace it. And somewhere in the middle, there is one simple swap that cost me nothing and made a shopkeeper in Florence call me signora with a whole new tone of voice. I will tell you that story when we get there.

Let's begin.

Number one. Sneakers with dresses.

I know. This one hurts, because it is everywhere, and it feels so easy. You put on a pretty dress, you want to walk all day, and you reach for your white sneakers. I did it for years.

But here is what happens to the eye. A dress, especially a soft, flowing dress, creates a long, graceful line from the shoulder to the hem. A chunky athletic shoe at the bottom of that line acts like a full stop in the wrong place. It cuts the leg, it adds visual weight to the ankle, and it makes the whole outfit look like two different people got dressed in the dark and shared the result. On a woman of twenty, it reads as playful. On a woman over sixty, the eye starts searching for the reason, and the reason it lands on is usually fatigue.

Italian women solve the comfort problem differently. They wear loafers. Soft leather loafers in brown, cognac, navy or black, with a thin, flexible sole and a little bit of shape at the toe. The loafer keeps the line of the leg clean, it looks intentional, and if it is good leather, it molds to the foot like a glove. With a dress, they will often choose a slightly lower vamp, so a bit of the top of the foot shows, which lengthens the leg.

Picture the passeggiata in a small town on the coast, early evening, when the whole town walks slowly up and down the main street. I watched a woman in a pale blue shirt-dress and tan loafers walk the length of that street three times, arm in arm with her sister, and she never once looked uncomfortable. She looked like she belonged to the evening.

Number two. Baggy T-shirts.

Now, I am not against a T-shirt. I am against the shapeless, oversized, slightly stretched T-shirt that hangs from the shoulders like a sheet on a clothesline.

The problem is fabric and line. Thin jersey that has been washed a hundred times clings in the wrong places and droops in others. It collapses around the bust, it pools around the waist, and it makes the shoulders look rounded. After sixty, our posture is something we have to protect, and a baggy T-shirt does the opposite. It tells the world we have stopped standing up straight, even when we haven't.

What do Italian women wear instead? A silk blouse. Not a fussy one. A simple, slightly loose silk blouse with a soft collar or a gentle V at the neck, in ivory, navy, sage, or a warm sand color. Silk catches the light. It moves when you move. And it reflects a little glow back up onto the face, which is something every woman over sixty can use.

If silk feels too delicate for your daily life, they reach for a heavier cotton blouse, or a fine-gauge knit with a clean neckline. The rule is the same. The fabric has to hold its own shape so you don't have to.

I remember a trattoria in Trastevere, a Sunday lunch, long tables, children running around, grandmothers presiding. The woman at the head of our neighboring table wore a rust-colored silk blouse tucked loosely into dark trousers. She served pasta to fourteen people and somehow never got a drop on that blouse. I am still not sure how.

Number three. Shapeless fleece.

The fleece jacket might be the most practical garment ever invented. It is warm, it is light, it is cheap. And it is the fastest way to look like you are about to go hiking when you are actually going to the bakery.

Fleece has a dull, flat surface that absorbs light. It has no structure at the shoulder, so it slopes. And it adds bulk around the middle without giving anything back in shape. Next to a face with fine lines and silver hair, that flat, synthetic texture can make the skin look tired.

Italian women of a certain age choose cashmere. Or a good wool and cashmere blend, if pure cashmere is beyond the budget. A cashmere cardigan, a long cashmere wrap coat, a crewneck sweater thrown over the shoulders. Cashmere has a soft, almost velvety surface that gives the face a gentle warmth. It drapes instead of puffs. And one good piece, cared for properly, lasts for years.

Picture Milan in November, the fog lifting off the canals, and women in camel cashmere coats walking into a café near the Duomo, unwinding their scarves as they step inside. Not a single fleece in sight. Just soft wool and quiet confidence.

Now, the next one is the one that taught me the biggest lesson of all.

Number four. Flip-flops in town.

On the beach, at the pool, on the boat, absolutely. But in the center of Rome or Florence, an Italian woman over sixty would no more wear rubber flip-flops than she would wear a bathrobe to the bank.

Why? Partly it is the sound. That slap, slap, slap on the cobblestones announces you before you arrive. Partly it is the shape. A flat strip of foam gives no support, so we shuffle, and shuffling ages the walk instantly. And partly it is the foot itself. After sixty, our feet deserve a little dignity, and a thin rubber sole with a plastic thong does not offer much.

Instead, they wear leather sandals. Flat or with a very small heel, with a strap that crosses the foot or wraps the ankle. Tan leather, gold leather, a soft black. The leather softens with wear, the sole is firm enough to walk properly, and there is no sound at all, just the quiet tap of a woman who knows where she is going.

And here is my little story from Florence. I had walked into a leather shop near the river in my flip-flops, and the woman behind the counter was polite, very polite, but she called me signora in that flat, tourist tone. A week later, I went back to buy a gift, wearing a pair of simple tan leather sandals I had bought at a market stall. Same woman. Same shop. And she greeted me like an old friend. Nothing else had changed. Just the shoes. I have never forgotten it.

Number five. The tiny nylon crossbody.

You know the one. It is small, it is black or gray nylon, it has three zippers and a strap that crosses the chest and makes everything underneath look rumpled.

The problem is twofold. Nylon has a shiny, cheap-looking surface that never really ages well. It pills, it fades, it bags. And the strap cutting diagonally across the body flattens the bust and wrinkles whatever beautiful blouse you chose. The bag sits right at the hip, which is exactly where most of us would rather not draw attention.

Italian women carry a leather tote. A structured one, in brown, black, cognac or a deep burgundy, with two handles that sit comfortably on the shoulder or in the crook of the arm. The leather gets better with every year. It holds its shape. And carried on the forearm, it pulls the posture upright, almost by itself.

Imagine Via Montenapoleone in Milan on a Saturday morning, women stepping out of shops with their leather totes over one arm, coats draped over their shoulders, walking with that unhurried pace. The bag is part of the silhouette, not an afterthought dangling at the hip.

We are halfway now. And I promised you that number ten is the one that changes everything. But first, there is one that I think many of you will be surprised by, because it seems like such a safe choice.

Number six. Beige everything.

Head-to-toe beige. Beige trousers, beige top, beige cardigan, beige shoes. It seems so elegant, so neutral, so safe. And on the right woman, in the right fabrics, a tonal look can be beautiful.

But for many of us over sixty, too much flat beige drains the face. As our hair silvers and our skin loses a little of its color, a pale, dusty beige from neck to toe can make us look washed out, almost blurry. When the fabrics are also flat cotton or polyester, there is nothing to catch the light, and the whole outfit becomes one soft, sleepy mass.

Italian women wear gold jewelry with their neutrals, and they choose warmer, richer tones. Instead of dusty beige, they choose camel, cognac, warm caramel. And then they add gold. A pair of gold hoops or knot earrings, a chunky gold chain, a thick gold bracelet. Gold near the face is like a little lamp. It brings warmth back into the skin and gives the eye a place to rest.

I think of a jewelry workshop on the old bridge in Florence, the light coming off the river and bouncing off all that gold in the windows. A woman next to me tried on a simple gold chain over her camel sweater, looked in the mirror, and said something in Italian that I understood perfectly without knowing the words. It was the sound of a woman who just saw herself come back to life.

Number seven. Cheap sunglasses.

Thin, flimsy plastic frames, the kind you buy at a gas station when you forgot yours at home. We have all done it.

But sunglasses sit right in the middle of the face. They are the first thing anyone sees. A thin, wobbly frame in shiny plastic makes the face look smaller and the features weaker. And very often the shape is wrong, too narrow or too sporty, which emphasizes lines rather than framing the eyes.

Italian women wear bold sunglasses. Thick acetate frames in tortoiseshell, deep brown or black. A soft cat-eye that lifts the outer corners. A square or slightly oversized shape that gives the face structure. These frames do something wonderful for a mature face. They create a strong line across the upper face that draws the eye upward, and the solid frame balances silver hair beautifully.

Picture the Amalfi coast, a terrace above the sea, a woman in a white shirt and enormous tortoiseshell sunglasses reading the newspaper with a coffee. She looks up when the waiter arrives, and she looks like a film still. That is the power of one good pair of frames.

There is one more thing I have noticed about how they wear them. Italian women do not push sunglasses up into their hair and leave them there all afternoon like a headband. When they go indoors, they fold them and slip them into the neckline of the shirt, or into a soft case in the tote. It is a small gesture, but it keeps the hair neat and the frames protected, and it treats the sunglasses as something precious rather than something disposable. That is really the whole philosophy in a single movement. Buy one good thing, and then take care of it as if it matters, because it does. When you choose your next pair, try them on in daylight, near a window, and look at whether the top of the frame follows the line of your eyebrows. If it does, you have found a friend for many summers. And by the way, I have so much more to say about sunglasses, I could fill a whole video. Maybe I will.

Number eight. Polyester blouses.

I know polyester has improved. Some modern blends are lovely. But the shiny, slippery polyester blouse with the busy print, the kind that never wrinkles and never breathes, is something Italian women of a certain age simply avoid.

Polyester has a particular kind of shine, a hard, plastic gleam, that the eye reads as inexpensive. It traps heat, so in a warm city you feel damp within an hour. It often clings to the back and the arms. And busy prints on a slippery surface tend to make the body look larger and the outfit look dated.

Instead, they wear crisp white shirts. A real cotton shirt, slightly oversized or perfectly fitted, with a proper collar. Sleeves rolled to the forearm. The top button or two open. Tucked into trousers or a skirt, or worn open over a simple top. The crisp white collar near the face works like a reflector in a photographer's studio. It lights up the skin, it sharpens the jawline, and it looks clean and fresh in any city, at any age.

Picture a Saturday market in a Roman neighborhood, stalls full of artichokes and lemons, and a woman in her late sixties in a white shirt, sleeves rolled, testing tomatoes with complete seriousness. Her shirt has a few wrinkles from the morning, and it doesn't matter at all, because cotton wrinkles honestly. It looks lived in, not tired.

Two more to go. And yes, the last one is coming. Stay with me.

Number nine. Ankle socks.

The little white sport sock peeking out above a shoe, or worse, a sock with a sandal. This is a small detail, but small details are where Italian women live.

The ankle sock creates a harsh horizontal line at the narrowest part of the leg. It interrupts the line between trouser and shoe, or between skin and shoe, and the eye stops right there. White cotton sport socks also carry the feeling of the gym, of practicality, of something we wear when we aren't thinking.

Italian women do one of two things. In warm weather, they go bare-legged with leather sandals or loafers, or they wear invisible liner socks that no one ever sees. In cooler weather, they wear fine knee-high socks or trouser socks in a color that matches the trouser or the shoe, navy with navy, gray with charcoal, camel with brown. The line from waist to floor stays unbroken. The leg looks longer. The outfit looks finished.

Picture a narrow street in Bologna under the porticoes, a woman stepping off a curb in charcoal wool trousers and black loafers, and between them, nothing but a whisper of matching sock. You wouldn't notice it. That's the point.

And now, number ten. The one I promised you. The one that changes everything.

Number ten. Oversized hoodies.

I see this one more than any other on women over sixty when they travel. The big, soft, oversized hoodie. It's comfortable on the plane, it's cozy in the hotel, and slowly it becomes the thing we wear everywhere.

But think about what a hoodie does. The hood sits behind the neck and adds bulk right where we want length. It pushes the head forward visually, which can make the posture look stooped. The heavy sweatshirt fabric hangs straight down from the widest point of the body and hides any trace of the waist. The kangaroo pocket adds bulk exactly at the tummy. And the whole garment says, in a very loud voice, I am on my way to the couch.

What do Italian women over sixty wear instead? Tailored trousers. I know that sounds like a strange answer for a hoodie, but stay with me. The hoodie is really a symptom. It is the uniform of a woman who has decided comfort and elegance are enemies. The Italian answer to that is the tailored trouser, a high-waisted, wide or straight leg trouser in wool, crepe, or linen, with a clean front and a hem that just brushes the top of the shoe.

Once you have a pair of trousers like that, everything on top changes. You don't want a hoodie anymore. You want a fine knit, a silk blouse, a crisp shirt, a soft blazer. The trouser sets the standard, and the rest of the wardrobe rises to meet it. And here's the secret: a good tailored trouser with an elastic or side-adjusted waist can be every bit as comfortable as sweatpants. Comfort was never the problem. Shape was.

And along with tailored trousers come two more friends that you will see on every elegant Italian woman over sixty: the linen suit and the belted dress. A linen suit in summer, soft, slightly crumpled, in navy, sand, olive or white, worn with a simple shirt and loafers. A belted dress, cinched gently at the natural waist with a leather belt, giving shape without tightness. These are the pieces that replace the hoodie, the T-shirt, the fleece and the sneakers all at once.

Let me give you one last picture. Florence, early evening. The Piazza della Signoria is golden, the stone glowing in that last light. And a woman in her seventies walks across it in wide cream trousers, a navy cashmere sweater over her shoulders, gold earrings and a brown leather tote. Her husband walks beside her in a linen jacket. They are not rich, necessarily. They are not trying to impress anyone. They are simply dressed for the life they are living, and the life they are living looks beautiful.

That is what I want you to take from this video. Italian women over sixty are not following rules. They are making choices. They choose fabrics that catch the light instead of absorbing it. They choose shapes that support their posture instead of hiding it. They choose fewer, better pieces, and they wear them often, and they wear them with joy.

Let me give you the ten swaps again, quickly, so you can remember them. Instead of sneakers with dresses, loafers. Instead of baggy T-shirts, silk blouses. Instead of shapeless fleece, cashmere knits. Instead of flip-flops in town, leather sandals. Instead of the tiny nylon crossbody, a leather tote. Instead of beige everything, warm neutrals and gold jewelry. Instead of cheap sunglasses, bold acetate frames. Instead of polyester blouses, crisp white shirts. Instead of ankle socks, a clean, unbroken line. And instead of oversized hoodies, tailored trousers, linen suits and belted dresses.

You don't need to change all ten tomorrow. Pick one. Just one. Maybe it's the loafers. Maybe it's the white shirt. Maybe it's a single gold chain. Wear it this week and notice how you feel when you walk down the street. Notice whether you stand a little taller. Notice whether someone, somewhere, calls you signora in that warm, respectful way.
