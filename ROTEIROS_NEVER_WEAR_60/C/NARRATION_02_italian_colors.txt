I want to tell you about a gray cardigan. It was soft, it was practical, and I had worn it on almost every trip for six years. One October morning in Milan, I put it on in my hotel room, looked in the mirror, and saw a woman who looked tired. Not old exactly. Just faded, as if someone had turned down the color on a photograph.

That same afternoon, I sat in a café near the Brera district and watched a woman about my age walk in wearing a terracotta knit sweater, olive trousers and a pair of brown loafers. Her hair was completely white. Her skin had the same lines mine has. And she glowed. She looked warm, awake, alive. The difference between us was not age, or money, or genetics. It was color.

I went back to the hotel, folded that gray cardigan, and put it at the bottom of my suitcase. I have barely worn it since.

So today I want to talk about why so many of us over sixty drift toward gray, why it so often works against us, and the eight colors I have seen Italian women over sixty wear instead, again and again, in Rome, in Milan, in Florence, and along the coast. Some of these colors will surprise you. And color number eight, the last one, is the one I think almost every woman watching can wear, regardless of her skin or hair, and it is the color I wish someone had told me about twenty years ago. I'll save it for the end.

But first, let's be honest about gray.

Gray feels safe. It goes with everything. It doesn't call attention. And as our hair silvers, many of us think gray is the natural, harmonious choice. Gray hair, gray sweater, gray coat. Neat and tidy.

Here is the problem I have observed. Many mid grays, especially the flat, heathered gray of a sweatshirt or a basic cardigan, have very little warmth in them. When you place a cool, dull color right under the face, it reflects that dullness back up onto the skin. Any shadows under the eyes look deeper. Any redness looks more obvious. And if your hair is also gray, the whole picture becomes one soft, monochrome blur with no point of focus. The eye has nowhere to land.

There is also a question of texture. Gray is very often the color of cheap, practical fabrics. Jersey, fleece, sweatshirt cotton, polyester blends. So gray carries the feeling of utility, of something we put on without thinking. The Italian women I have watched rarely dress without thinking. Even their simplest outfits feel chosen.

Now, I am not saying gray is forbidden. A rich charcoal wool coat, a pearl gray silk blouse, these can be beautiful. But the everyday dull gray, the one that has quietly taken over so many closets, is the one I want you to replace. Let's look at what to replace it with.

Color number one. Terracotta.

Terracotta is the color of the roofs in Florence, the color of old flower pots on a Roman balcony, the color of the earth in Tuscany after the rain. It is a warm, earthy orange-brown with a little red in it, and it is one of the most flattering colors I know for a mature face.

Why does it work? Because it contains warmth, and warmth near the face reads as health. Terracotta echoes the natural pink and peach in the skin and brings it forward. It makes the cheeks look rosier, the eyes look brighter, and it gives silver or white hair a beautiful contrast without being harsh.

How do Italian women wear it? Most often in knitwear. A terracotta crewneck sweater in fine merino or cashmere. A terracotta linen shirt in summer. A long terracotta skirt with a cream blouse. They pair it with denim, with cream, with chocolate brown, or with olive, which happens to be our next color. If a full sweater feels like too much, start with a terracotta scarf near the face.

Picture Florence just before sunset, looking out over the city from a hill above the river, all those terracotta roofs glowing in the evening light. Now picture a woman on that terrace in a terracotta knit, and you understand that she is not wearing a color. She is wearing the city.

Color number two. Olive.

Olive green is one of those colors that seems quiet, but it does so much work. It's the color of the leaves on the olive trees that cover the hills of Puglia and Tuscany, a soft, gray-green with a warm undertone.

Here is the trick. Olive gives you all the calm and neutrality of gray, but it has life in it. It is a living neutral. It flatters warm and neutral skin beautifully, and it looks extraordinary with silver hair, because it adds a little green depth that makes the hair look intentionally bright instead of faded.

Italian women wear olive in trousers, in utility-style jackets with a feminine cut, in long linen dresses, in suede. An olive suede jacket over a cream sweater with gold earrings is, to my eye, one of the most elegant outfits a woman over sixty can wear. Olive trousers with a white shirt and tan loafers is another. Olive is also wonderful because it hides a little wear and travel, and it pairs effortlessly with brown leather.

Imagine a country lunch in an agriturismo, long tables under a pergola, bread and oil on the table. Every woman there seems to be wearing some shade of olive, sage or khaki, and they blend into the landscape in the most graceful way.

Color number three. Ivory.

Now, you might be thinking, ivory? Isn't that just another neutral? Yes, and no. Ivory is a warm, creamy white, softer than pure optic white, and it is one of the secrets of mature Italian elegance.

Pure bright white can sometimes be harsh against older skin. It can emphasize every line and make the skin look a little gray by contrast. Ivory has a touch of yellow or cream, and that softness flatters. It gives you the freshness of white, the light-reflecting quality that brightens the face, but without the shock.

Italian women wear ivory in winter as much as in summer. Ivory cable knits. Ivory wool trousers. An ivory silk blouse under a camel coat. An ivory linen dress in July. And they love ivory on ivory, a tonal look, in different textures, wool with silk, linen with cotton, which looks incredibly luxurious without a single logo anywhere.

Picture a winter morning in Milan, a woman in ivory wool trousers and a matching ivory turtleneck under a long camel coat, crossing the square in front of the cathedral. Against the pale stone, she looks like light itself. I'll be honest, I could talk about white and ivory for a whole video, and I just might, because there are mistakes with white that make it look cheap, and I want to tell you about every one.

Before we go further, I'd love to know something. Are you a gray wearer, like I was? How many gray pieces are hanging in your closet right now? Tell me the number in the comments. Now, the next color is the one that will feel the boldest. But please don't skip it, because I've seen it transform women who thought they could never wear a bright color.

Color number four. Cobalt blue.

Cobalt is a deep, saturated, vivid blue. Not navy. Not baby blue. The blue of a Mediterranean ceramic plate, the blue painted on the tiles in the towns of the Amalfi coast.

Why would a woman over sixty wear something so bright? Because strong, clear color in the right place can lift the whole face. Cobalt has enough intensity to stand up next to silver or white hair, and it makes the whites of the eyes look whiter and blue or gray eyes look extraordinary. Where dull gray blurs, cobalt defines.

Italian women of a certain age wear cobalt in a few clever ways. A cobalt silk scarf knotted at the neck against a navy or white outfit. A cobalt cashmere sweater with white trousers. A cobalt linen shirt in summer. They rarely wear it head to toe. Usually it is the single bold note in an otherwise calm outfit, and they place it near the face.

Imagine the coast in late summer, a little town of pastel houses stacked up the cliff, and down by the harbor a woman in white trousers and a cobalt shirt is buying fish from a boat. She is the brightest thing on the dock, and she knows it, and she doesn't mind at all.

Color number five. Lemon yellow.

Now I can already hear some of you. Yellow? At my age? Yes. Lemon yellow, the soft, clear yellow of the lemons that grow on the terraces around Sorrento and Amalfi.

I'll admit, yellow is the color I was most afraid of. But here is what I learned watching Italian women wear it. The key is the shade. A mustard or a dusty yellow can be tricky. But a clean, slightly soft lemon yellow, a bit pale, has a wonderfully cheerful effect. It reflects sunshine onto the face. It looks joyful. And joy, I have come to believe, is the most youthful thing we can wear.

How do they wear it? In summer especially. A lemon yellow linen shirt with white trousers. A pale yellow cotton dress with tan sandals. A yellow cardigan over a navy dress. Or simply a yellow scarf, a yellow bag, a yellow pair of espadrilles. If you are nervous, start with an accessory, then move a little closer to the face.

Picture a little shop in Positano with crates of lemons stacked outside, lemon soaps, lemon ceramics, lemon everything, and the woman who runs it wearing a pale yellow linen dress and gold earrings. She looks like summer itself. One more tip about yellow: keep it away from yellowed or cream fabrics in the same outfit, and pair it instead with crisp white, navy, or denim. That clean contrast is what keeps lemon looking fresh rather than faded, and it is how I finally learned to wear it without feeling like a highlighter pen. And customers buy from her because she makes them happy just by standing there.

Color number six. Burgundy.

Now we move into the deeper colors, the ones for autumn and winter, for evenings, for dinners in candlelit trattorias. Burgundy is the color of red wine, a deep red with a touch of purple and brown.

Burgundy does something wonderful for a mature woman. It gives the depth and seriousness of black or charcoal, but it has warmth and blood in it. It flatters the lips, it brings color to the cheeks, and it looks especially rich in luxurious fabrics, velvet, cashmere, silk, wool crepe.

Italian women wear burgundy in coats, in sweaters, in long skirts, in leather bags and shoes. A burgundy cashmere sweater with gray flannel trousers, notice, gray can return when it is next to a living color. A burgundy velvet blazer for dinner. A burgundy leather bag with a camel coat. And very often, a burgundy lipstick or a soft wine-colored lip that echoes the clothes.

Picture a trattoria in Rome in December, rain outside, steam on the windows, red wine on the table. A woman in her seventies sitting by the window in a burgundy turtleneck, gold chain, white hair swept up, laughing with her friends. The color of her sweater matches the wine in her glass, and the whole scene looks like a painting.

We have two more colors, and the eighth, the one I promised you, is coming. But first, a neutral that every Italian woman seems to own.

Color number seven. Camel.

If there is one color that defines Italian elegance over sixty, it might be camel. The warm, golden tan of a good wool coat.

Camel replaces gray beautifully because it does the same job. It is neutral, it goes with everything, it never shouts. But it is warm. It contains gold and brown and a little red, and that warmth flatters almost every skin tone. It makes silver hair look shiny. It makes the face look sunlit even in the depth of winter.

How do Italian women wear camel? The camel coat is legendary. Long, simple, often belted or wrap-style, worn over everything from jeans to evening dresses. But camel also appears in knitwear, in trousers, in suede shoes and leather bags. Camel with ivory is a classic. Camel with navy is elegant. Camel with black is dramatic. Camel with burgundy is rich and autumnal. And camel with gold jewelry is something close to perfection.

Picture Via Montenapoleone in Milan on a cold January afternoon. I once counted, very unscientifically, just by watching from a café table, and it felt as though every second woman walking past was wearing some version of a camel coat. Different lengths, different cuts, but all that warm golden color moving through the gray street like sunlight.

And now. Color number eight. The one I promised. The one I wish I had discovered twenty years ago.

Color number eight. Sea blue.

Not cobalt, not navy, not turquoise exactly. Sea blue. The soft, deep, slightly green-tinged blue of the Mediterranean on a clear day, when you look down from a cliff into the water. A blue that sits somewhere between teal and denim, softer than cobalt, richer than pale blue.

Why is this the one I believe almost every woman can wear? Because it balances warm and cool. It has enough blue to flatter cool skin and enough green to flatter warm skin. It is not so bright that it overwhelms, not so dark that it drags you down. It makes blue eyes bluer, green eyes greener, brown eyes deeper. And next to silver or white hair, sea blue creates a contrast that looks fresh and calm at the same time, like a breeze.

And here is something I noticed. Sea blue is the most direct replacement for gray. Where you used to reach for a gray sweater, reach for a sea blue sweater. Where you would have chosen a gray scarf, choose sea blue. Where you would have bought a gray cardigan for travel, buy a sea blue one. It has the same quiet, easy, wear-with-anything quality, but it brings your face to life instead of fading it.

Italian women wear it in linen in summer, in cotton knits, in cashmere in winter, in scarves all year long. Sea blue with white is the classic coastal look. Sea blue with camel is unexpected and gorgeous. Sea blue with olive feels like the countryside meeting the coast. And sea blue with gold jewelry is simply beautiful.

Let me paint one final picture. The coast, early evening, a little restaurant on the rocks right above the water. A woman in her late sixties sits at the edge of the terrace in a sea blue linen shirt, white trousers, gold hoops, her white hair loose in the breeze. The sea behind her is exactly the color of her shirt. And when the waiter brings the wine, he looks at her and says, bellissima. She smiles as if she has heard it a thousand times, and maybe she has.

That was the moment, sitting nearby, when I finally understood. Italian women over sixty are not dressing against their age. They are dressing with the colors of their world. The earth, the olives, the stone, the sea, the lemons, the wine. Colors with warmth, with life, with memory. And they bring those colors close to their faces, so that every time someone looks at them, they see light.

Let me give you a few practical tips before we finish.

First, start near the face. You don't need to replace your whole wardrobe. Replace the gray scarf, the gray top, the gray cardigan, the things that sit closest to your face. Keep your gray trousers if you love them, and wear terracotta, burgundy or sea blue on top.

Second, test colors in daylight. Stand by a window with no makeup and hold a piece of fabric under your chin. Look at your eyes and your skin, not the fabric. If your face looks brighter and fresher, that color is your friend. If your face looks tired, put it back.

Third, use fabric to your advantage. The same color in a dull cotton jersey and in a lustrous silk or cashmere will look completely different. Richer fabrics carry color more beautifully.

Fourth, pair your new color with a warm neutral. Camel, ivory, chocolate brown, navy. These neutrals frame the color and make it look expensive and intentional.

And fifth, finish with gold. A pair of gold earrings with any of these eight colors will lift the whole look.

Now let me show you how these colors work together, because I think this is where so many of us get stuck. We buy one beautiful terracotta sweater, bring it home, and then we don't know what to wear it with, so it sits in the drawer and we go back to gray.

Here is the way I have seen Italian women build a small, living palette. They choose two warm neutrals as the foundation. Usually camel and ivory, sometimes navy and chocolate brown. These are the coats, the trousers, the skirts, the shoes and the bags. Then they choose two or three of the living colors as the voices of the wardrobe. A woman might choose olive and terracotta for autumn, burgundy and camel for winter, sea blue and lemon for summer. Everything in the closet can be worn with everything else, because the neutrals are warm and the colors all come from the same landscape.

Let me give you three outfits so you can picture it. For a winter morning, ivory wool trousers, a burgundy cashmere sweater, a camel coat, brown suede loafers and small gold hoops. For a spring afternoon, olive trousers, an ivory silk shirt, a terracotta scarf knotted loosely, and a tan leather tote. For a summer evening, white linen trousers, a sea blue linen shirt with the sleeves rolled, gold sandals, and a lemon yellow clutch. Not one of these outfits contains gray, and not one of them is difficult.

I tried this myself one spring in Rome. I packed a small suitcase with only camel, ivory, olive and terracotta, plus one sea blue scarf. Two weeks, and I never once felt I had nothing to wear. One morning, I was waiting for a tram near the river, and an older gentleman standing next to me said something kind about my scarf. I won't pretend that it didn't make my whole day. But what struck me more was how I felt when I caught my reflection in the tram window. I didn't look faded. I looked like myself, only brighter.

And one more thought about makeup, because color in clothing and color on the face talk to each other. When you move from gray to warmer colors, you may find that a little warmth on the cheeks and lips suddenly looks more natural. A soft peach or rose blush, a lipstick in a muted brick, berry or wine tone. Many of the Italian women I admire wear very little makeup, but they almost always have some color on the lips, and it ties the face to the clothes.
