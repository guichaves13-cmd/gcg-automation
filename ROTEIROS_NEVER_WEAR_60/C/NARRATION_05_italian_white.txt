Mistake number one. See-through, thin white.

This is the mistake from my story, and I think it's the most common one of all. Thin white cotton, thin white jersey, thin white linen, sheer white gauze. In the shop, under soft lighting, it looks lovely. Outdoors in strong sun, it becomes a window.

Why does thin white look cheap? First, transparency. When fabric is so thin that the body and the underwear show through, the garment stops looking like clothing and starts looking like a lining. Second, thin white fabrics wrinkle in a limp, tired way rather than a crisp one. Third, they cling, especially in heat, and clinging white shows every line of the body. And fourth, thin white loses its shape quickly in the wash, so after a few weeks it begins to look gray and stretched.

What do Italian women wear instead? Heavyweight cotton.

A white shirt in a thick, dense cotton poplin or oxford. A white T-shirt in a heavy, substantial jersey that holds its shape. White trousers in a thick cotton twill or a heavier linen. The rule is simple. Hold the fabric up to the light. If you can see your hand clearly through it, it's too thin to wear alone.

Heavyweight cotton does something beautiful. It holds its own shape, so the garment skims the body instead of clinging. It reflects light evenly, which brightens the face. It wrinkles crisply, which looks honest and lived in. And it stays white longer.

Picture a Sunday morning in a Roman piazza, the bells ringing, and a woman in her seventies walks out of church in a heavy white cotton shirt tucked into navy trousers, a gold brooch at the collar. Her shirt is so crisp it almost seems to glow against the old stone. Nothing about it is transparent. Everything about it is clear.

There's one more benefit of heavyweight fabric that I didn't understand until I was in my sixties. It's kinder to the body. A thin white fabric follows every curve, every soft place, every line of a bra strap. A heavier fabric drapes over them, smoothing the silhouette like a gentle hand. That's why a dense white shirt can look so flattering on a woman who has never felt comfortable in white before. It isn't hiding the body. It's simply refusing to report every detail. And when you are shopping, feel the fabric between your fingers. If it feels substantial and slightly cool, you have found the right kind. If it feels flimsy and warm, keep looking.

Mistake number two. Optic white against pale skin.

Now, not all whites are the same. There's optic white, that very bright, slightly bluish white you see on brand new sneakers or printer paper. And then there's a whole range of softer whites, ivory, cream, chalk, off-white, ecru.

Optic white can be beautiful on deep or golden skin, where the contrast is striking. But on many women over sixty, especially women with fair or pale skin and silver hair, optic white can be harsh. It's so bright and so cool that it can make the skin look a little gray or sallow by comparison. It emphasizes every shadow under the eyes. And it can make silver hair look yellowish instead of bright.

Italian women of a certain age very often choose ivory instead. A warm, soft white with a touch of cream or yellow in it. Ivory flatters mature skin. It gives you all the freshness and light of white, but with warmth, so the skin looks rosy and luminous rather than pale. It looks beautiful with silver hair. And it often looks more expensive, perhaps because it suggests natural, undyed fibers like silk, wool and linen.

The test is easy. Stand by a window in daylight, hold a piece of optic white and a piece of ivory under your chin, one after the other, and look at your skin. Very often, the ivory wins.

Picture Florence in late spring, a woman crossing the old bridge over the river in an ivory silk blouse and ivory trousers, the jewelry shops glittering on either side, the river glowing below. In that warm golden light, her ivory looks like candlelight. Optic white would have looked like a hospital.

Mistake number three. White with white sneakers.

This is one I see everywhere. White trousers, a white top, and white athletic sneakers. It seems so coordinated. All white, head to toe.

But here's the problem. White sneakers are a sporty object with a very particular shape, thick, rubbery, padded. When you put them at the end of an elegant white outfit, they don't blend in, they stand out. They pull the eye to the feet. And because they're bulky, they make the feet look larger and break the line of the leg. The whole outfit starts to look like a tennis uniform. And if the sneakers are even slightly dirty or scuffed, against all that white, it shows immediately.

Italian women pair white with camel. Camel or tan leather loafers. Cognac sandals. A tan suede espadrille. A camel belt. A tan leather bag. And often, a camel knit thrown over the shoulders or a camel coat in cooler weather.

Why camel? Because it's warm. It brings out the warmth in ivory and cream. It grounds the outfit, giving it a base. It makes white look rich instead of clinical. And the combination of white and camel, sometimes with a touch of gold, is one of the most classic, effortless pairings in Italian style. It looks right on the coast, in the city, at lunch, at dinner, at any age.

Picture a beach club on the coast, rows of striped umbrellas, and an older woman walking along the wooden boardwalk in white linen trousers, a white shirt, tan leather sandals, a camel straw bag and a camel leather belt. She's dressed in two colors, and she looks like a magazine cover.

Mistake number four. Wrinkled polyester.

Wait, you might say, doesn't polyester resist wrinkles? Some of it does. But many polyester and synthetic blends, especially the thinner ones, crease in a particular way. Sharp, shiny, stubborn creases that don't fall out, that look like crumpled plastic rather than soft fabric. And synthetic white has another problem. Over time, it often turns slightly gray or yellow, and it can hold onto odors and stains in a way natural fibers don't.

The shine of polyester in white is also unforgiving. In bright light, it reflects in a hard, glassy way. And because white shows everything, every pull, every pill, every shiny patch becomes visible.

Italian women choose cream linen instead. Natural linen in cream, ivory or off-white.

Now yes, linen wrinkles too. Of course it does. But linen wrinkles honestly. It creases softly, in a way that looks relaxed and natural. In Italy, a slightly rumpled linen shirt is considered elegant, not careless. And cream linen has a beautiful, slightly irregular texture that catches light softly. It breathes in the heat. It gets softer and more beautiful with every wash.

The secret Italian women seem to know is this. Iron linen once, when it's slightly damp, or hang it in a steamy bathroom, and then simply let it live. Don't fight every crease. The creases are part of the charm.

Picture an elegant hotel terrace in a seaside town, white tablecloths, lemon trees in pots, and a woman in her late sixties in a cream linen suit having lunch alone with a book. Her jacket is creased at the elbows, her trousers creased at the knees, and she looks like the most relaxed, elegant person on the entire coast.

Mistake number five. The wrong underwear.

And here it is. The mistake I told you about. The one nobody else can see, but everybody notices.

Many of us believe that under white clothes, we should wear white underwear. It seems logical. White under white. But here's what actually happens. White underwear shows through white clothing. It creates a visible outline, a bright shape under the fabric, because it's whiter and brighter than your skin. The edges of a white bra, the lines of white briefs, they glow right through even fairly thick fabrics, especially in sunlight.

And then there's the other version. Black or colored underwear under white, which of course is even more visible. Or lace that creates a texture under smooth fabric.

Italian women wear nude underwear. That is, underwear in a shade close to their own skin tone. Not necessarily beige, but whatever tone matches your skin. Smooth, seamless, without lace or heavy seams. Under white, a nude bra and nude briefs almost disappear, because there's no contrast. The white fabric looks clean and uninterrupted.

This is such a small detail, and yet it changes everything. White clothing worn over the right underwear looks expensive and polished. The same clothing over the wrong underwear looks cheap, no matter how much it cost.

It's also worth thinking about slips. A nude slip under a white linen dress or skirt can make even a slightly sheer fabric perfectly modest.

Mistake number six. White denim too tight.

White jeans are a summer classic, and I love them. But there's a particular version that so many of us buy, skinny, stretchy, and tight. And on a mature body, tight white denim can be very unforgiving.

Here's why. White reflects light, which makes things look slightly larger than darker colors do. When white denim is tight, it pulls across the thighs and hips, and every curve, every bump, every pocket shape is highlighted. Stretch denim also tends to lose its shape during the day, so by afternoon it's baggy at the knees and still tight at the hips. And thin stretch white denim can show the pockets and seams through the fabric.

Italian women wear straight white jeans instead. A straight or slightly wide leg, in a thicker denim with less stretch, high-waisted, falling straight from the hip to the ankle. Often cropped just above the ankle bone or with a small turn-up at the hem.

The straight leg skims instead of clinging. The thicker denim holds its shape and doesn't show the pockets. The high waist gives a smooth line through the hip. And the straight shape creates a long, elegant column from the waist to the shoe.

They wear them with a navy striped knit, a crisp white or blue shirt, a camel cashmere sweater, or a soft linen blouse. With loafers, leather sandals or espadrilles. It's one of the most versatile outfits in any Italian woman's summer wardrobe.

She has a coffee in one hand and a small leather bag in the other, and she looks like the easiest, most effortless thing in the world. That ease comes from the cut.

Mistake number seven. All white without texture.

The all-white outfit. White trousers, white top, white cardigan, all in the same smooth, flat fabric. It sounds elegant. It can look elegant. But when every piece is the same flat white, the eye has nowhere to go. The outfit looks like a uniform. It can make the body look like one large white shape. And it can look flat and slightly clinical, like a nurse or a chef.

Here's the secret. Italian women mix textures.

When they wear all white, or all ivory, or all cream, they choose pieces with different surfaces. A chunky cable knit sweater with smooth linen trousers. A silk blouse with cotton twill trousers. A cream linen jacket over a fine cotton top. A crochet or textured knit with soft denim. A woven straw bag, a suede belt, a raffia sandal.

Each texture catches the light differently. Silk shines softly. Linen has a slightly irregular, matte surface. Wool creates shadows in its ribs and cables. Cotton is crisp. Straw and raffia add natural texture at the edges. When you combine them in one color, the outfit comes alive. It looks rich, layered, thoughtful and expensive, even if every piece was simple.

And then they add warmth with one or two accents. Camel shoes. A gold necklace. Tortoiseshell sunglasses. A brown leather belt. These accents frame the white and keep it from looking cold.

Let me give you a few practical tips for wearing white after sixty.

First, check the weight. Hold the fabric up to the light. If you can see through it clearly, wear something under it or choose a heavier piece.

Second, choose your white. Test optic white and ivory under your chin in daylight. Most mature skin glows in ivory or cream.

Third, choose the right underwear. Nude, seamless, matched to your skin tone. This one change transforms every white garment you own.

Fourth, pair with warm accents. Camel, cognac, tan, gold, tortoiseshell. They make white look rich.

Fifth, mix textures. Knit, linen, silk, cotton, straw. Never all one surface.

Sixth, choose shape over tightness. Straight, relaxed cuts look more elegant in white than tight ones.

And seventh, take care of your white. Wash it often and gently, keep it away from harsh chemicals that can yellow it, store it clean, and have a little stain-removing pen in your bag for emergencies.
