On the train from Rome to Florence, I once sat across from a woman of perhaps seventy-five who had a small tan leather bag on her lap. It was not new. The corners were softly worn, the handle had darkened where her hand had held it for years, and the brass clasp had the gentle shine that only comes from being opened and closed thousands of times. As the train left the station, she opened it, took out a pair of reading glasses, a folded newspaper, and a small linen handkerchief, and closed it again with a little click. Everything she needed, and nothing she didn't.

Beside me, on the seat, was my own bag. An enormous canvas tote, so heavy that my shoulder ached, stuffed with a water bottle, two guidebooks, a sweater, an umbrella, a makeup bag, a phone charger, snacks, receipts, and somewhere at the bottom, I hoped, my train ticket. When the conductor came, I spent two full minutes digging for it while she watched me with a kind, slightly amused expression.

When I finally found it, she leaned across and said, in careful English, the bag carries you, or you carry the bag? Then she smiled and went back to her newspaper.

I've thought about that question for years. And over many seasons in Rome, in Milan, in Florence and along the coast, I've watched how Italian women over sixty choose and carry their handbags. And I've noticed there are five mistakes they simply don't make.

So today, I'll share those five handbag mistakes, and exactly what they carry instead. I'll tell you about the bag shape that I think is the most flattering for a mature woman's posture, and why it changes the way you walk. And at the end, I'll share the one evening bag I believe every woman over sixty should own, the one I reach for more than any other. Stay with me.

First, why do handbags matter so much after sixty?

A handbag is not just an accessory. It's part of the silhouette. It changes how we stand, how we walk and how we move. A heavy bag pulls one shoulder down. A long strap bouncing at the hip widens the hips. A backpack rounds the shoulders. And a bag is something people see from every angle, from across the street, across the room, across the table. As we get older, our shoulders, backs and necks may be more sensitive, so a bag has to be kind to the body as well as beautiful. The Italian women I've watched seem to understand both.

Mistake number one. The heavy oversized tote.

This is the bag from my train story. A huge, slouchy tote, usually in canvas or soft synthetic material, big enough to carry your whole life, and often carrying it.

Why does it age us? First, the weight. An oversized tote invites us to fill it, and once it's full, it's heavy. Carried on one shoulder, it pulls that shoulder down and tilts the whole body. Over a day, the posture collapses to one side. Second, the shape. A slouchy, shapeless bag hangs against the hip and adds width right where we least want it. Third, the disorder. When you have to dig for your keys or your glasses, you look flustered, and flustered looks older. And fourth, many large canvas or synthetic totes lose their shape quickly and start to look tired.

What do Italian women carry instead? A structured leather top-handle bag.

A medium-sized bag in leather, with a firm, defined shape, flat bottom, straight sides, and one or two short handles on top. Often with a detachable shoulder strap too. In tan, cognac, black, navy, burgundy, olive, taupe.

And here's the shape I promised you, the one that changes your posture. When you carry a top-handle bag in the crook of your arm, or holding it by the handle, something happens to your body. The arm bends at the elbow. The shoulder lifts slightly. The chest opens. The back straightens. You can't slouch while carrying a top-handle bag. It's almost physically impossible. And the bag sits in front of you or at your side, not bouncing at the hip.

The structure also means the bag holds its shape when you set it down, it stands upright on a table or a chair, and everything inside stays in order. You open it, you see what you need, you close it.

Picture Via Montenapoleone in Milan on a bright autumn morning, a woman in her late sixties in a navy coat walking past the shop windows with a structured cognac leather bag held in the crook of her arm. Her back is straight, her chin is up, and she moves through the crowd like a dancer. Her bag is part of the reason.

Mistake number two. The logo-covered bag.

The bag covered in logos, printed all over, or with a giant metal logo on the front. It promises status and recognition.

But what I've observed is that the Italian women over sixty who look the most elegant rarely carry bags with big visible logos. There's a kind of quiet confidence in their style that doesn't need to announce where things came from. A bag covered in logos can feel like it's speaking louder than the woman carrying it. It can look like trying to impress. And, of course, when a logo is everywhere, it's also easily copied, so it can make even a real bag look questionable.

Italian women carry a bucket bag instead. Or more generally, beautiful leather without any obvious branding.

A bucket bag is a soft, rounded bag, shaped a little like a bucket, with a drawstring or a simple closure at the top and a single shoulder strap or handle. In smooth or pebbled leather, suede, or woven leather. In tan, chocolate, black, olive, burgundy, cream.

Why a bucket bag? Because its beauty is in the leather, the shape and the craft, not in a logo. It has a relaxed, softly structured shape that sits well against the body. It's easy to open and close. It holds a reasonable amount without becoming enormous. And it looks just as right with a linen dress in summer as with a cashmere coat in winter.

The principle is the same for any bag. Let the quality of the leather and the beauty of the shape speak for you. A plain bag in beautiful leather looks more expensive than almost any logo.

Picture a little leather workshop in Florence, near the river, the smell of leather in the air, bags hanging on hooks on the walls. A craftsman shows an older woman a soft olive suede bucket bag, and she runs her fingers over the stitching, opens the drawstring, closes it again, and smiles. There's no logo anywhere on it. She doesn't need one.

When you shop for a bag like this, look closely at the details that never appear in a logo. Is the stitching even and tight? Are the edges of the leather smoothly finished? Does the bag feel balanced when you hold it? These quiet details are where real quality lives.

Now, the next mistake is one I see constantly on travelers, and it's the one that ages the posture most.

Mistake number three. The nylon backpack in town.

The practical nylon backpack. I understand completely. It distributes weight across both shoulders, it frees the hands, and for hiking or long travel days, it makes sense.

But in town, in the center of Rome, Florence or Milan, a nylon backpack changes your whole silhouette. It pulls the shoulders back and then, to compensate, the head forward, so the posture rounds. It adds a big lump on the back, which makes a woman look hunched from the side. The nylon fabric, often black or gray, looks sporty and utilitarian. It doesn't go with a linen dress, a camel coat or a silk shirt. And practically, in crowded city centers, a bag on your back is a bag you can't see.

What do Italian women carry instead? A woven leather tote.

A tote made of woven leather strips, a beautiful, textured basket weave in soft leather. Medium-sized, with two shoulder handles. In tan, cognac, black, navy, cream, or a deep brown.

Why a woven leather tote? Because it gives you the practicality of a tote, a bag big enough for a scarf, a bottle of water, a book and a light sweater, without the heaviness or the sporty look. The woven leather is surprisingly light and flexible. The texture is beautiful, it catches the light and looks artisanal. It sits on the shoulder rather than the back, so you can see it and reach into it easily. And it works in every season, with summer linen and with winter wool.

And here's the important part. Carry less. A woven tote is meant for the essentials of a day out, not for everything you own. Many women I've watched carry it with the top open, so they can glance inside at a café table and find what they need immediately.

Picture a summer morning at a market in a small town on the coast, crates of lemons and tomatoes and bunches of basil, and an older woman in a white linen shirt-dress walking between the stalls with a tan woven leather tote over her shoulder. She drops in a bunch of basil and a paper bag of peaches, and the bag doesn't sag at all. She looks like the morning itself.

Mistake number four. The tiny micro bag.

Now we go to the opposite extreme. The tiny bag. So small that it holds a lipstick and a single key, and sometimes not even a phone. They're very fashionable, and they can look playful on a young woman.

But on a mature woman, a micro bag creates a few problems. First, proportion. A tiny bag next to an adult body makes the body look larger by contrast. Second, practicality. When the bag can't hold your glasses, your phone and your wallet, you end up carrying things in your hands or your pockets, which looks cluttered. And third, a micro bag can look like we're trying to follow a trend that wasn't designed for us. It reads as costume rather than choice.

Italian women of a certain age carry a small crossbody in tan instead.

Not a micro bag, but a small, proper bag. Big enough for a phone, reading glasses, a slim wallet, keys and a lipstick. In smooth or pebbled tan leather, with a simple flap or zip closure and an adjustable strap.

Now, earlier on this channel, I talked about the tiny nylon crossbody that bounces at the hip, and I want to be clear about the difference. The Italian version is leather, not nylon. It's structured, not floppy. And here's the key, the strap is adjusted so the bag sits higher, at the waist or just below the ribs, not bouncing at the hip. Many women wear it across the body when they're walking through a crowd, and then shorten the strap to wear it on the shoulder for lunch or dinner.

Why tan? Because tan leather is the great neutral. It goes with navy, black, white, ivory, olive, camel, denim, terracotta, almost everything. It looks warm and natural. And it ages beautifully, developing a soft patina over the years.

Picture a crowded summer evening on a pedestrian street in Rome, people strolling, gelato in hand, and a woman in her seventies walking with a small tan leather crossbody worn high across her navy dress, her hand resting lightly on the flap. Her hands are free for her gelato, her bag is safe, and she looks completely relaxed.

We're almost at the end. One more mistake, and then the evening bag I promised you, the one I reach for more than any other.

Mistake number five. Faded fake leather.

Faux leather, or synthetic leather, can look acceptable when it's brand new. But there's a particular kind of bag I see often, a synthetic leather bag that has begun to peel at the corners, crack along the handles, fade at the edges, and flake onto clothes. Many lower-quality synthetic leathers simply don't age well.

Why does this mistake make a bag look cheap? Because real leather tends to grow more beautiful with time, softening, darkening, developing character. Many synthetic leathers do the opposite. They deteriorate. Peeling and cracking are very visible, and they make the whole outfit look neglected. And synthetic materials often have a flat, plastic shine that the eye reads as inexpensive.

I want to say clearly, this isn't about spending a fortune. There are good-quality alternatives, and there are well-made bags at many prices. The point is to choose materials that age gracefully, and to retire a bag when it's past its best.

What do Italian women carry instead? For the evening, a clutch in suede. And this is the bag I promised you.

A suede clutch. A flat, envelope-shaped or softly structured bag without a long strap, held in the hand or tucked under the arm. In black, chocolate, navy, burgundy, olive, taupe, or a deep emerald. Sometimes with a small wrist strap. Big enough for a phone, a lipstick, reading glasses, a card and a key.

Why suede? Because suede has a soft, velvety surface that absorbs light and glows gently, perfect for candlelit dinners, concerts and evenings out. It looks rich and tactile. It's quiet, it doesn't shine or crackle. And it pairs beautifully with silk, cashmere, velvet and wool, the fabrics of the evening.

Why a clutch? Because it changes the way you hold yourself. When you hold a clutch in your hand or under your arm, your hands become part of your elegance. You carry it with intention. And at the table, it sits neatly beside your plate, never hanging off the chair or sitting on the floor.

Many of the Italian women I've admired treat their evening bag almost like a piece of jewelry. It's small, beautiful, and chosen with care.

Let me paint one final picture. A winter evening in Florence, at the opera house, people arriving in the lamp-lit foyer. An older woman steps in from the cold in a long black coat, unwinds a cashmere scarf, and under her arm, a chocolate suede clutch. She opens it, takes out her ticket, closes it with a soft click, and hands the ticket to the usher. No digging. No fumbling. Just grace.

That moment is the answer to the question the woman on the train asked me years ago. The bag carries you, or you carry the bag? Italian women over sixty carry the bag. Always.

Before the practical tips, let me tell you how I finally answered her question myself.

After that train ride, I went home and emptied my enormous tote onto the kitchen table. It took up the whole table. I sorted everything into three piles. Things I used every day. Things I used sometimes. Things I carried just in case. The just-in-case pile was the biggest by far. I put that pile away, kept the sometimes pile at home, and carried only the everyday things.

Then I bought a medium-sized structured tan leather bag with a top handle and a removable strap. It cost me more than I'd ever spent on a bag. And I've carried it almost every day since. It's softened, darkened at the handles, scratched in one corner, and it has never looked better. When I set it down on a café table in Rome last spring, the waiter, an older man, looked at it and said, good leather. Then he looked at me and said, good choice. I'll take that compliment to my grave.

And I want to share what I've noticed inside the bags of the women I admire, because I think the inside matters as much as the outside.

They seem to carry very few things, but each one is chosen. A slim leather wallet, not a thick one bursting with receipts. Reading glasses in a soft case. A phone. Keys on a single simple ring. A lipstick, often just one. A small comb or brush. A folded linen or cotton handkerchief. Sometimes a small bottle of scent. In summer, a fan or a pair of sunglasses in their case. In winter, a pair of leather gloves. That's nearly all.

And very often, these things live in small pouches. A little leather or fabric pouch for the lipstick and comb. Another for the glasses. When they change bags, from the day bag to the clutch, from the tote to the crossbody, they simply move the pouches. Nothing gets lost. Nothing gets forgotten. It takes ten seconds.

I've adopted this habit myself, and it has changed my life more than I expected. I used to avoid changing bags because it was such a nuisance, so I carried the same tired bag everywhere, to the market and to dinner. Now I move two little pouches and I'm ready. It means I actually use my evening bag. It means my day bag stays tidy. And it means I never again have to dig for a train ticket while a stranger watches with amusement.

There's also the question of color. Many of the women I've watched choose their bag to harmonize with their shoes, not match exactly, but belong to the same family. Tan loafers and a cognac bag. Black ankle boots and a black or burgundy bag. Navy ballet flats and a navy or tan bag. Gold sandals and a cream woven tote. When the bag and shoes speak to each other, the whole outfit feels connected from head to toe, even if the clothes in between are very simple.

And finally, they treat the bag with respect in public. In a café, the bag sits on a chair or on the lap, not on the floor. On a train, it sits close, not sprawled across the seat. When they open it, they open it with both hands. These are tiny gestures, but they add up to a feeling of order and calm.

Let me give you some practical tips for choosing and carrying a handbag after sixty.

First, empty your bag. Today, if you can. Sort everything into what you need daily, sometimes, and just in case. Carry only the first pile.

Second, choose medium over enormous. A medium bag holds what you need without becoming a burden. If you need to carry more on certain days, use a separate tote just for those days.

Third, choose structure. A bag that holds its shape keeps your things organized, stands up on its own, and looks polished.

Fourth, choose leather or suede, and care for it. Condition leather occasionally, brush suede, store bags stuffed with paper in a dust bag. A cared-for leather bag can last for many years.

Fifth, watch the strap length. A crossbody should sit high, at the waist, not bounce at the hip. A shoulder bag should sit snugly under the arm. A top-handle bag can be carried in the hand or the crook of the arm.

Sixth, choose three bags for most of life. A structured day bag, a woven or soft leather tote for markets and travel, and a small evening bag. With those three, you can go almost anywhere.

And seventh, switch shoulders. If you carry a shoulder bag, switch sides through the day. Your back will thank you.

Let me remind you of the five mistakes and their solutions. Instead of the heavy oversized tote, a structured leather top-handle bag. Instead of a logo-covered bag, a beautiful leather bucket bag. Instead of a nylon backpack in town, a woven leather tote. Instead of a tiny micro bag, a small crossbody in tan worn high. And instead of faded fake leather, a suede clutch for the evening, and quality leather that ages beautifully.
