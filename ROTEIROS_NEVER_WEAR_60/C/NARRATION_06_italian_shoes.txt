In a tiny cobbler's workshop in a side street in Florence, there is a wooden shelf by the window where the shoes wait to be collected. The first time I walked past it, I stopped and counted the pairs. There were brown suede loafers with the heels freshly replaced. Black leather ballet flats with new soles. A pair of tan driving moccasins, so old and so soft that they had taken the shape of someone's foot exactly. Low block-heeled pumps in navy. Ankle boots in chocolate brown, polished until they shone like chestnuts.

Not one pair of running shoes. Not one pair of plastic clogs. Not one pair of stilettos.

The cobbler, an older man with reading glasses on the end of his nose, saw me looking and came to the door. I asked him, in my clumsy Italian, who owned all these shoes. He shrugged and said, the ladies of the neighborhood. Then he picked up one of the brown loafers, turned it in his hands, and said something I have never forgotten. A good shoe, he said, is not bought. It is kept.

That's the whole philosophy of Italian women and their shoes in one sentence. And over many seasons in Rome, in Milan, in Florence and along the coast, I've watched women over sixty walk the cobblestones with an ease and grace that has very little to do with luck and a great deal to do with the shoes on their feet.

So today I'll share six shoes Italian women over sixty never wear, and exactly what they wear instead. I'll tell you the shoe that solves the comfort problem better than any sneaker I've ever owned. And the sixth one on this list may surprise you, because it's a shoe many women believe is the height of elegance. I'll explain why the women I've watched in Italy quietly put it away after a certain age, and what they choose that looks even more beautiful. Stay with me.

But first, why shoes matter so much after sixty.

Shoes change the way we walk, and the way we walk changes the way we look. A woman who shuffles looks older than she is. A woman who wobbles looks unsure. A woman who strides comfortably, with her head up and her shoulders back, looks confident at any age. Our feet change as we get older. They may widen, the arches may soften, the joints may complain. So the shoe has to do two things at once. It has to support us properly, and it has to finish the outfit with grace. Italian women don't believe these two goals are in conflict. They believe the right shoe does both.

Number one. Chunky running shoes.

Big, padded, cushioned running shoes, often in white or gray, with thick soles and mesh uppers. They have become the default travel shoe for so many of us.

For running, for long hikes, for the gym, they're wonderful. But in town, they create a heavy, bulky shape at the end of the leg. The thick sole adds height and weight to the foot. The padding makes the ankle look thicker. The technical mesh looks out of place next to wool, linen, silk and leather. And whatever you wear above them, a dress, wide trousers, a nice coat, the running shoe pulls the whole look toward the gym.

What do Italian women wear instead? Driving moccasins.

A driving moccasin is a soft, flexible leather or suede loafer with a sole made of small rubber nubs that extend up the back of the heel. They were originally designed for driving, but they've become one of the great walking shoes for women who want comfort and elegance at the same time.

Here's why they work so well. The leather is soft and unstructured, so it moves with the foot rather than against it. The low profile keeps the foot looking slender and the line of the leg clean. The flexible sole lets the foot move naturally. They come in beautiful colors, tan, cognac, navy, olive, burgundy, and they look right with jeans, with linen trousers, with a cotton dress, with a skirt.

One honest note. Because the sole is thin, some women find classic driving moccasins less cushioned on hard pavement or for very long walks. So look for versions with a slightly thicker rubber sole, or add a thin leather insole for extra support. And if you have specific foot problems, please follow your own podiatrist's advice. Comfort is personal.

Picture a sunny Saturday morning on the coast, a woman in her late sixties walking down to the harbor in navy driving moccasins, white trousers and a striped shirt, a basket over her arm. She steps from the cobblestones onto the wooden boards of the pier without breaking stride. Her shoes make no sound at all.

Number two. Crocs and plastic clogs.

You know the ones. Rubber or foam clogs with holes in the top, in bright colors, sometimes decorated with little charms. Many people find them comfortable, and in the garden, or the kitchen, or by the pool, I understand them completely.

But in town, they create several problems. The shape is wide and round, which makes the foot look large. The material, foam or rubber, has a flat, matte surface that looks like a toy next to real clothing. The bright colors draw the eye straight to the feet. And there's the sound, a soft slap with each step, and the way the foot moves inside them, slightly sliding, which changes the walk.

Italian women wear leather sandals instead, in warm weather, and in cooler weather we'll get to the alternatives in a moment.

A good leather sandal has a firm footbed, often molded leather or cork, and straps that hold the foot securely. Two straps across the foot, a crisscross over the toes, a strap around the ankle. In tan, natural, gold, black or white.

The leather breathes. It softens to the foot over time. The secure straps mean the foot doesn't slide around, so the walk stays smooth and confident. And a leather sandal looks right with nearly any summer outfit.

Picture a market in a Sicilian town, stalls piled high with blood oranges and pistachios, and an older woman in a cotton dress and tan leather sandals moving slowly from stall to stall, tasting olives, arguing about the price of tomatoes. Her sandals have probably been resoled several times. They look like part of her.

Number three. Cheap flip-flops.

Rubber flip-flops are close cousins of the clog, and I've mentioned them before, but they deserve their own place on this list because they're so common in summer, and so damaging to the look of an outfit.

Thin foam soles offer almost no support. The foot grips with the toes to keep them on, which changes the walk into a shuffle. The slapping sound announces you. The thin rubber strap between the toes can rub and blister. And in a city, with cobblestones and uneven pavements, flip-flops can simply be unsafe.

Italian women save flip-flops for the beach, the pool and the boat, and that's it. In town, they wear low block heels.

Now that might sound like a strange swap. Flip-flops for heels? But stay with me. A low block heel, perhaps three to five centimeters, with a wide, stable base, gives the foot a slight lift that makes the walk more upright and graceful. Many people find a small heel comfortable, but everyone's feet are different, so choose what feels right for you. A wide block spreads the weight evenly and doesn't wobble on cobblestones the way a thin heel does. In a sandal, a slingback, a pump or a mule, a low block heel can be worn all day.

They choose them in leather or suede, in colors like black, navy, tan, nude, burgundy, and they wear them with dresses, trousers, skirts. For many women over sixty, a low block heel is the perfect balance between height and stability.

Picture a summer evening in Rome, the light turning gold in the narrow streets of the old center, and a woman in her early seventies walking to dinner in a navy dress and tan suede slingbacks with a small block heel. She walks over the uneven stones confidently, chatting with her friend, never looking down at her feet once.

Now, the next one is a mistake that has nothing to do with style and everything to do with care. And it might be the most important lesson in this video.

Number four. Scuffed flats.

Ballet flats are wonderful, and we'll talk about them in a moment. But there's one version Italian women don't wear, and that's the scuffed, worn-out flat. Cracked leather at the toes, heels worn down to the plastic, the shape collapsed, the color faded.

Why does this matter so much? Because worn shoes tell a story, and it's not a good one. They suggest a woman who has stopped paying attention. A beautiful outfit, a great haircut, perfect jewelry, all of it can be undermined in a single glance at a pair of tired, scuffed shoes. And worn-down heels also change the way you walk, tilting the foot and putting strain on the knees and hips.

What do Italian women do instead? They wear suede loafers, and they take care of them.

Suede loafers are one of the most versatile shoes I know. Soft, warm-looking, quietly luxurious. In tan, chocolate, navy, olive, burgundy. With trousers, jeans, skirts and dresses. The texture of suede catches light softly, and it looks wonderful with wool and cashmere in autumn and winter.

But here's the real lesson. The cobbler. In Italy, I've noticed, women of a certain age have a relationship with their cobbler the way some women have a relationship with their hairdresser. They take their shoes in for new heels before the old ones wear through. They have soles replaced. They have suede brushed and leather polished and stretched. That's what the old man in Florence meant. A good shoe is not bought. It is kept.

Brush suede regularly with a soft suede brush. Protect it with a spray before you wear it. Keep it away from heavy rain if you can. Stuff the toes with paper when you store them. And take them to a cobbler at the first sign of wear. A shoe cared for like this can last for years, and it looks better every season.

Picture a quiet street in Bologna, under the long arcades, and a woman in her seventies stepping out of a tiny cobbler's shop with a paper bag in her hand. She sits on a bench, takes out a pair of chocolate suede loafers with fresh new heels, slips off her old shoes, and puts them on right there. She stands up and walks away a little taller.

I think about that woman often, because she showed me that elegance is not only about what we buy. It is about what we maintain. A pair of loafers with fresh heels and brushed suede says, quietly, I take care of myself and my things. And that message is far more attractive than any new trend. If you do one thing after this video, gather up the shoes you love but have stopped wearing because they are tired, and take them to a cobbler. You may find you already own the most elegant shoes of your life.

Number five. Platform sneakers.

These are sneakers with very thick, chunky soles, sometimes several centimeters high, often white. They're very fashionable, and they promise both height and comfort.

But on a mature woman, platform sneakers have several problems. The thick, stiff sole changes the way the foot rolls, which can make the walk heavy and clumpy. The added height without a heel can feel unstable for some people, particularly on uneven ground. The bulk at the end of the leg makes the feet look enormous. And the overall look is very young and trendy, and on a woman over sixty, the eye can read it as trying a little too hard.

Italian women wear ballet flats in leather instead.

A real leather ballet flat, with a thin but slightly cushioned sole, a soft rounded or gently pointed toe, and a shape that hugs the foot without pinching. In black, navy, nude, red, tan, metallic gold or silver.

Why leather? Because it molds to the foot, it breathes, and it looks elegant. Why a ballet flat? Because it's light, it's feminine, and it keeps the line of the leg long and clean. A slightly pointed toe lengthens the leg even more. A low vamp, showing a little of the top of the foot, does the same.

They wear ballet flats with cropped trousers, with straight jeans, with midi skirts, with shirt-dresses. They're perfect for city walking, for museums, for lunch, for travel.

One tip. Many ballet flats have very thin soles, which can be uncomfortable on stone streets. Look for a pair with a slightly thicker sole or a little cushioning inside, or ask your cobbler to add a thin rubber sole underneath. It protects the leather and gives you more grip.

Picture the Uffizi in Florence on a quiet weekday morning, an older woman walking slowly through the galleries in black leather ballet flats, cropped navy trousers and a white shirt, stopping in front of each painting. Her footsteps are silent on the marble. She could walk there for hours.

And now, number six. The one that might surprise you.

Number six. High stilettos.

Many of us grew up believing that the stiletto, the very high, very thin heel, is the most elegant shoe a woman can wear. And in a photograph, on a red carpet, for a very young woman, it can be. But the Italian women over sixty I've watched almost never wear them.

Here's why. On cobblestones, uneven pavements and old marble steps, a thin, high heel is unstable, and a fall is something none of us can afford. High heels push the weight onto the front of the foot, which many mature feet find painful. They change the posture, tipping the body forward and arching the back. And when a woman is uncomfortable, it shows. She walks stiffly, she takes small careful steps, she looks down. That's not elegance. Elegance is ease.

So what do Italian women of a certain age wear instead? Ankle boots.

Leather or suede ankle boots with a low to moderate block heel or a small stacked heel. In black, chocolate brown, cognac, tan or burgundy. With a slim, close-fitting shaft that sits just at or above the ankle bone. Sometimes with a side zip, sometimes a pull-on, sometimes with a little elastic gusset.

Why ankle boots? First, stability. The boot wraps the ankle, supporting it, and the block heel gives a broad, stable base. Second, warmth and protection in cooler months. Third, versatility. Ankle boots look right with trousers, with jeans, with skirts, with dresses. Fourth, they give a little height and a little lift to the posture, without any of the danger or pain of a stiletto. And fifth, a sleek ankle boot in good leather looks sharp, modern and powerful on a woman of any age.

They wear them with straight trousers that fall over the top of the boot, creating a long, unbroken line. With a midi skirt and opaque tights in the same color as the boot, so the leg looks endless. With straight jeans, slightly cropped, so a little of the boot shows. With a wool dress and a long coat.

Let me give you one final picture. Milan in November. A cold, bright morning. A woman in her late sixties walks along Via Montenapoleone in a long camel coat, charcoal wool trousers, and chocolate suede ankle boots with a low stacked heel. She walks fast. She has places to be. Her stride is long and confident, her head is up, and as she passes the shop windows, she glances at her reflection with a tiny smile. She doesn't need a stiletto to feel powerful. She's already wearing it in the way she walks.

Before the practical tips, I want to tell you about the day I finally learned this lesson myself.

I was in Rome, and I had brought a pair of very high heels for a special dinner. Beautiful shoes. I'd worn them perhaps three times in my life. The restaurant was only a ten-minute walk from my hotel, so I decided to walk. Ten minutes on Roman cobblestones in high heels felt like an hour. I arrived with aching feet and a fixed, tight smile, and I spent the whole evening thinking about my toes instead of the conversation.

At the next table sat a woman perhaps ten years older than me, in a black silk shirt, wide black trousers and black suede ankle boots with a small heel. She'd clearly walked too, because she came in with her coat over her arm and a little color in her cheeks from the evening air. She looked relaxed, glamorous and completely at home. When she left, she walked out the door and down the street as if she could go on for miles.

I took my heels home in my suitcase, and I never wore them again. The next season, I bought my first pair of good leather ankle boots. I still have them. They've been resoled twice.

Let me give you some practical tips for shoes after sixty.

First, comfort is not optional. If a shoe hurts in the shop, it will hurt more on the street. Shop in the afternoon, when your feet are a little larger, and walk around the shop for several minutes before you decide.

Second, choose leather and suede. Natural materials breathe, mold to the foot and last for years with care.

Third, think about the sole. For city walking, a sole with some grip and a little cushioning is worth more than any decoration.

Fourth, find a cobbler. Have heels and soles replaced before they wear through. Polish, brush, protect. A cared-for shoe looks elegant for years.

Fifth, match your shoes to the line of your outfit. Low vamps and pointed toes lengthen the leg. Matching your shoe to your trouser or tights color creates an unbroken line.

And sixth, buy fewer, better pairs. Most of the Italian women I've watched seem to rotate a small collection of beloved shoes, a pair of loafers, a pair of ballet flats, sandals, ankle boots, a pair of low heels, rather than dozens of pairs they barely wear.

And a seventh tip, a little bonus, about color. If you are building a small shoe wardrobe from the beginning, I have noticed that the women I admire tend to start with three colors. A warm brown or cognac, which goes with camel, olive, ivory, navy and denim. A deep navy or black, which goes with gray, white, black and evening clothes. And one metallic or soft neutral, a gold sandal, a nude ballet flat, a pale tan, which disappears into summer outfits and lengthens the leg. With those three colors, across five or six shapes, almost every outfit you own has a partner. And when you travel, you can pack two or three pairs and never feel underdressed.

I'll add one personal observation. When I look back at photographs from my first trips to Italy, I can date them by my shoes. The running-shoe years. The flip-flop years. The sports sandal years. In every one, I look like a visitor. In the photographs from later trips, wearing loafers, ankle boots and leather sandals, I look like I live there. The clothes were not that different. The shoes were.
