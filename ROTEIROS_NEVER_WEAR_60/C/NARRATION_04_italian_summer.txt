It was the middle of July in Rome, almost forty degrees in the shade, and I was standing in line for gelato near the Pantheon in a polyester sundress with a bright tropical print. The dress was sticking to my back. My shoulders were turning pink. My sports sandals were making my feet sweat, and I had a floppy straw hat that kept falling over my eyes every time I bent my head to look at the flavors.

In front of me stood a woman, perhaps sixty-eight, perhaps seventy. She wore a sand-colored linen shirt-dress, belted loosely, a pair of flat tan leather sandals, and a small straw hat with a firm brim and a black ribbon. Her arms were covered to the elbow. She had a silk scarf tied to the handle of her bag. She was not sweating. She was not rushing. She ordered pistachio and lemon, said something charming to the young man behind the counter, and walked out into the blazing piazza as if it were a spring morning.

I remember thinking, how? How is she so cool, so composed, so elegant, in exactly the same heat that is melting me?

Over many summers since, in Rome, in Florence, in the little towns of the Amalfi coast, and on the islands, I have watched women over sixty handle the Italian summer with a grace that I have tried very hard to learn. And I have noticed that there are certain mistakes they simply don't make.

So today I want to share seven summer style mistakes Italian women over sixty never make, and what they do instead. Some of these are about elegance. Some are about comfort. And a few are about protecting ourselves from the sun in ways that also happen to look beautiful. Mistake number six, the straw hat, is one I made for years without realizing, and the fix is so simple you'll laugh. And the seventh is the one I see every single summer, on women from every country, and it is the easiest of all to change. Stay with me.

Mistake number one. The polyester sundress.

I understand why we buy them. They are inexpensive, they come in cheerful prints, they never need ironing, and they fold into a tiny corner of a suitcase. But in real summer heat, polyester is a trap.

Polyester doesn't breathe the way natural fibers do. It holds heat against the body and doesn't let moisture escape easily. Within an hour of walking, it clings. It sticks to the back, the hips, the tummy, exactly where none of us want to feel fabric clinging. And the shine on many polyester fabrics catches the harsh midday light in a hard, plastic way that the eye reads as cheap. Add a large, busy print, and the dress starts to look like a costume rather than clothing.

What do Italian women wear instead? A linen shirt-dress.

A shirt-dress is cut like a long shirt, with a collar, buttons down the front, and usually sleeves to the elbow or rolled to the forearm. In linen, it is one of the most perfect summer garments I know. Linen breathes. It feels cool on the skin. It doesn't cling, it skims. And it creases in that soft, honest way that, in Italy, is considered part of its charm rather than a flaw.

They choose solid colors, or very simple stripes. Sand, white, ivory, navy, olive, terracotta, sea blue. They belt it loosely at the waist with a thin leather belt or a tie of the same fabric. They leave the top button or two open. And with a pair of leather sandals, that's the whole outfit, ready for the market in the morning and dinner at night.

Picture a small town on the coast during the evening passeggiata, everyone strolling slowly along the seafront as the heat breaks. An older woman in a white linen shirt-dress walks with her granddaughter, the hem moving gently in the breeze off the water. Her dress is creased from the day, and she looks absolutely perfect.

Mistake number two. Cargo shorts.

I know. Shorts feel like the only possible answer when it's that hot. And cargo shorts, with all those pockets, feel so practical for travel.

But think about what cargo shorts do to the silhouette. They are usually cut straight and boxy, ending somewhere at the knee or just above, which is one of the least flattering lengths for most legs over sixty. The bulky side pockets add width right at the hips and thighs. The heavy cotton twill is stiff and holds heat. And the overall look is very utilitarian, closer to a safari than a summer in Italy.

There is also something cultural here. In many Italian cities, especially in churches and in the more formal parts of town, shorts on adults are noticed. And in many churches, covering the knees and shoulders is expected for visitors. So shorts can limit where you go.

Italian women over sixty wear wide linen trousers instead.

Wide-leg linen trousers, high-waisted, often with an elastic or drawstring waist in summer, that fall to the ankle or just above. In white, cream, navy, black, olive or striped. They are as cool as shorts, sometimes cooler, because air moves freely around the legs. They protect the skin from the sun. They cover the knees, so you can walk into any church. And they create a long, fluid line from the waist to the floor that makes every woman look taller.

They wear them with a simple tank in silk or cotton, a loose linen shirt, or a striped knit. And with flat leather sandals or espadrilles.

Picture Florence on a summer afternoon, the stone of the cathedral glowing, and a woman in her seventies in wide navy linen trousers and a white shirt walking slowly across the square, the fabric of her trousers moving like water around her legs. She can walk straight into the church and out again without a second thought. That's freedom.

One small detail makes wide linen trousers look polished rather than sloppy, and that is the length. The hem should just graze the top of the foot when you wear your sandals, or sit right at the ankle bone. Too long, and the linen drags on the ground and collects dust. Too short, and the trousers look like they shrank. It's worth a quick visit to a tailor, because the right length is what makes inexpensive linen look considered.

Mistake number three. Sports sandals.

The thick, padded sandals with Velcro straps and rubber soles. I owned three pairs. I loved them for hiking and for walking on uneven paths.

But in town, they create a heavy, chunky shape at the bottom of the leg. The wide straps and thick soles make the foot look large and the ankle look thick. The rubber and nylon materials look technical next to linen and cotton. And in the heat, the synthetic padding can make feet sweat more, not less.

What do Italian women wear instead? Leather sandals. Simple, flat or with a very low heel, with thin to medium leather straps. In tan, natural, gold, black or white. Some cross over the toes, some wrap around the ankle, some are a simple slide.

Why leather? It breathes. It softens with wear and molds to your foot. It looks elegant with everything, from wide trousers to a linen dress to a cotton kaftan. And a well-made leather sandal, with a sole that has a bit of structure and a little cushioning, can be surprisingly comfortable for a full day of walking.

There's a reason so many of the coastal towns are known for their sandal makers. Picture a narrow lane in a seaside town, little shops with sandals hanging in the doorways, and an older woman sitting on a small stool while a craftsman fits a strap to her foot. She stands, takes a few steps on the cobblestones, nods, and walks out wearing them.

Mistake number four. Bare shoulders, burnt.

The sleeveless top, the strapless sundress, the thin spaghetti straps. In the moment, it feels cooler to show more skin. But under a strong Mediterranean sun, bare shoulders and arms are exposed for hours, and by evening they're red, sore, and sometimes peeling.

Beyond the discomfort, there's a style issue. Sunburned skin never looks elegant. The redness can clash with every color you wear. And the shoulders and upper arms are areas many of us feel a little self-conscious about after sixty anyway.

The Italian answer is the cotton kaftan, and more generally, light layers that cover without heating.

A kaftan is a long, loose garment, often with wide sleeves and a simple neckline, in light cotton, cotton gauze, or silk. It floats around the body. It lets air in. And it covers the shoulders and arms from the sun. In white, in soft stripes, in a simple print, with a little embroidery at the neckline.

Italian women also cover their shoulders with a light linen shirt worn open over a tank, a thin cotton cardigan, or a silk scarf draped around the arms. The idea is always the same. Cover the skin with something light and natural, and let the fabric do the work of protecting you.

Of course, protecting yourself from the sun matters for your health, so please follow your own doctor's advice about sunscreen and sun exposure. But from a purely style perspective, the women I've watched seem to understand that covered shoulders in light fabric look more elegant than exposed, reddened ones.

Picture a long lunch on a boat anchored in a bay, the water impossibly blue, and an older woman sitting in the shade of the awning in a white cotton kaftan with wide sleeves, sunglasses on, a glass of something cold in her hand. Everyone else is turning pink. She is serene.

Mistake number five. The beach cover-up in town.

Now this one is subtle, but I see it constantly. A woman spends the morning at the beach, puts a sheer mesh cover-up or a thin sarong over her swimsuit, and then walks straight into town for lunch, shopping or a coffee.

Here's the problem. A beach cover-up is designed for the beach. Often it is sheer, so the swimsuit shows through. It is often short, sometimes very short. The fabric is thin, crochet or mesh or chiffon. And in town, among people dressed for daily life, it looks undressed. In many Italian seaside towns, you'll notice that people change before they leave the beach. Walking through town in swimwear or something that looks like it can be noticed, and in some places it's even discouraged.

The Italian solution is simple. Bring a real garment to the beach, so you can change into it after. Very often, that garment is the cotton kaftan we just talked about, a long one, opaque, that can go from the sand to the café. Or a linen shirt-dress. Or wide trousers and a shirt. Something that looks like clothing, not like a beach accessory.

They keep a small bag with a dry swimsuit change, a hairbrush, a lipstick, and a pair of leather sandals or espadrilles. In five minutes, they go from the beach to the town square looking composed.

Picture a little café on the main square of a coastal town at one o'clock, just after the beaches empty for lunch. At one table, an older couple has just come up from the sea, her hair still damp and pulled back, wearing a long white kaftan and gold sandals, ordering a spritz. She looks as if she's been there all morning. Nobody would guess she was swimming twenty minutes ago.

We're almost there. Two more. And I promised you that the straw hat mistake is one you'll laugh about, because the fix is so simple.

Mistake number six. The straw hat flopping.

Every summer, we buy a big floppy straw hat. It has a wide, soft brim, it looks romantic in the shop, and it seems like the perfect way to protect our faces from the sun.

But here's what actually happens. The soft brim flops down over the eyes. It blows in the wind. It hangs in front of the face so that people can't see you, and you can't see them. You spend the whole day holding it with one hand. And because the brim collapses, the hat often doesn't shade the face properly anyway. Visually, a limp brim drags the face downward, which is exactly the opposite of what we want.

The Italian answer is a structured straw hat.

A hat with a firm, shaped brim that holds its line. A medium brim, not enormous. Often a classic shape with a defined crown and a ribbon or leather band around it. In natural straw, ivory, or sometimes black. The firm brim shades the face evenly, stays where you put it, and creates a strong, flattering line across the forehead, a little like a good pair of sunglasses does.

And here's the simple fix I promised. If you already own a floppy straw hat, look for one with a wire in the edge of the brim, or ask a hat maker whether yours can be stiffened. Or simply choose your next hat by holding it upside down by the crown. If the brim keeps its shape, it will keep its shape on your head too. That's it. I laughed when someone told me that, because I had spent years fighting my hats instead of choosing better ones.

Picture a summer festival in a small town, lights strung across the piazza, and a group of older women sitting on a bench in the late afternoon, all in structured straw hats with black ribbons, chatting and watching the children play. Their faces are shaded, their hats don't move, and they look like they stepped out of an old photograph.

Mistake number seven. Sneakers.

And here it is. The one I see every summer, on women from every country, including my own. White athletic sneakers with everything. With linen dresses, with wide trousers, with summer skirts.

I understand. Sneakers feel safe. We walk a lot in summer, especially when we travel. But in the heat, athletic sneakers are hot, closed and heavy. And visually, they add a bulky, sporty block at the end of the leg that breaks the line of a summer outfit. A linen dress with chunky sneakers looks like two different ideas fighting each other.

What do Italian women wear instead? Espadrilles. The classic summer shoe with a canvas or leather upper and a sole made of braided rope. Flat ones for walking, wedges for a little height. In navy, natural, black, red, or striped.

Espadrilles are light, breathable, comfortable, and they have a relaxed Mediterranean elegance that sneakers simply can't match. They look right with linen trousers, with a shirt-dress, with a kaftan. The rope sole gives a soft, natural texture that echoes a straw hat or a woven bag. And a low wedge espadrille gives just a little lift, which lengthens the leg without the instability of a heel.

And to finish the summer look, Italian women add a silk scarf. Not as a heavy layer, but as a little touch of color and protection. Tied around the neck to shade it from the sun, knotted to the handle of a straw bag, wrapped around the crown of a hat, tied loosely in the hair. A silk scarf weighs almost nothing, it protects delicate skin on the neck, and it brings color right up near the face.

A woman in her late sixties walks along the harbor in wide white linen trousers, a navy linen shirt, navy espadrilles, a silk scarf in blue and white tied at her neck, and a structured straw hat in her hand now that the sun is low. Before the practical tips, let me walk you through a whole summer day the way I have seen it done, because I think it helps to see how these pieces work together from morning to night.

The day begins early, because in an Italian summer the best hours are the cool ones. At eight in the morning, a woman of a certain age goes to the market in wide white linen trousers, a navy cotton top, flat tan leather sandals and her structured straw hat. She carries a woven basket or a leather tote. She buys peaches, tomatoes, a little cheese, flowers if they are good that day. She stops for a coffee standing at the bar, exchanges news with the barista, and is home before the heat really arrives.

In the middle of the day, she rests. The shutters close. The streets go quiet. This is not laziness, it is wisdom. Nobody elegant is walking around a city at two in the afternoon in July if she can help it.

At around five or six, she showers, and changes into the evening version of herself. Perhaps the linen shirt-dress, belted, with gold sandals and gold earrings. Perhaps the cotton kaftan with a silk scarf at the neck. She puts on a little lipstick, a little scent, and walks out for the passeggiata, the slow evening stroll, when the whole town comes outside to see and be seen. Later, dinner, often outside, often late, often long.

Notice what is missing from that day. No sweating through polyester. No sunburned shoulders. No hat blowing into the street. No heavy shoes. The clothes support the rhythm of the day instead of fighting it. And the rhythm itself, early mornings, quiet afternoons, long evenings, is part of the elegance.

I tried living that rhythm for a whole month one summer in a small town on the coast. I packed only linen, cotton and silk, two pairs of leather sandals, one pair of espadrilles, one structured hat and three silk scarves. I've never packed so little or felt so well dressed. And I've never been so relaxed, either. When your clothes are easy, your mind is free for the things that matter, the conversation, the view, the peach that is so ripe it drips down your wrist.

Let me give you some practical summer tips.

First, choose natural fibers. Linen, cotton, silk. They breathe, they feel cool, and they look better as the day goes on.

Second, cover strategically. Shoulders, arms and the back of the neck are the places that suffer most in strong sun. Light, loose layers protect them without heat.

Third, choose looseness over tightness. In summer, clothing that skims the body is always cooler and more flattering than clothing that clings.

Fourth, think about your day. If you'll go from the beach to town, bring a change. If you'll visit churches, cover your knees and shoulders. If you'll walk on cobblestones, choose sandals or espadrilles with a sole that has some grip.

Fifth, keep your palette light and simple. White, ivory, sand, navy, sea blue, terracotta, olive. These colors look cool in the heat and beautiful in the summer light.

And sixth, finish with one or two pieces of gold jewelry and a silk scarf. That is all a summer outfit needs to look complete.
