On a ferry from Naples to Capri, many summers ago, I was standing at the rail in a pair of wraparound sport sunglasses I had bought for a cycling trip. They were silver, with mirrored blue lenses, and they curved all the way around my head like a visor. Next to me stood a woman who must have been seventy-five. White hair in a low knot, a navy cotton dress, and a pair of thick tortoiseshell sunglasses that tilted up very slightly at the outer corners. She turned to look at the island coming closer, and the light caught the edge of those frames, and I swear she looked like a portrait in a museum.

Then I caught my own reflection in the ferry window. I looked like I was about to compete in a triathlon.

That was the day I started paying attention to sunglasses in Italy. And over many seasons since, in Rome, in Milan, in Florence, and all along the coast, I have noticed that women over sixty there treat sunglasses with almost the same seriousness they give to their shoes. They are not an afterthought you grab at the checkout. They are the frame of the face. And there are certain styles these women simply do not wear.

So today I am going to share seven sunglasses Italian women over sixty never wear, and exactly what they wear instead. I'll also tell you about the one lens color I see on so many elegant Italian faces, which changes how the whole world looks back at you, not only how you look. That comes later, so stay with me. And at the very end, I'll share the one frame shape that I think is the single most flattering choice for a mature face, the one I now wear almost every day.

Before we begin, let me explain why this matters so much after sixty.

As we age, the face changes in quiet ways. The brows may thin and lower a little. The outer corners of the eyes may soften downward. The skin around the eyes shows lines and shadows. None of this is a problem, it is simply life. But it means the frame we put across the middle of the face has more power than ever. The right frame can lift, define and balance. The wrong one can drag the face down, shrink the features, or draw attention to every line. Sunglasses are the biggest single accessory we wear on our faces. Let's choose them well.

Number one. Wraparound sport sunglasses.

These are the sunglasses I was wearing on that ferry. Curved, close to the head, usually in thin metal or shiny plastic, often with mirrored or colored lenses.

They are made for speed and wind, and for those purposes they are perfect. But on a woman walking through a city, they create several problems. The curved shape follows the head instead of framing the face, so there is no strong line across the brow. Instead of lifting the face, they wrap it. The thin, shiny material looks technical, even aggressive. And the overall effect is sporty in a way that clashes with almost any elegant outfit, a linen dress, a silk blouse, a cashmere sweater.

What do Italian women wear instead? A tortoiseshell cat-eye. A frame in warm, mottled brown acetate, the material that looks like polished tortoiseshell, with a gentle upward sweep at the outer corners. Not an extreme cat-eye, not a costume, just a soft lift.

That little lift is the whole secret. It draws the eye up and outward, exactly where our faces start to soften downward. It acts almost like a gentle facelift without any surgery at all. And the warm brown tones of tortoiseshell flatter nearly every skin tone and look wonderful with silver, white, blonde or dark hair.

Picture the Piazzetta on Capri at noon, everyone sitting at the little café tables in the square, and an older woman in a striped top and white trousers lowers her tortoiseshell cat-eyes to read the menu. That gesture alone, glasses sliding down the nose, eyes looking over the top, is pure Italian theater.

Number two. Tiny oval sunglasses.

These come and go in fashion. Small, narrow, oval lenses, often in thin wire frames, sitting high on the nose.

On a very young face with smooth skin, they can look playful. On a mature face, they almost always look too small. The lens doesn't cover the area under the eye, so shadows and lines show around the edges. The narrow shape makes the face look wider by contrast. And tiny frames tend to look like a fashion trend we are trying to chase, rather than something chosen for our own face.

Italian women choose square acetate instead. A medium to slightly large square or rectangular frame in solid acetate, black, dark brown, deep green, or burgundy, with soft rounded corners.

The square shape gives structure. It creates clean horizontal lines across the brow and under the eye that sharpen the face. The solid acetate has visual weight, which balances a full head of hair and looks confident. And the larger lens covers the delicate skin around the eye, protecting it from the sun and hiding a sleepless night.

Imagine a Roman street in late morning, the light bouncing off ochre walls, and a woman walking briskly with her shopping bag, square dark brown frames on her face, scarf at her neck. She looks like she knows exactly where she's going and has no time to waste. That is the feeling a good square frame gives you.

Number three. Mirrored lenses.

Mirrored lenses, those shiny, reflective surfaces in silver, blue, gold or rainbow, have their moments in fashion. And I understand the appeal. They feel modern and a little mysterious.

But mirrors on the face do something unflattering for a mature woman. They hide the eyes completely and reflect the world instead. That creates a cold, closed expression. People can't see your warmth. They see themselves. The shiny surface also draws attention away from your features and onto a sort of hard, glassy spot in the middle of your face. And very often, mirrored lenses come in cheap, sporty frames, which brings us back to the first mistake.

Instead, Italian women of a certain age often choose a gold aviator, with solid, non-mirrored lenses in brown or gray-green. The aviator is a classic shape, a soft teardrop with a thin metal frame and a bridge across the top. In gold, it is warm and gentle against the skin, and it echoes gold jewelry beautifully.

Here is the important detail. The aviator works best on a mature face when it is not too large, and when the lens is a warm, slightly transparent tint, so a little of the eye shows through. That softness keeps the face open and friendly, while the gold frame adds a little shine.

Picture a summer lunch at a beach club on the coast, umbrellas lined up in perfect rows, and an older couple at a table near the water. She wears a white cotton shirt, gold aviators and a gold chain, and she is laughing at something her husband said. You can see her eyes crinkle behind the lenses. That's the difference. You can see her joy.

A small note on aviators. Many women tell me aviators feel too masculine for them. The way I have seen Italian women soften the shape is with a slightly rounder lens, a thin gold frame rather than silver, and a warm outfit around it, a silk scarf, a coral lip, small gold earrings. The aviator then becomes glamorous rather than military.

Number four. Cheap clear plastic frames.

You know these. Thin, transparent, slightly flimsy frames, often in clear or pale pink plastic. Sometimes they come free with a magazine, sometimes they cost almost nothing at a beach stall.

Clear frames can be lovely on prescription glasses, in good, thick acetate. But cheap, thin, clear plastic on sunglasses tends to disappear on the face in a way that leaves the lens floating, with no structure and no frame around it. It can look unfinished. The thin plastic bends, the hinges loosen, and the frames sit crookedly. And a crooked frame on the face makes the whole face look crooked.

Italian women choose a thick black frame instead. Strong, solid black acetate, slightly substantial, in a square, rectangular or soft cat-eye shape.

Now, you might think black is too harsh for a woman over sixty. And it can be, if the frame is thin and severe. But a thick, glossy black frame has a different effect. It creates a bold, graphic line that anchors the face. With silver or white hair, it gives the most wonderful contrast, very chic, very confident. With a red lip, it is unforgettable. And with a simple outfit, a white shirt, a black sweater, a camel coat, it finishes everything like a signature at the bottom of a letter.

Picture Milan, a gray winter day, a woman in her seventies stepping out of a tram in a black coat and thick black sunglasses even though the sun is hidden behind clouds. She doesn't need the sun. She wears the glasses because they make her feel like herself. And as she walks past the shop windows on Via Montenapoleone, she looks like she might own every one of them.

Number five. Rimless sunglasses.

Rimless sunglasses have lenses with no frame around them, just a thin bar at the bridge and the arms attached directly to the lens.

The idea is to look minimal and light. But on a mature face, the effect is often vague. Without a frame, there is no line to define the brow or lift the eye. The lenses look like they are floating on the face. And the overall impression can be a little dated, a look that belonged to a particular moment and hasn't quite found its way back.

Instead, Italian women choose a rounded brown frame. A soft round or slightly oval shape, but substantial, in a warm brown acetate, coffee, chestnut, honey or a darker tortoiseshell.

Round frames soften an angular face and add gentleness. In warm brown, they look intellectual and kind at the same time, like a woman who reads a great deal and has opinions about everything. The brown is softer than black, which makes it easier for women with lighter hair or fair skin. And the round shape echoes the curves of the cheeks in a way that looks natural.

Picture a bookshop in Florence, a narrow one with shelves to the ceiling, and a woman at the counter in a camel cardigan and round brown sunglasses pushed up just above her forehead while she flips through a novel. Okay, I know I said Italian women rarely leave sunglasses in their hair, but she was only checking the price.

Number six. Oversized bug-eye sunglasses.

Now this might surprise you, because I'm about to tell you that Italian women love large sunglasses. And they do. But there is a difference between oversized and bug-eye.

Bug-eye sunglasses are very round, very large, often in thin plastic, and they cover almost half the face, sometimes reaching down to the cheekbones. They can look dramatic in a photograph, but in real life, on a mature face, they can swallow the features. The face beneath looks smaller. The lenses can sit on the cheeks and leave marks. And the shape, being so round and so large, can make the face look round and wide.

And here, finally, is the lens color I promised you. Italian women choose green-gray lenses. A classic, deep green with a little gray in it, the kind of lens that has been used in sunglasses for decades.

Why does this lens matter? It's not only about how you look. It's about how you see. A green-gray lens tends to keep colors looking fairly true and natural, so the sea still looks like the sea and the stone still looks like stone. It feels calm on the eyes. And from the outside, that deep green tint looks rich and classic, and it softens the eye area without the coldness of a gray lens or the orange cast of some brown lenses.

Many of the women I have watched wear that green lens in a classic frame, and something about it simply looks right in the Italian landscape. Picture a terrace above a lemon grove on the coast in the late afternoon, the sea glittering below, and a woman in a white linen dress looking out through deep green lenses. The world through those lenses is cool and calm. The world looking at her sees elegance.

And now, number seven, and the frame shape I promised you at the beginning, the one I think is most flattering of all for a mature face.

Number seven. Gradient pink lenses.

Gradient lenses darken at the top and fade toward the bottom. In soft pink, they are very fashionable, and they are often marketed as feminine and pretty.

But here is the problem I have seen on so many faces. A pink gradient lens fades exactly where our skin needs the most help, under the eye. The lighter bottom half reveals shadows, lines and puffiness, and the pink tint can make redness or broken capillaries look more obvious. The pink also tends to clash with silver hair and with warm neutral clothing. What was meant to be soft becomes a little sugary, and a little cheap.

So what do Italian women wear instead? This is the one. The oversized square.

A large, but not bug-eyed, square frame in solid acetate. Black, deep brown or tortoiseshell. Slightly wider than the face at the temples, with the top line of the frame following or sitting just above the brow, and a deep, even lens, often in that green-gray we just talked about, or a solid brown.

Why is this the most flattering shape for a mature face? Because it does everything at once. The strong top line replaces a thinning brow and lifts the whole upper face. The width at the temples creates the impression of higher cheekbones and a narrower lower face. The deep lens covers the under-eye area completely, hiding shadows and protecting delicate skin. And the bold, square shape reads as confident, decisive and glamorous, without being a costume.

When I finally bought my first oversized square frame in dark tortoiseshell, I stood in front of the mirror in a little optician's shop in Rome and actually laughed. I looked like a woman who had stories. And I do have stories. I just hadn't been wearing a face that told them.

Picture the Spanish Steps in Rome in the early morning, before the crowds. A woman in her late sixties sits halfway up, in a navy dress and a pair of oversized square tortoiseshell sunglasses, a small leather bag on her knees, simply watching the city wake up. She is not posing. She is not waiting for anyone. She is just there, and she looks magnificent.

Before the practical tips, I want to tell you what that optician in Rome taught me, because it changed how I shop for frames forever.

I had walked in with a very clear idea. I wanted something small and discreet, because I thought large frames were for younger, bolder women. She listened politely, and then she handed me a mirror and a pair of oversized tortoiseshell frames and said, simply, try. I put them on and immediately wanted to take them off. They felt enormous. She told me to wait. Look away, she said, then look back. And when I looked back, the frames had stopped being glasses and had become part of my face.

She explained something I have thought about ever since. When we try on sunglasses, we look at the glasses. When other people see us, they look at the face. The question is never whether the frames are beautiful in your hand. The question is whether your face is more beautiful with them on. She also told me to always try frames with my hair the way I actually wear it, and in the clothes I wear most. A frame that is perfect with a ponytail might feel very different with my hair down around my shoulders.

So here is a little ritual I now follow. I wear my usual clothes, I do my hair the usual way, and I bring a friend, or at least my phone. I try the frames in front of a mirror, then I step back about two meters, because that's the distance most people see us from on the street. I take a photograph. And I look at the photograph the next day, with fresh eyes. It's astonishing how often the pair I loved in the shop looks wrong in the picture, and the pair I nearly dismissed looks wonderful.

Another thing I learned is to think about your hair color with your frame color. If your hair is bright silver or white, black and dark tortoiseshell create a beautiful, graphic contrast. If your hair is a soft gray-blonde or a warm brown with silver, honey tortoiseshell and gold metal blend gently and look natural. If you still color your hair a deep shade, a solid brown, burgundy or dark green acetate can look rich and unexpected.

And finally, think about your wardrobe. If you wear a lot of black, navy and gray, a black or dark tortoiseshell frame will feel at home. If you wear camel, olive, ivory and terracotta, warm brown and gold will make everything sing. Your sunglasses are part of your outfit every single day of summer, so let them belong to the rest of your life.

Let me give you a few practical tips for choosing your frames.

First, follow the brow. The top line of the frame should follow the curve of your eyebrows or sit just above them. If you can see your brows above the frame, the glasses will often look too small or sit too low.

Second, watch the cheeks. When you smile, the frame shouldn't lift off your nose or press into your cheeks. If it does, it's too large or the wrong shape for your face.

Third, choose acetate over thin plastic. Acetate has depth, weight and a beautiful polish. It looks expensive even when it isn't, and it can usually be adjusted by an optician to fit properly.

Fourth, choose warm colors if you're unsure. Tortoiseshell and brown flatter almost everyone. Black is dramatic and beautiful with silver hair. Gold metal is soft and glamorous.

Fifth, protect your eyes. Whatever the frame, make sure the lenses offer proper protection from the sun. That matters more than any style rule I could give you.

And sixth, take care of them. Keep them in a case, clean them gently, and have them adjusted when they loosen. Well-fitted sunglasses sit straight on the face, and a straight frame makes everything look balanced.

Let me remind you of the seven swaps. Instead of wraparound sport frames, a tortoiseshell cat-eye. Instead of tiny ovals, square acetate. Instead of mirrored lenses, a gold aviator with soft tinted lenses. Instead of cheap clear plastic, a thick black frame. Instead of rimless, a rounded brown frame. Instead of oversized bug-eyes, the classic green-gray lens. And instead of gradient pink lenses, the oversized square, the frame I believe can flatter almost every mature face.
