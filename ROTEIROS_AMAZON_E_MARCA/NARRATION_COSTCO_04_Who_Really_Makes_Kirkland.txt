You have probably put a Kirkland Signature product in your cart this year without thinking twice. Coffee, batteries, paper towels, maybe a bottle of olive oil. But here is the question almost nobody asks at the register: who actually makes it? Costco does not own most of the factories behind its own label, and it rarely says who does. Today we follow the paper trail, separate confirmed facts from popular myths, and show you how to use this knowledge to save real money.

Let's start with the basics, because the scale of this story matters. Kirkland Signature was launched in 1995. The name comes from Kirkland, Washington, the town where Costco had its headquarters at the time. Back then, the idea of a warehouse club building its own premium brand seemed like a gamble. Store brands had a reputation in America for being the cheap, plain-label option, the thing you bought when money was tight and you did not care much about quality. Costco set out to change that image entirely. The goal was a private label that would sit on the shelf right next to the famous national names and hold its own, or even beat them.

Nearly three decades later, the results are hard to argue with. Kirkland Signature sales are reported to run in the tens of billions of dollars every single year. To put that in perspective, that figure would make Kirkland, on its own, larger than many well-known consumer companies that are household names. And Kirkland does not run television commercials. It does not sponsor halftime shows. It does not pay celebrities to hold up its products. It grows mostly through word of mouth and through the simple fact that millions of members walk past it every week.

So that brings us back to the central question. If Costco is not running giant factories for coffee, batteries, tuna, diapers, and golf balls, then who is? The answer is a business arrangement that is extremely common in retail but rarely explained to shoppers. It is called co-manufacturing, sometimes called private label manufacturing or contract manufacturing.

Here is how it works in plain English. A retailer like Costco decides it wants a product. It sets the specifications: the ingredients, the quality standards, the packaging, the size, and the price it is willing to pay. Then it goes to a manufacturer that already has the equipment, the expertise, and the supply chain to make that kind of product. Sometimes that manufacturer is a small specialist you have never heard of. Sometimes it is one of the biggest and most famous brand owners in the world. The manufacturer produces the item, puts the retailer's label on it, and ships it out.

Why would a famous brand agree to make a product that competes with its own name on the same shelf? It sounds strange at first, but it makes business sense. Factories are expensive. A plant that runs at full capacity is far more efficient than one that sits partly idle. A huge, steady order from a retailer the size of Costco can keep production lines humming, spread fixed costs over more units, and guarantee volume. For many manufacturers, it is better to make the private label product themselves than to let a competitor win that contract.

Now, here is the part that frustrates curious shoppers. Costco rarely confirms who makes its Kirkland products. In many cases, the contracts include confidentiality terms, and the company generally does not publish a list of suppliers. Suppliers can also change over time. A product made by one company five years ago might be made by a different company today, or by several companies in different regions. So when you see a viral social media post claiming to reveal the secret maker of every Kirkland item, you should be careful. Some of those claims are confirmed. Some are reported by credible outlets. And some are pure rumor that has been repeated so often people treat it as fact.

So let's go through the most talked-about examples one at a time, and be honest about which category each one falls into.

We begin with the clearest case of all: coffee. If you pick up certain bags of Kirkland Signature coffee, you do not need a detective. The packaging itself says custom roasted by Starbucks. That is about as close to an official confirmation as you will ever get. Costco and Starbucks have a long-standing relationship on these products, and the label is right there on the bag. That does not mean the Kirkland coffee is identical to a specific Starbucks retail blend you might buy at the coffee shop down the street. Custom roasted means it was roasted to Costco's specifications. But it does tell you that one of the most recognizable coffee companies in the world is behind those beans. For a household that drinks a pot of coffee every morning, that is a meaningful piece of information, because the price per pound of the Kirkland version is often noticeably lower than many name-brand bags.

Next, batteries. This is one of the most widely repeated Kirkland stories, and it has been reported for years that Kirkland Signature alkaline batteries are made by Duracell. A number of news outlets and consumer writers have reported this connection, and it has been discussed publicly for a long time. As always, keep in mind that manufacturing arrangements can change, and Costco itself does not typically advertise who makes its batteries. But this is one of the better-supported reports out there. For retirees and families who go through batteries in remote controls, smoke detectors, hearing-related devices, flashlights, and grandchildren's toys, the price difference over a year can add up.

Now let's talk about something every household uses: bath tissue, and for families with young children, diapers. Kirkland bath tissue and Kirkland diapers have historically been associated with Kimberly-Clark, the company behind well-known brands in that category. This association has been reported in the press over the years. But this is a category where you should be especially careful with the word "historically." Paper and personal care products are produced in enormous volumes, and suppliers can change or be split among several makers. So the honest way to say it is this: Kimberly-Clark has been reported as a Kirkland manufacturer in this category in the past, and that history is part of why many shoppers trust the product. What is made where today is something Costco does not routinely disclose.

Here is one that matters a great deal to young families and to grandparents who help out: baby formula. Kirkland Signature infant formula has been reported as made by Perrigo, a large company that specializes in producing store-brand health and nutrition products for many retailers. Perrigo is one of the biggest names in the private label world that most consumers have never heard of. And this is a good moment to pause on something important. In the United States, infant formula is regulated by the Food and Drug Administration, and all formula sold here must meet federal nutrient requirements. That is one reason store-brand formula can be a sensible choice for budget-conscious families. If you are helping a son or daughter with the cost of raising a baby, this is a conversation worth having with their pediatrician.

Next, tuna. Kirkland Signature canned tuna has been reported to be produced by Bumble Bee, one of the best-known tuna brands in the country. Again, this is reported rather than officially advertised in most cases, but it is one of the commonly cited connections. Canned tuna is a pantry staple for many older Americans, an inexpensive source of protein that keeps for a long time. Knowing that a major tuna company is reported to be behind the Kirkland version may give you more confidence trying it.

Now we come to a story that turned into a small legend in the sports world: golf balls. When Kirkland Signature golf balls first appeared, they caused a stir among golfers, because reviewers found that they performed surprisingly well for a fraction of the price of premium balls. Those original Kirkland golf balls were reported to be made by Nassau, a Korean manufacturer. The story became even more interesting because of legal disputes that followed in the golf ball industry, which got plenty of coverage. Later versions of the ball have reportedly come from different sources. If you are a retired golfer who plays a few rounds a week and loses a ball or two in the water every time, you already understand why this one got so much attention.

Stay with me, because the next part is where most of the internet gets it wrong, and it is the story you have almost certainly heard repeated at a dinner party or a family barbecue.

Let's talk about Kirkland vodka. For years, a rumor has circulated that Kirkland Signature vodka is secretly Grey Goose. You will see it on social media, in forums, and in conversations at the liquor aisle. Here is the honest answer: that claim is not confirmed. It is a myth, or at the very least, an unproven rumor. Costco has not confirmed it, and Grey Goose has not confirmed it. Both products are described as French vodka, and some people believe that the geographic connection is evidence enough. It is not. Plenty of spirits can come from the same country without coming from the same producer. Blind taste tests have been done by various writers and enthusiasts with mixed results, and taste tests cannot tell you who manufactured a product anyway. So if anyone tells you as a fact that Kirkland vodka is Grey Goose in a different bottle, you can tell them that is a widely repeated rumor and nothing more. That is exactly the kind of claim this channel will never present as fact.

The vodka myth is a good lesson in how these stories spread. Somebody notices a similarity, posts about it, and a few years later it is treated like common knowledge. That is why it is worth separating three things: what the label tells you, what credible reporting has said, and what is simply repeated online.

So let's think about the different ways you can learn who makes a product, without needing any inside connection.

The first way is the label itself. Sometimes, as with the coffee, the manufacturer's name is printed right on the package. Other times, the label says something like "manufactured for" or "distributed by" Costco Wholesale. That phrase is a clue in itself. It tells you that Costco is not the factory, that someone else made the product for Costco. In some food categories, you may also see a plant code or a country of origin, which can offer a small hint.

The second way is product recalls. This is something many shoppers do not realize. When a food or consumer product is recalled, the recall notice often names the company responsible for manufacturing it, because regulators need to identify who produced the affected batches. Over the years, recall notices have occasionally shed light on who was making certain store-brand items at a given time. That does not mean a product you buy today comes from the same place, but it is one of the few public windows into the private label supply chain.

The third way is credible reporting. Business journalists, consumer advocates, and financial analysts have written about Kirkland suppliers for years. When a report is consistent across several reputable outlets and has been around for a long time, it carries more weight than a single viral video.

And the fourth way is simply knowing what you do not know. Costco's general approach is not to confirm. If you see a definitive claim with no source, treat it as a maybe.

Now let's step back and ask the bigger question. Why does Costco do it this way? And why does it matter to you as a shopper?

The answer goes back to Costco's pricing philosophy, which company executives have described publicly over the years. Costco has long been reported to cap its markup at about fourteen percent on branded items and about fifteen percent on Kirkland Signature products. Those numbers have been cited by company leaders in interviews and discussions for a long time, and they are a big part of the Costco legend. Compare that to many traditional retailers, where markups on certain items can be far higher.

Kirkland gives Costco a powerful tool. When Costco controls its own brand, it does not have to pay for a national brand's advertising, marketing, or the premium that comes with a famous name. Those savings can be passed along in a lower price, while Costco still earns its modest margin. And here is the clever part: Kirkland also gives Costco leverage with the name brands. If a national brand will not offer a good enough price, Costco has the option to put a Kirkland product in that spot instead. That pressure helps keep the prices on name-brand items down as well.

There is another side to this. When Costco puts its own name on a product, its reputation is on the line. If a Kirkland item disappoints, it is not some outside brand that takes the blame. It is Costco. That gives the company a strong reason to set high standards. Many shoppers have noticed that some Kirkland items are sold in larger sizes or with specifications that are not identical to the famous brand version. That is by design. Costco is not simply relabeling; it is often asking for a product built to its own specification.

But let's be fair and balanced here, because this channel is not in the business of telling you that everything at Costco is perfect. Not every Kirkland product is a better deal than the name brand. Not every Kirkland product is made by a famous company. Some are made by excellent specialist manufacturers you have never heard of, and some may not suit your taste. Store-brand products can taste different, feel different, or work differently, even when the maker is reported to be a big name, because the recipe or specification can differ. And of course, buying in bulk is only a bargain if you actually use what you buy. A giant container of something that goes stale or expires before you finish it is not a deal at all.

And to be clear, this channel is not affiliated with Costco in any way. We are not paid by Costco, and we are not speaking for the company. We are simply looking at what has been publicly reported, so you can make your own decisions.

So how do you put all this to work? Let's go through some practical steps, especially useful if you are living on a fixed income and every dollar matters.

Step one: compare unit prices, not package prices. Costco shelf tags usually show a unit price, such as price per ounce, per count, or per pound. That number is your best friend. A Kirkland item might look more expensive because the package is huge, but the unit price may be far lower than what you pay at the supermarket.

Step two: start with the categories where the maker is best documented. Coffee labeled custom roasted by Starbucks is a natural starting point, because the label tells you directly. Batteries and tuna, where the reported connections have been widely discussed, are other low-risk places to experiment.

Step three: be skeptical of myths, especially the expensive ones. If you are buying a bottle of vodka because you believe it is secretly a luxury brand, buy it because you like the taste and the price, not because of a rumor. Your own taste buds are a better guide than the internet.

Step four: think about how much you will really use. Bulk buying works best for items that are shelf-stable and that you use regularly: paper goods, batteries, canned foods, coffee, and similar staples. For fresh foods and items that spoil, consider splitting a purchase with a neighbor, a friend, or a family member. Many retirees do exactly that, and it can turn a warehouse membership into a shared money saver.

Step five: remember that Costco has historically been known for a generous return policy on many products. If you try a Kirkland item and you are genuinely unhappy with it, check the current return policy at the membership counter. That policy has been part of what makes trying a store brand less risky. Policies can have exceptions for certain categories such as some electronics, so always ask about the current rules rather than assuming.

Step six: watch for recalls. This is important for safety, not just savings. If you buy any packaged food, including store brands, it is wise to keep an eye on recall announcements from the Food and Drug Administration and the Department of Agriculture. Costco has a reputation for contacting members about recalls on items they bought, because it has their purchase records through the membership card. That is one benefit of shopping with a membership that many people overlook.

Now, let's address a question many viewers ask: if I can buy what is reported to be the same product made by the same company, why would anyone pay more for the famous brand? There are a few honest answers. Some people simply prefer a specific taste or formula. Some products under the famous brand truly are different. And some shoppers do not have a Costco membership, or do not have room to store bulk packages. The point of this video is not to tell you to stop buying name brands. It is to make sure that when you pay more, you are doing it because you choose to, not because you did not know there was an alternative.

There is also a lesson here about the modern American grocery store in general. Kirkland is the most famous example, but it is far from the only one. Supermarket chains, pharmacies, and big-box stores across the country sell store-brand products that are made by outside manufacturers, sometimes the same companies that make the national brands. Store-brand pain relievers, allergy medications, vitamins, and nutrition products are often produced by specialist private label companies. In the case of over-the-counter drugs, store brands have to contain the same active ingredient in the same strength as the brand-name version they are compared to, which is why many pharmacists will tell you a store brand can be a sensible choice. Always check the active ingredient on the label, and if you take prescription medications or have health conditions, ask your pharmacist or doctor before switching anything.

So what is the big picture? Who really makes Kirkland Signature? The honest answer is many different companies. In a few cases, like coffee custom roasted by Starbucks, the label tells you outright. In others, like batteries reported to be made by Duracell, tuna reported to be made by Bumble Bee, baby formula reported to be made by Perrigo, bath tissue and diapers historically associated with Kimberly-Clark, and the original golf balls reported to be made by Nassau, credible reports have pointed the way. And in still other cases, like the famous vodka rumor, the story is a myth that has never been confirmed.

What ties all of it together is a business model. Costco uses co-manufacturing to put high-quality products under its own name, keeps its reported markup cap of around fifteen percent on Kirkland items, skips the cost of national advertising, and uses its own brand as leverage to keep name-brand prices in check. The company rarely confirms who makes what, and suppliers can change over time. That is the full picture, without the hype.

For you, the shopper, the takeaway is simple. Read the label. Compare the unit price. Trust credible reporting over viral rumors. Buy in bulk only what you will truly use. And try the store brand in the categories where the evidence is strongest. Small decisions like these, repeated week after week, can add up to hundreds of dollars a year for a typical household, which for many retirees is the difference between a tight month and a comfortable one.

One final reminder: everything we discussed today is based on information that has been publicly reported, and manufacturing relationships can change without notice. If a specific product matters to you, especially for health reasons, read the current label and ask questions before you buy.
