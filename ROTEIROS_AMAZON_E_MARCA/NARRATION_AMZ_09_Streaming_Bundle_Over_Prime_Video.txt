If you pay for Amazon Prime, you may have noticed something changed on your television. Since January 2024, Prime Video shows commercials, unless you pay extra to remove them. Millions of households are now paying more for less, and many have never looked at the alternatives. Cord-cutting reviewers keep recommending a streaming setup that can cost a fraction of what most families pay. Stay with us, and we will walk you through every piece of it, honestly, including what you give up.

Let's start with what happened. In January of 2024, Amazon began showing advertisements during movies and shows on Prime Video for Prime members in the United States. Before that, Prime Video had been ad-free as part of the Prime membership. Amazon said the ads would be limited, with fewer ads than traditional television and other streaming services, though how many ads you see can vary. Members who want to go back to the ad-free experience can pay an extra fee. As of this report, that ad-free add-on costs two dollars and ninety-nine cents a month, on top of the Prime membership itself.

Let's put that in perspective. Prime currently costs fourteen ninety-nine a month, or one hundred and thirty-nine dollars a year. Add the ad-free option for twelve months, and that is roughly another thirty-six dollars a year. So a household that wants Prime Video the way it used to be is now paying about one hundred and seventy-five dollars a year on the annual plan, and more on the monthly plan. Prices can change, and Amazon has raised them before, so check your own account for the exact figures.

There was another change, too. Amazon used to run a separate free, ad-supported streaming service called Freevee, which anyone could watch without a Prime membership. Amazon has folded Freevee into Prime Video, and the separate Freevee brand has been phased out. Amazon has said a selection of free, ad-supported content remains available inside Prime Video for people without a Prime membership, though what is offered can change. If you used to watch Freevee and wondered where it went, that is the answer.

Now, Prime Video still has a large library, and some original shows and live sports that are only available there, including certain professional football and basketball games. For some households, that alone is reason enough to keep it. This report is not about telling you to cancel anything. It is about showing you what else is out there, so you can make an informed choice. Let's talk about what cord-cutting reviewers often call the foundation of a low-cost setup. It is something your grandparents had, and it has quietly gotten better. The antenna.

Broadcast television is still free over the air in the United States. If you live within range of local TV towers, an indoor or outdoor antenna can pick up your local stations, often including affiliates of ABC, CBS, NBC, Fox, and PBS, along with dozens of smaller channels, many of them showing classic shows, old movies, game shows, westerns, and news. There is no monthly fee. You buy the antenna once, and that is it. Antennas can range from inexpensive indoor models to larger outdoor ones, and prices vary.

Today's broadcast signals are digital, and in many areas, the picture quality is excellent. In fact, reviewers often point out that free broadcast TV can look sharper than the same channel delivered through cable, because it is less compressed. Local news, many major sporting events, prime-time network shows, and big national broadcasts are all available this way in many markets.

How well an antenna works depends on where you live, how far you are from the towers, and what is in between, such as hills or tall buildings. Before you buy, the Federal Communications Commission offers a free online tool that shows which channels you may be able to receive at your address. Many reviewers also suggest buying from a store with an easy return policy, just in case. And if your television is fairly modern, it likely has a built-in tuner. You simply connect the antenna, go into the settings menu, and run a channel scan.

One common question about antennas is whether you can record shows the way you could with a cable box. The answer is yes, with the right equipment. Several companies sell over-the-air DVRs, which are devices that connect to your antenna and let you record programs, pause live TV, and skip ahead. Some work with a hard drive you plug in, and some offer a program guide that may carry a small subscription fee. Reviewers suggest comparing the total cost, including any guide fee, before you buy. For many people, though, simply watching live, the way television used to work, is perfectly fine.

You may also hear about something called NextGen TV, a newer broadcast standard that some stations have started using in many cities. It can offer improved picture and sound, but it requires a compatible television or tuner, and it is still rolling out. You do not need it to enjoy free broadcast TV today, so reviewers generally say not to feel pressured to upgrade just for that.

And here is a free extra that many viewers love. PBS offers a free app and website where you can watch many of its programs, and your local PBS station may stream live online too. Some PBS stations also offer a membership perk with a larger library of past shows for people who donate at a certain level. If you enjoy documentaries, mysteries, and British drama, it is worth a look.

Now, layer two. Free streaming services. And this is where many people are genuinely surprised.

There are several large streaming services that cost absolutely nothing. They are supported by ads, much like traditional television. You do not need a credit card to use many of them. Let's go through the big names.

Tubi is owned by Fox and offers a very large library of movies and TV shows on demand, free with ads. Its catalog includes many older films, classic series, and some newer titles, and it changes over time. Pluto TV is owned by Paramount, and it is built to feel like old-fashioned cable, with hundreds of live channels you can flip through, including channels devoted to a single classic show, news channels, movie channels, and more, plus a library of on-demand titles. The Roku Channel is offered by Roku, and you do not need a Roku device to watch it. It is available through a website and on many other devices. It offers free movies, shows, and live channels with ads.

There are others as well. Many smart televisions come with their own free channel services built in. Samsung, LG, and Vizio televisions, for example, have offered free live channel lineups. And Plex offers free movies and live channels with ads. Many of these services also carry free live news channels from well-known news organizations.

Here is how cord-cutting reviewers often describe these free services. Their libraries are big, but they lean toward older content. You are not usually going to find the newest blockbuster or the latest hit series on them. But if you enjoy classic television, older movies, westerns, mysteries, and game shows, which many of our viewers do, you may find more than you could ever watch. And remember, Prime Video now has ads too, unless you pay extra. So if you are already watching commercials, the question becomes whether you are getting enough extra value from the paid service to justify the price.

Now, here is something worth sticking around for. In a few minutes, we will share the single strategy that cord-cutting reviewers say saves more money than almost anything else, and it does not involve giving up any of your favorite paid shows. But first, let's talk about a resource that is sitting in your wallet, or should be.

Your public library card. Many libraries now offer free streaming through services called Kanopy and Hoopla. Kanopy offers films, including classic movies, award-winning independent films, documentaries, and educational series, all without ads, free to library cardholders at participating libraries. Hoopla offers movies, TV episodes, music, audiobooks, ebooks, and comics, also free with a participating library card. Libraries that offer these services usually set a monthly limit on how many titles you can borrow, and that limit varies by library.

And through the Libby app, many libraries offer free ebooks and audiobooks, which is not television, but it is a lot of entertainment for no cost. Not every library offers every service. Check your library's website or ask at the desk. If your library does not offer them, ask whether a neighboring library system allows you to get a card. Some do, sometimes for a fee, sometimes free for residents of the same state.

Libraries also lend physical DVDs and Blu-ray discs, usually at no charge. If you still have a DVD player, your library may hold thousands of movies and complete TV series you can borrow for free. Many people forget this option entirely.

Using Kanopy or Hoopla is usually straightforward. You download the free app on your smart TV, streaming device, tablet, or phone, or visit the service's website. You choose your library from a list, then enter your library card number and, in many cases, a PIN that the library gives you. Once you are signed in, you can browse and start watching. If you get stuck, librarians are often happy to help, and many libraries hold free technology classes for older adults, covering exactly these kinds of apps. Some even offer one-on-one appointments. It is one of the best-kept secrets in many communities.

There is one more benefit to library streaming that reviewers point out. Because these services are offered through libraries, Kanopy in particular tends to focus on high-quality films, such as classic Hollywood movies, foreign films, and acclaimed documentaries, rather than whatever is trending. For viewers who feel that modern streaming menus are cluttered and hard to navigate, that curated feel can be a welcome change.

So far, we have built a setup with an antenna for local channels, free streaming services for on-demand movies and shows, and your library card for ad-free films and more. Total monthly cost, potentially zero, after the one-time cost of an antenna and assuming you already have internet service and a television or device that can stream.

That last point is important. To stream anything, you need internet service at home, and that is often the biggest bill of all. If cost is a concern, ask your internet provider whether it offers a low-income plan. Many providers offer discounted plans for households that qualify, often including people who receive SNAP, Medicaid, or other benefits. Rules and prices vary by company and change over time, so call and ask.

You also need a way to get streaming apps onto your television. Many newer TVs are smart TVs with apps built in. If yours is not, a streaming stick or box from companies like Roku, Amazon, Google, or Apple can plug into your television. Prices vary, and they often go on sale. Reviewers often suggest choosing one with a simple remote, and many models now offer voice search, which can be very helpful if small buttons or on-screen keyboards are frustrating.

Now, the strategy we promised. Cord-cutting reviewers call it rotating, or sometimes churning, your subscriptions.

Here is how it works. Instead of paying for four or five streaming services every single month, all year long, you pick one, maybe two, at a time. You subscribe for a month or two, watch what you want on that service, then cancel and switch to another one. Most major streaming services let you cancel anytime with no penalty and resubscribe later. Your watch history and profile are often saved if you come back.

Think about it this way. Most people do not watch every paid service every month. A new season of a favorite drama comes out on one service. A documentary everyone is talking about is on another. A big movie lands on a third. By rotating, you pay for each service only when it has something you actually want to watch. Reviewers often say this approach can cut a household's streaming spending dramatically over a year, compared with keeping everything running all the time.

A few tips to make rotating work. First, keep a simple list, on paper or on your phone, of the shows you want to see and which service carries them. Second, when you subscribe, set a reminder on your calendar a few days before the billing date, so you can decide whether to cancel or keep it another month. Third, cancel early. With many services, if you cancel partway through a month, you can usually keep watching until the end of the billing period you already paid for. Check each service's terms, but this is common. Fourth, consider waiting until a whole season of a show is available, then binge it in one month instead of paying for several months to watch it week by week.

This same idea applies to Prime itself. Some households keep Prime only during the months when they shop heavily, like the holidays, or when a show or sports season they love is on Prime Video. The monthly option makes that possible. We covered more about whether Prime is worth it for shipping in another report on this channel.

Let's run a simple example to show how this can add up. These are round, made-up numbers, not quotes from any service, so check real prices yourself. Imagine a household paying for four streaming services at around fifteen dollars each, every month. That is about sixty dollars a month, or roughly seven hundred and twenty dollars a year. Now imagine that same household keeps an antenna and the free services as its everyday base, and pays for just one fifteen-dollar service at a time, rotating through the year. That is about one hundred and eighty dollars a year. The difference, in this example, is more than five hundred dollars. Your own numbers will be different, but the principle is the same, and that is why reviewers keep coming back to rotation.

A word about accessibility, because it matters for many of our viewers. Nearly every streaming service, and broadcast television, offers closed captions. If dialogue is hard to follow, turn them on in your TV or app settings. Many services also offer audio description for people with vision loss, where a narrator describes what is happening on screen. And if voices are hard to hear over background music, many televisions and sound bars have a dialogue or voice enhancement setting. Reviewers often say these settings make a bigger difference than buying a new television.

If a family member usually helps you with technology, consider asking them to set up your antenna, free apps, and library apps all at once, during one visit. Once everything is on the home screen, day-to-day watching is usually as simple as picking an app and pressing play.

Let's also talk about ad-supported tiers on paid services. Many major streaming services now offer a cheaper plan with ads, and a pricier plan without them. Prime Video's approach is different, since ads are the default and you pay extra to remove them. Reviewers often say that if you do not mind commercials, choosing the ad-supported tier on whatever service you are using that month is another easy way to save. If commercials truly bother you, it may be worth paying for ad-free on one service you watch heavily, rather than across all of them.

And bundles are worth a look. Some streaming companies sell bundles that combine two or three services at a lower price than buying them separately. Some cell phone plans, internet plans, and credit cards include a streaming service as a perk. Before you pay for any service, check whether you already have it through a phone, internet, or credit card account. Many people are surprised to find they have been paying twice. Terms of these perks change often, so read the details.

Now, the fair side of the story. When does keeping Prime Video, maybe even with the ad-free add-on, make sense?

If you are already a Prime member for shipping and other benefits, Prime Video is included. In that case, the real question is only whether the two ninety-nine add-on is worth it to you, not whether Prime Video itself is worth paying for. If you watch a lot of Prime Video and commercials frustrate you, three dollars a month may be a reasonable price for peace and quiet. That is a personal choice.

Prime Video may also make sense if you follow sports or shows that are only available there. Amazon has secured rights to certain live sports, and those rights change from season to season, so check what is available in your area.

And if your household simply values having everything in one place, with one login and one bill, that convenience is worth something. Not everyone wants to manage a rotation, and there is nothing wrong with that.

A quick word about safety, because scammers target streaming customers too. Be wary of emails or text messages saying your streaming account has been suspended or your payment failed, with a link to click. These are a common trick to steal passwords and credit card numbers. Do not click the link. Instead, open the app or type the official website address yourself to check your account. Be cautious of anyone selling cheap lifetime streaming access or devices loaded with free premium channels. Those are often illegal and can expose your devices to malware. And never give someone remote access to your computer because they claim to be from a streaming company's tech support.

So let's put together the setup cord-cutting reviewers keep recommending. Start with a free antenna setup for local channels, news, and network shows. Add free, ad-supported services like Tubi, Pluto TV, and the Roku Channel for a huge library of movies and classic shows. Use your library card for Kanopy and Hoopla, where available, plus free DVDs. Then, if you want something only a paid service has, subscribe to one service at a time, watch what you want, and rotate. And check whether your phone, internet, or credit card already gives you a service for free.

For many households, that approach can provide more viewing than they have time for, at a cost that is far below a stack of monthly subscriptions. Your mileage will vary depending on what you like to watch and where you live, and prices and lineups change all the time. So treat this as a starting point, and check current details before you sign up or cancel.

Before you make any changes, one practical step. Sit down and write out every streaming service you pay for right now. Check your credit card and bank statements, because many people find a subscription they forgot about. Next to each one, write the monthly cost and when you last actually watched it. That simple list is often eye-opening, and it is the first step reviewers recommend before anything else.
