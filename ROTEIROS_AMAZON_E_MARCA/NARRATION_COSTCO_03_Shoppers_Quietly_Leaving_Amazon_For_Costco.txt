Now let's get practical about bulk buying, because this is where many people either save a lot of money or waste it.

The golden rule of buying in bulk is simple. A bargain is only a bargain if you use it. Buying a giant container of something at a low unit price doesn't save you a dime if half of it goes stale, spoils, or expires in the back of the pantry. So think about which items are truly safe to buy in bulk.

The best bulk purchases tend to be things that don't spoil, or that last a long time. Paper towels. Toilet paper. Facial tissues. Trash bags. Aluminum foil and plastic wrap. Laundry detergent. Dish soap. Dishwasher pods. Batteries. Canned goods. Rice and dried pasta. Coffee, if you drink a lot of it and store it well. These are the items where shifting from Amazon to Costco often makes the most sense, because you'll use every bit of them eventually.

Then there are items that can work in bulk if you plan ahead. Meat and fish can be divided into smaller portions at home and frozen. Bread can be frozen and toasted as needed. Shredded cheese and butter freeze reasonably well. Many frozen vegetables and fruits come in large bags that keep for months in the freezer. If you have a decent freezer, these can be smart purchases.

And then there are items you may want to be careful with, especially in a small household. Fresh produce in large quantities, big tubs of salad greens, large packs of fresh berries, and big bakery items can be hard to finish before they go bad. For those, a single-item purchase from a regular grocery store or from Amazon may actually make more sense.

Storage is the other half of the equation. If you live in a smaller home, an apartment, or a retirement community with limited closet space, a thirty-pack of paper towels can be a real problem. Before you shift your spending, take an honest look at where you'd put everything. Some people split bulk purchases with a neighbor, a friend, or an adult child. You buy the big pack, they pay you for half, and everybody gets the lower unit price without the storage headache. Just remember that Costco memberships are meant for the members and the household cardholder named on the account, and Costco now scans cards at the entrance, so the shopping trip itself should be made by a member.

There's also the question of transportation. A heavy Costco haul can mean cases of water, big jugs of detergent, and bulky boxes. If lifting is hard for you, think about how you'll get everything from the cart to the car and from the car into the house. Costco employees can often help load items into your vehicle if you ask. And for the heaviest items, like cases of water, it may be worth considering delivery, even if it costs a little more, rather than risking an injury.
