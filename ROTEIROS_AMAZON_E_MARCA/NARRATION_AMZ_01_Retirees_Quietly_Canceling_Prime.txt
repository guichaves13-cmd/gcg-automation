But first, you need to know about one of the biggest consumer protection cases in recent memory, because it is directly connected to how hard canceling Prime used to be.

The Federal Trade Commission, the FTC, sued Amazon over its Prime sign-up and cancellation practices. The government's complaint said Amazon used what are known as dark patterns. That is a term for website designs that steer people into choices they did not intend to make, like signing up for something without clearly realizing it, or making cancellation confusing and drawn out. The FTC described Amazon's old cancellation process in a way many of you may remember. You click to cancel, then you get a page reminding you of everything you will lose, then another page offering a different plan, then another page, and another, until many people simply gave up.

In September of twenty twenty-five, Amazon agreed to settle that case for a total of two and a half billion dollars. That included a one billion dollar civil penalty and one and a half billion dollars in refunds to customers. Under the settlement, eligible customers could receive up to fifty-one dollars. Amazon did not admit wrongdoing as part of the settlement, which is common in cases like this. The claims process ran during twenty twenty-six, and that claim window is now closed. So, how do you figure out whether Prime is worth it for you? Here is a simple, practical method that anyone can follow, whether you shop on a computer, a tablet, or a phone.

Step one. Look at your order history. On Amazon's website or app, go to your account and open the section called Your Orders. You can usually filter by year. Count how many orders you placed over the last twelve months. Be honest with yourself. If you see dozens and dozens of orders, many of them small, everyday things like batteries, vitamins, paper towels, or pet food, Prime may be earning its keep. If you see only a handful of orders, and most of them were over thirty-five dollars anyway, that is a sign you might not need it.

Step two. Look at how much you spent per order. If most of your orders are small, say ten or fifteen dollars, then free shipping matters a lot, because without Prime you would either pay shipping or have to wait until you had enough items to reach the free shipping threshold. If your orders are already larger, the shipping benefit is worth less to you.

Step three. Think about Prime Video. Ask yourself how many hours you actually watched in the past month. Do you watch with ads, and do they bother you? Are you also paying for other streaming services like Netflix, Hulu, or others? Many retirees discover they are paying for three or four streaming services and only really watching one. If Prime Video is not your main source of entertainment, it may not be worth keeping Prime just for that.

Step four. Check whether you are using any of the other benefits. Amazon Music. Photo storage. Grocery delivery. Whole Foods discounts. The RxPass prescription program, which we will touch on later. If you are not using any of these, then you are paying for features that do nothing for you.

Step five. Look at your billing date and how you pay. Go to the Prime section of your account, which Amazon usually labels something like Your Prime Membership or Manage Membership. There you can see when your membership renews, how much it will cost, and whether you are paying monthly or annually. Write that renewal date down on your calendar. This one step alone can save you from being surprised by a charge you forgot about.

Now let's say you have done the math and you have decided to cancel. Here is how to do it safely, and how to protect yourself along the way.

First, go to the Prime membership page in your account. Look for an option that says something like End Membership or Cancel Membership. Amazon may show you several screens before the cancellation is complete. They may remind you of the benefits you will lose. They may offer you a cheaper plan, or suggest switching from monthly to annual, or offer to remind you a few days before renewal instead. Take your time and read each screen carefully. Do not click the biggest, brightest button just because it is there. Look for the choice that actually ends your membership, and keep going until you see a clear confirmation that your membership has been canceled or will end on a specific date.

Second, check your email. Amazon typically sends a confirmation message. Save it. If you ever need to prove you canceled, that email is your evidence.

Third, understand what happens to your remaining time. Depending on your situation, Amazon may let your benefits continue until the end of your current billing period, or it may offer a refund. According to Amazon's own help pages, members who have not used their Prime benefits since their last payment may be eligible for a full refund, and members who have used benefits may be eligible for a partial refund in some cases, particularly on annual plans. The exact rules can change, so read the cancellation screens carefully, and if you are unsure, contact Amazon customer service directly through the official website or app. Never call a phone number you found in a random text message, pop-up, or email. Scammers love to pretend to be Amazon, especially when they know people are thinking about their Prime memberships. Amazon will not call you out of the blue asking for your bank details or asking you to buy gift cards. If anyone does, hang up.

Fourth, consider whether pausing makes more sense than canceling. In some cases, Amazon has offered members the option to pause their membership or be reminded before renewal, instead of canceling outright. Availability of these options can vary, and they may not appear for every account. But if you know you will want Prime again around the holidays, for example, it is worth seeing what choices appear on your screen. Just be sure you understand exactly when billing will start again, and put that date on your calendar.

And fifth, after you cancel, check your bank or credit card statement over the next billing cycle. Make sure no new Prime charge appears. If one does, contact Amazon right away through the official channels.

Let me also share a few common mistakes retirees make when it comes to Prime, so you can avoid them.

The first mistake is forgetting about the renewal date. An annual Prime membership renews automatically. The third mistake is having two memberships in the same household without realizing it. Sometimes a husband and wife each have their own Prime account, both paying full price.
