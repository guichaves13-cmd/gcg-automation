If you are paying for Amazon Prime mainly to get free shipping, you may be spending close to one hundred and forty dollars a year on something you could get for little or nothing. Consumer advocates have been pointing to the same handful of alternatives for years, and most people have never added them up. In the next few minutes, we will walk through every one of them, the real prices, the fine print, and the one situation where Prime still comes out ahead.

Let's start with the number that matters. As of this report, Amazon Prime costs fourteen dollars and ninety-nine cents a month, or one hundred and thirty-nine dollars a year if you pay up front. Prices can change, and Amazon has raised them before, so always check the current figure on your own account page. But at today's rate, a monthly member pays roughly one hundred and eighty dollars over twelve months. An annual member pays one hundred and thirty-nine. That is real money, especially for households on a fixed income, and especially for retirees who have watched grocery bills, insurance premiums and utility costs climb over the past few years.

Now, Prime is not just shipping. It bundles in Prime Video, music, photo storage, some grocery perks, and other extras. For some households, that bundle is worth every penny. We are not here to tell you to cancel anything. We are here to lay out the options so you can decide with your eyes open. And the first option is one that a surprising number of shoppers do not realize exists at all.

Amazon itself offers free shipping to customers who do not pay for Prime. On eligible orders, non-Prime shoppers can get free standard shipping once their order reaches thirty-five dollars. That threshold has moved over the years, so check the current rules at checkout, but as of this report, the figure is thirty-five dollars on eligible items. The catch is speed. Free standard shipping for non-members usually takes longer than the fast delivery Prime members are used to. Often it is several days, sometimes about a week. Not every item qualifies, and items sold by some third-party sellers may have their own shipping charges.

But think about how most people over fifty-five actually shop. Consumer advocates often point out that many older shoppers do not need a package on their doorstep tomorrow morning. They are restocking household basics, ordering a gift ahead of a birthday, or replacing a kitchen gadget. If you can plan a few days ahead, and if you can group your purchases so each order clears thirty-five dollars, you may be getting much of what you pay Prime for, without paying for Prime.

Here is a simple habit that money-saving writers recommend. Keep a running list. When you notice you are low on something, add it to your Amazon cart, but do not check out right away. Let the cart build until it passes thirty-five dollars. Then place one order. You get free shipping, you cut down on impulse buys, and you may find you spend less overall, because you are no longer clicking buy on every small thing the moment it occurs to you.

One more point on this. Amazon's "Subscribe and Save" program, which offers discounts on regularly delivered items, does not require a Prime membership. The discount levels and rules vary by product and change over time, so read the details on each item. But for things you buy on a schedule, like paper towels, vitamins or pet food, it is worth a look, member or not.

So that is option one, and it costs nothing. Option two comes from Amazon's biggest rival.

Walmart has its own membership, called Walmart Plus. As of this report, it costs ninety-eight dollars a year, or twelve dollars and ninety-five cents a month. That is about forty dollars a year less than Prime on the annual plan. Walmart Plus includes free shipping on online orders with no minimum, and it includes free grocery delivery from your local store on orders over a set amount, which Walmart lists as thirty-five dollars. Some fees can apply, such as for express delivery or for certain smaller orders, so read the terms. Walmart Plus has also offered a fuel discount at participating gas stations and a streaming perk, and those extras have changed over time, so check what is included when you sign up.

Here is the part many retirees and lower-income households miss. Walmart offers a discounted Walmart Plus membership, at fifty percent off, for people who receive certain government assistance, including Medicaid and SNAP benefits. That brings the cost down to roughly half the regular price. Amazon has a similar discount program for Prime, called Prime Access, for people on qualifying government assistance, so if you receive benefits, it is worth comparing both. But if grocery delivery is what you value most, consumer advocates often say the discounted Walmart Plus deserves a serious look.

And for many older shoppers, grocery delivery is the real prize. Carrying heavy bags of water, detergent, or pet food from the car to the kitchen is no small thing as we age. Having those items dropped at the door can make a real difference in safety and independence. If you live near a Walmart that delivers, and you buy most of your groceries there anyway, Walmart Plus may simply be a better fit than Prime for your household.

Now, here is something worth staying for. In a moment, we are going to talk about an option that costs nothing at all, requires no membership, and that consumer advocates say is one of the most underused ways to skip shipping fees entirely. Most people walk past it every single week. But first, let's talk about Target.

Target's paid membership is called Target Circle three sixty. As of this report, it costs ninety-nine dollars a year for the general public. There is a lower price for members of Target's credit and debit card program, and Target has run promotional pricing at times, so check the current rate. Target Circle three sixty includes same-day delivery from your local store on orders over thirty-five dollars, along with free two-day shipping on many items ordered online.

But here is what consumer advocates find more interesting. You do not need to pay for anything to get free shipping from Target in many cases. Target offers free standard shipping to members of its free loyalty program, Target Circle, on many orders over a certain amount. That threshold has been thirty-five dollars, but terms change, so check at checkout. Target also offers free shipping on many orders for holders of its own card. And Target Circle, the free version, gives you access to weekly deals and a birthday reward. It costs nothing to join.

That brings us to the option we promised you. The one most people walk past.

It is called buy online, pick up in store. Walmart, Target, Best Buy, Home Depot, Lowe's, Kohl's, and many other big retailers let you order on their website or app, pay online, and then pick up the item at your local store, often the same day, and usually for free. No membership required. No minimum order in many cases. No waiting several days for a delivery truck.

Many stores now offer curbside pickup or drive-up service, where you park in a marked spot, tap a button in the app, and an employee brings your order out to your car and puts it in your trunk. Target calls this Drive Up. Walmart offers free curbside pickup for grocery orders, although minimums and fees can apply depending on your store, so check before you order. For seniors with mobility challenges, or anyone who simply does not want to walk through a giant store, this can be a real gift. You never have to leave the driver's seat.

Consumer advocates like this option for a few reasons. First, it is free. Second, you avoid the risk of a package sitting on your porch where it could be stolen, which has become a real problem in many neighborhoods. Third, if something is wrong with the item, you can often return it right there at the store. And fourth, you are not locked into one retailer. You can compare prices across several stores and pick up wherever it is cheapest.

Here is a practical tip. Before you buy something on Amazon, take thirty seconds to check the same item on the website of a store near you. Many retailers show local stock right on the product page, and some will tell you if an item is ready for pickup within a couple of hours. If the price is close, picking it up yourself might save you the shipping fee or the membership fee entirely.

Now let's talk about the warehouse clubs, because many of our viewers already belong to one.

Costco and Sam's Club both charge annual membership fees, and both have raised those fees in recent years. As of this report, Costco's basic Gold Star membership is roughly sixty-five dollars a year, and Sam's Club's basic Club membership is roughly fifty dollars a year. Both offer higher tiers with extra rewards. Prices change, so confirm the current rates directly with each club.

What do these memberships have to do with shipping? Quite a bit, as it turns out. Sam's Club offers free curbside pickup for members on many orders, and its higher Plus tier includes free shipping on many items. Costco ships many items with the shipping cost already built into the online price, and it offers same-day grocery delivery in many areas through a partner service, though prices for delivered items are often higher than in the warehouse. Neither of these is a perfect Prime replacement. But if you already pay for a warehouse club, consumer advocates often suggest you look at what that membership already includes before you pay for another one on top of it.

The bigger lesson here is about stacking. A lot of households are paying for Prime, Costco, Walmart Plus, and a pile of streaming services, all at once, without realizing how much the benefits overlap. Sitting down once a year and listing every membership you pay for, what it costs, and what you actually use, is one of the simplest money moves financial counselors recommend. Many people find at least one membership they could drop.

Now, here is a source of free stuff that has nothing to do with shopping at all, but that can dramatically cut down on how much you need to order. Your local public library.

A library card is free in most communities for residents. And today's library is not just books on a shelf. Through apps like Libby, many libraries let you borrow ebooks and audiobooks on your phone, tablet, or e-reader, without ever leaving home. Through Hoopla, offered by many libraries, you can stream movies, TV shows, music, and audiobooks. Through Kanopy, also available at many libraries, you can stream films, documentaries, and classic movies. Which services your library offers depends on where you live, so ask at the front desk or check your library's website.

Why does this matter for shipping? Because a lot of the packages people order are books, DVDs, puzzle books, and magazines. Many libraries also lend things you might not expect: board games, tools, kitchen equipment, museum passes, even telescopes or sewing machines in some places. Before you order something you will only use once or twice, it is worth asking whether your library has it. Every item you borrow instead of buy is one less package and one less charge on your card.

And many libraries offer free home delivery of books for patrons who are homebound because of age, illness, or disability. If getting to the library is hard for you or for a parent you care for, call and ask. That service exists in many places, and many people who qualify have never heard of it.

Before we wrap up the alternatives, let's do a little math together, because this is where the decision really gets made. Grab a pencil if you like.

Start with how many Amazon orders you placed in the last year. Your account will show you your order history, and you can scroll back through it. Let's say you placed twenty orders. If you paid one hundred and thirty-nine dollars for an annual Prime membership, and shipping was the only benefit you used, you paid just under seven dollars per order for shipping. Now ask yourself how many of those twenty orders would have cleared thirty-five dollars anyway, or could have been combined with another order. If most of them could, the math starts to tilt away from Prime.

Now flip it around. Let's say you placed eighty orders, many of them small, many of them things you needed quickly. That works out to less than two dollars per order. At that pace, Prime is doing a lot of work for you, and dropping it might cost you more than you save.

Consumer advocates say the right answer is not the same for every household, and that is exactly why it is worth running your own numbers instead of following what a neighbor or an advertisement says. A ten-minute look at your order history can tell you more than any review.

There is another factor that does not show up in the math, and that is returns. Amazon has made returns fairly easy, with drop-off points at places like Whole Foods, Kohl's, and UPS stores in many areas, and many returns are free. Free return policies apply to many items whether or not you are a Prime member, but rules vary by item and seller, so check the return window and conditions before you buy. Walmart and Target also accept many online returns in store. If you return things often, find out which of your local stores makes that easiest, because a hassle-free return can be worth more than a few days of faster shipping.

And do not forget the smaller retailers in your own town. Many local pharmacies, hardware stores, and independent shops now take phone or online orders and will set items aside for you at the counter. Some will even deliver locally for older customers. It never hurts to ask, and you may be supporting a business that has served your community for decades.

One more idea that families tell us works well. If you have adult children or grandchildren who already pay for Prime, Amazon allows some benefit sharing within a household through a feature called Amazon Household. The rules about who counts as a household member and which benefits can be shared are specific, so read them carefully on Amazon's site. But for some families, it means one membership can cover more than one person under the same roof. What you should not do is share passwords with people outside your household, which can break a service's terms and put your account security at risk. When in doubt, keep your login to yourself.

So let's put it all together. We have looked at Amazon's own free shipping over thirty-five dollars for non-members. Walmart Plus at ninety-eight dollars a year, with half off for people on Medicaid or SNAP. Target's free Circle program and its paid Circle three sixty at ninety-nine dollars a year. Free buy-online, pick-up-in-store at many major retailers. Costco and Sam's Club, if you already belong. And your free library card.

Now we owe you the other side of the story. Because there are real situations where Amazon Prime still wins, and it would not be fair to pretend otherwise.

Prime still wins if you live in a rural area far from big-box stores. If the nearest Walmart or Target is an hour's drive away, buy online and pick up in store is not really an option, and gas costs money too. For many rural households, fast doorstep delivery is genuinely valuable, and Prime's delivery network reaches a lot of places.

Prime may also win if you order many small items frequently and cannot easily wait to bundle them. If you are someone who regularly needs a single item quickly, say a phone charger, a specific medical supply, or a part for a home device, the cost of shipping each one separately without a membership could add up fast.

Prime may win if your household genuinely uses the other benefits. If you watch Prime Video regularly, use Amazon Music, store family photos with Amazon Photos, and shop at Whole Foods with Prime discounts, the bundle value can outweigh the cost. The question is whether you would pay for those things separately if Prime did not exist.

And Prime may win if you rely on Amazon for things you cannot easily find locally. Specialty products, certain mobility aids, hard-to-find hobby supplies. Amazon's selection is enormous, and that matters.

There is one more thing consumer advocates suggest. If you are not sure, you do not have to decide all at once. You can turn off auto-renewal on Prime and see how it goes. Amazon also lets members pause or cancel, and depending on your usage, you may be eligible for a partial refund. Check the terms on your account page. Some shoppers switch to the monthly plan during the holiday season, when they order more, and cancel the rest of the year. Others sign up only during big sales events. That flexibility is worth using.

A word of caution here. Be careful with free trials of any membership, whether it is Prime, Walmart Plus, or something else. Free trials almost always turn into paid memberships automatically if you do not cancel in time. Put a reminder on your calendar a few days before the trial ends. And watch out for scams. Criminals often send fake emails and text messages pretending to be from Amazon, Walmart, or delivery companies, claiming there is a problem with your membership or your package. Never click links in unexpected messages. Go directly to the official website or app by typing it in yourself, or call the number on the back of your card.

So here is a quick checklist to take with you. One, look at your last few months of Amazon orders and ask how many truly needed to arrive fast. Two, if you can wait a few days, try grouping orders to clear the thirty-five dollar free shipping threshold. Three, if grocery delivery is your priority, compare Walmart Plus and Prime, and if you receive Medicaid or SNAP, check the discounted versions of both. Four, try buy online, pick up in store at least once, especially curbside. Five, list every membership you pay for and see where they overlap. And six, get a library card if you do not already have one, and ask what digital services and home delivery options they offer.

None of this requires giving anything up you truly value. It is simply about paying for what you use, and not a dollar more. Prices and programs change often, so whatever you decide, check the current terms directly with each company before you commit.
