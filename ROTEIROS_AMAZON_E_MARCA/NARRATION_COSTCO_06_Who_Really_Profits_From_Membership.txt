Sixty-five dollars. That is what millions of Americans hand over every year just for the right to walk into a Costco warehouse. It feels like a small price for cheap gas and bulk groceries. But follow that money, and you find something remarkable: those fees make up the majority of Costco's operating profit. So who is really getting rich off your card? Today we trace every dollar, from shareholders to employees, and show you exactly how to make that card pay you back.

Let's begin with the numbers that matter. Since September 2024, the basic Costco Gold Star membership in the United States costs sixty-five dollars a year. The Executive membership costs one hundred thirty dollars a year. Before that change, the fees were sixty dollars and one hundred twenty dollars, a level that had been in place since 2017. Costco has historically raised its fees roughly every five to six years, and each increase tends to make headlines, because so many households pay it.

Now here is the first surprising fact. Costco is a retailer that sells tens of billions of dollars of merchandise every year, yet the majority of its operating profit does not come from selling merchandise at all. It comes from membership fees. Let that sink in for a moment. A company famous for selling televisions, tires, rotisserie chickens, and giant packs of paper towels earns most of its operating profit from the cards in its members' wallets.

How can that be? The answer goes back to Costco's pricing philosophy. Company executives have described over the years a policy of capping markups at roughly fourteen percent on branded items and fifteen percent on Kirkland Signature products. On top of that, Costco keeps costs low by carrying a limited selection, selling in bulk, and running no-frills warehouses. That means the profit on each product is thin. The company makes enough on merchandise to cover the cost of running the business, more or less, and then the membership fees flow through as something close to pure profit.

Think of it this way. When you pay sixty-five dollars, Costco does not have to buy anything, ship anything, or stock anything in exchange for that money. It is revenue that comes with very little direct cost attached. Multiply that by tens of millions of paying members, and you start to see why Wall Street watches Costco membership numbers so closely.

And this brings us to the second remarkable number: the renewal rate. Costco has reported a renewal rate of around ninety percent in the United States and Canada. That means roughly nine out of every ten members who could renew, do renew. In the world of subscriptions, that is an extraordinary level of loyalty. Many streaming services, magazines, and gym memberships would love to keep even a fraction of that. For Costco, it means a reliable, predictable stream of income year after year, which investors value very highly.

So let's answer the question in the title, one group at a time. Who really profits from your sixty-five dollar card?

The first group is Costco's shareholders. Costco is a publicly traded company, and like any public company, its profits ultimately belong to its owners, the people and institutions that hold its stock. That includes large investment funds, pension plans, and everyday Americans who own Costco through retirement accounts or index funds. If you own a broad stock market fund in your IRA or 401(k), there is a good chance you own a tiny slice of Costco yourself, which means you may be on both sides of this story. You pay the fee as a member, and a very small share of the profit may come back to you as an investor.

Costco has rewarded its shareholders with a regular quarterly dividend, and on several occasions it has also paid large special dividends, one-time cash payments returned to stockholders. One of the most notable was a special dividend of fifteen dollars per share announced in late 2023 and paid in early 2024. Payments like that are possible in part because of the steady cash generated by membership fees. When analysts talk about Costco being a dependable business, the membership model is usually at the heart of the conversation.

But to be fair, it is not only shareholders. The second group that benefits is Costco's employees. Costco has long been known for paying its warehouse workers more than many other retailers, and for offering benefits that are considered generous in the industry. The company has announced wage increases on several occasions in recent years, and its pay and benefits have been widely reported to be above what many retail competitors offer. Costco has also been known for relatively low employee turnover compared with much of retail, and many observers connect that to how the company treats its workers.

Why does this matter to you as a member? Because a stable, experienced workforce can mean better service, faster checkout lines, and staff who actually know the products. Some business writers have argued that Costco's approach of paying well and keeping prices low only works because the membership fees provide such a steady base of profit. In other words, part of your sixty-five dollars helps support a model that many observers view as better for workers than the typical retail approach.

Stay with me, because the third group that profits from your membership card may be the one you least expect, and it is the one this video is really about. It might be you.

That's right. The third group that can profit from the membership fee is the member. That is the entire bargain Costco is offering. The company is essentially saying: pay us a fee up front, and we will sell you merchandise at prices kept close to our cost. If you shop enough, the savings on what you buy can be far bigger than the fee. If you do not shop enough, Costco keeps your fee and you do not get much in return. The fee is a bet, and whether you win that bet depends on how you use the card.

So let's look at exactly how to make sure the card pays off for you.

Start with a simple question: how much do you actually spend at Costco in a year? If you are not sure, Costco can show you your purchase history, because every transaction is tied to your membership number. You can ask at the membership counter, or check your account online. Many members are surprised by the number when they finally look.

Now let's talk about the Executive membership, because this is where the math gets interesting. The Executive membership costs one hundred thirty dollars, which is sixty-five dollars more than the Gold Star membership. In exchange, Executive members earn an annual reward of two percent on most eligible Costco purchases. When the fees went up in September 2024, Costco also raised the maximum annual reward for Executive members to twelve hundred fifty dollars, up from one thousand dollars. Some categories are excluded from the reward, such as gasoline, tobacco, and certain other items, so check the current terms.

Here is the break-even math, and it is easy to remember. The extra cost of the Executive membership is sixty-five dollars. Two percent of three thousand two hundred fifty dollars is sixty-five dollars. So if you spend more than about three thousand two hundred fifty dollars a year on eligible purchases, the Executive reward covers the extra cost of the upgrade. Below that amount, the basic membership is usually the better choice. That works out to about two hundred seventy dollars a month. For a household that does most of its grocery shopping at Costco, that is quite achievable. For a single retiree who stops in once a month for a few items, it may not be.

And here is a helpful detail. Costco has a long-standing policy that if your Executive reward does not cover the difference between the Executive and Gold Star fees, you can ask to downgrade, and you may be refunded the difference. Policies like this can change, so confirm the current rules before relying on them. But historically it has meant the Executive upgrade carried less risk than many people assume.

Now let's go beyond the reward check and look at the other ways members recover the cost of the card.

The first is gasoline. For many members, Costco gas stations are one of the biggest reasons to keep the card. Costco gas prices are often lower than nearby stations, although the gap varies by region and by week. If you drive a lot, even a modest savings per gallon can add up over a year. Do the math for yourself: if you buy fifteen gallons a week and save a dime or more a gallon, that alone can cover a large part of the fee over a year.

The second is prescriptions and pharmacy. Many people do not realize that, under federal law, you do not need a membership to use a Costco pharmacy. However, members may have access to additional pharmacy discount programs. If you take medications regularly, especially ones not fully covered by insurance, it can be worth comparing Costco pharmacy prices with what you pay elsewhere. Always talk with your pharmacist and check with your insurance plan or Medicare Part D plan before switching.

The third is hearing aids and optical. Costco has a large hearing aid business and optical departments in many warehouses. For older adults, hearing aids and eyeglasses are significant expenses, and these departments are often mentioned by members as places where the membership paid for itself many times over. Compare prices and services carefully, and keep in mind that the right choice depends on your individual needs and your audiologist or eye doctor's advice.

The fourth is the food court and the loss leaders. The hot dog and soda combo has been priced at one dollar and fifty cents since 1985. The rotisserie chicken is sold for four dollars and ninety-nine cents, widely described as a loss leader, and Costco built its own poultry operation in Nebraska, Lincoln Premium Poultry, to help supply chickens and keep that price stable. These items will not pay for your membership on their own, but they are a reminder of how far the company will go to protect its reputation for value.

The fifth is the larger purchases. Tires, appliances, electronics, travel packages, and other big-ticket items can produce savings that dwarf the cost of the membership. If you are planning one or two major purchases in a year, compare carefully, and include any Executive reward and any extended warranty or return policy in your comparison.

And the sixth is simply your weekly staples. Coffee, paper goods, cleaning supplies, batteries, canned goods, olive oil, and Kirkland Signature items generally. This is where steady, quiet savings come from. The key is to buy what you actually use and to compare unit prices, not just package prices.

Now let's be honest about the other side, because this channel is not here to sell you a membership. There are people for whom the Costco card does not pay off. If you live alone, have limited storage space, rarely drive, or only shop occasionally, the fee may cost you more than you save. Bulk buying can also tempt people into spending more than they planned. A cart full of impulse purchases can erase the savings in a single trip. And perishable food that spoils before you can eat it is money thrown away, no matter how low the unit price was.

There are a few ways to soften those risks. One is to share. A Costco membership typically includes a free household card for another person living at the same address. That means a spouse or partner can shop on the same membership. Some people also shop with a friend or neighbor who is a member and split bulk purchases. Another option is to try the membership for a year and check your results. Costco has historically offered a satisfaction guarantee on the membership itself, allowing a refund of the fee if you are not satisfied, though, as always, check the current terms before you rely on that.

There is one more trap worth mentioning, and it catches many people: the membership you forgot you had. Many members set up automatic renewal on a credit card and never think about it again. That is convenient, and for frequent shoppers it makes sense. But if your shopping habits have changed, perhaps because you moved, downsized, lost a spouse, or simply stopped driving to the warehouse, an automatic renewal can keep charging you year after year for a card you rarely use. Take a moment to check your credit card statements for the renewal charge, and decide on purpose whether you still want it.

The same goes for the Executive upgrade. Costco staff sometimes mention the upgrade at the register, often pointing out how much your reward would have been on that day's purchase. That information can be genuinely useful. But a single big shopping trip does not tell you what a whole year looks like. Before you upgrade, look at your full annual spending, not just one receipt.

It is also worth understanding why Costco is so focused on renewals, because it explains a lot about the experience inside the warehouse. A company that earns most of its operating profit from fees needs members to feel, every single visit, that they are getting value. That is one reason the company protects symbols like the one dollar fifty hot dog combo and the four ninety-nine rotisserie chicken. Those prices are not just about food. They are about reminding you, trip after trip, that the card is worth keeping. When you understand that, you can enjoy those deals for what they are while still keeping your own budget firmly in charge.

And there is a lesson here about the fee increases themselves. When Costco raised its fees in September 2024, many members wondered whether they should cancel. History offers some perspective. After past increases, the renewal rate has remained strong, which suggests most members concluded the savings still outweighed the cost. But averages do not pay your bills. What matters is your own situation. A five dollar increase on the basic card is small for a big family that shops weekly, and more noticeable for a single retiree who shops occasionally. Run your own numbers rather than following the crowd.

Finally, consider the value of things that are hard to put a price on. Some members say they value the return policy, the recall notifications that come because Costco knows what each member bought, or simply the time saved by buying in bulk and making fewer trips to the store. For an older adult who finds shopping tiring, fewer trips can be worth something real. Others find the large warehouses, crowds, and heavy packages exhausting. Both views are valid, and both belong in your decision.

So let's step back and look at the full picture of who profits.

Costco profits, because membership fees make up the majority of its operating profit and come with very little direct cost. Its shareholders profit, through regular dividends, occasional special dividends, and a business model that investors view as remarkably steady. Its employees benefit, from a company widely reported to pay and treat workers better than many retail competitors, an approach that the reliable membership income helps support. And you, the member, can profit too, but only if you use the card in a way that makes the savings bigger than the fee.

There is something worth appreciating about that arrangement. Many companies make money by finding ways to charge you more on each purchase. Costco's model, as company executives have described it, works the other way. It makes most of its operating profit up front, from the fee, and then has an incentive to keep prices low so that you feel good about renewing. That ninety percent renewal rate is the scorecard. If members stopped feeling like they were getting a good deal, that number would drop, and Wall Street would notice immediately.

That does not mean you should renew automatically. The smartest members treat the fee like any other expense and check it once a year. Here is a simple annual review you can do in about ten minutes.

First, look up your total Costco spending for the past year through your membership account or at the membership counter.

Second, if you have the Executive membership, look at your reward. If it was less than sixty-five dollars, consider whether the basic membership would serve you better. If you spent well over three thousand two hundred fifty dollars, the Executive membership likely made sense.

Third, think about your biggest savings. Gas, pharmacy, hearing aids, glasses, tires, and big-ticket purchases. Write down a rough estimate of how much you saved compared with other stores.

Fourth, think about your waste. Did you throw away food? Did you buy things you did not need? Be honest with yourself.

Fifth, decide. If your savings comfortably beat the fee, renew with confidence. If they did not, it is perfectly reasonable to let the membership lapse, share a card within your household, or shop with a friend.

For retirees in particular, this kind of yearly check can be valuable. On a fixed income, sixty-five or one hundred thirty dollars a year is real money. But so are the savings on gas, prescriptions, hearing aids, and groceries. The point is not that Costco is good or bad. The point is that the membership is a financial decision, and like every financial decision, it deserves a little arithmetic.

Let's also put the fee in perspective with a quick comparison. Sixty-five dollars a year works out to about five dollars and forty cents a month, or roughly eighteen cents a day. For a household that saves even ten dollars a month on groceries and gas, the basic membership pays for itself and then some. For a household that rarely shops there, those eighteen cents a day are simply a cost. That is the whole equation, stripped down to its simplest form.

And it is worth remembering how the money moves through the system. Your fee helps fund the business. The business keeps product markups low, which is what you see on the shelf. The steady profit supports the dividends paid to shareholders and helps support the wages and benefits paid to employees. And if you shop wisely, the low prices return more to you than the fee you paid. When it works well, everyone in that chain gets something. When it does not work for you, you are quietly subsidizing everyone else.

So who really profits from your sixty-five dollar Costco card? Costco does, its shareholders do, and its employees do. The surprising part is that you can be on that list too, but only if you do the math, shop with a plan, and use the card for what it does best.
