That was the day Costco raised its membership fees in the United States and Canada for the first time in about seven years. The basic Gold Star membership, and the Business membership, went from sixty dollars a year to sixty-five dollars. The Executive membership went from one hundred and twenty dollars to one hundred and thirty dollars. At the same time, Costco raised the maximum annual reward that Executive members can earn. That two percent reward on qualifying purchases used to top out at one thousand dollars a year. After the change, the cap rose to one thousand two hundred and fifty dollars.

Now, on paper, those are modest increases. Five dollars here, ten dollars there. Costco's renewal rate in the United States and Canada has stayed remarkably high, at roughly ninety percent, according to the company's own reporting. People love the store. That has not changed. But what has changed, for a lot of older shoppers, is the way they look at that Executive upgrade. Because when you are living on a fixed income, every recurring charge gets a second look. So let's do that math together, slowly, the way you would at your kitchen table.

The Executive membership costs one hundred and thirty dollars. The Gold Star membership costs sixty-five dollars. The difference between them is sixty-five dollars. The main thing you get for it is that two percent annual reward on most Costco purchases. So the question becomes simple. How much do you have to spend at Costco in a year for two percent to equal sixty-five dollars?

Here is the answer. Sixty-five divided by point zero two. That comes out to three thousand two hundred and fifty dollars. Three thousand two hundred and fifty dollars a year in qualifying purchases. That is your break-even point. If you spend more than that, the Executive card pays for its own upgrade, and every dollar above that line is a genuine bonus. If you spend less than that, you are paying Costco more than you are getting back.

Let's put that number into monthly terms, because that is how most of us think about groceries. If a big chunk of your Costco spending is fill-ups at the gas pump, that money may not be moving you toward your break-even at all.

Now here is where the retiree story really comes into focus. For a family of five, with teenagers who eat like a small army, spending three or four hundred dollars a month at Costco is easy. When the household shrinks, the Costco cart shrinks with it. And when the cart shrinks, that two percent reward shrinks too. A couple who once spent six thousand dollars a year at Costco, earning a comfortable one hundred and twenty dollar reward, might now be spending twenty-five hundred dollars a year. At that level, the reward is only about fifty dollars. They are paying sixty-five extra to get fifty back. They are losing fifteen dollars a year, and they may not even realize it, because the reward certificate still shows up every year and it still feels like free money.

That is the quiet trap. The reward feels like a gift. But it is not a gift. It is a partial refund of a fee you already paid. And if the refund is smaller than the extra fee, you are behind.

So how do you find out where you actually stand? You don't have to guess. Costco tracks this for you. If you have the Costco app or an online account, you can usually see your accumulated Executive reward balance. Your annual reward certificate also tells you exactly what you earned the previous year. Take that number and compare it to sixty-five dollars. If your reward was less than sixty-five dollars, the Executive upgrade did not pay for itself last year. If it was well above sixty-five, it did. It really is that straightforward.

There's one more wrinkle worth knowing. Costco has long promoted a guarantee on the Executive upgrade. But first, let's be fair to the Executive card, because Costco has not been sitting still. The company clearly knows that the Executive membership is a big part of its business, and it has been adding reasons to keep it.

The biggest new perk arrived on June thirtieth, twenty twenty-five. Starting that day, Executive members got access to special early shopping hours at most Costco warehouses in the United States. The general idea is that Executive members can get in and shop for about an hour before regular opening at most warehouses. Costco also adjusted some of its regular operating hours around the same time, and it extended evening hours at many of its gas stations for Executive members as well. Let's also talk about the card scanners, because they've changed the experience of shopping at Costco. So let's say you've done the math, and you've decided the Executive card isn't pulling its weight anymore. How do you actually downgrade?

The process is usually simple, and you generally do it in person at the membership desk inside your warehouse. Bring your membership card and a photo ID. Tell the employee you'd like to change your membership from Executive to Gold Star. In many cases, you can also contact Costco member services by phone to ask about it, though procedures can vary.

Now here's the refund rule we promised you, and this is the part that trips people up. Costco has a well-known policy that it will refund your membership fee at any time if you are dissatisfied. That is one of the most generous policies in retail. But when it comes to the Executive upgrade, Costco's terms generally say that if you cancel or downgrade, any Executive reward you've already earned or received can be deducted from the refund of the upgrade fee. In plain English, Costco won't let you collect the two percent reward and then also get the upgrade fee back. The two are linked.

So timing matters. Let's walk through two examples. In the first example, you downgrade early in your membership year, before you've earned much of a reward. In that case, you may be able to get back much of the upgrade fee difference, because there's little reward to subtract. In the second example, you wait until after your annual reward certificate has been issued and you've already spent it. At that point, there may be little or nothing left to refund on the upgrade, because you've already received the benefit.

That's why many people find the cleanest moment to downgrade is right around renewal time. At renewal, you simply choose to renew at the Gold Star level instead of the Executive level. You pay sixty-five dollars instead of one hundred and thirty. You use up whatever reward certificate you've already earned, and you move forward on the cheaper plan. There's no awkward calculation about refunds, because you're simply choosing what to buy for the year ahead.

If you're unsure how the refund would work in your particular case, ask the desk employee to explain the numbers before you sign anything. Ask directly, "If I downgrade today, how much will I get back, and will any reward be deducted?" A good employee will walk you through it. And remember, policies can change, so the answer you get at the desk is the one that counts.

There's another point worth mentioning. That card's rewards are separate from the Executive reward. So if you downgrade from Executive to Gold Star, you would typically still earn whatever cash back your credit card provides. You'd only be giving up the extra two percent from the Executive membership itself. That's important, because some people mistakenly believe that downgrading wipes out all their Costco rewards. It doesn't. The two programs are different. As always, check the current terms of your own card, because credit card benefits change.

Now let's be balanced, because this is not a story about everyone ditching the Executive card. For a lot of households, the Executive card is a great deal. So when should you keep it?

Keep it if you comfortably spend more than about three thousand two hundred and fifty dollars a year on qualifying Costco purchases. Keep it if you truly value the early shopping hour. If quiet aisles and an easy parking lot make the difference between dreading a Costco trip and enjoying it, that's a real benefit. Only you can decide what it's worth.

On the other hand, consider downgrading if your annual reward has been under sixty-five dollars for a year or two in a row. Consider it if your household has shrunk and you're buying less in bulk.
