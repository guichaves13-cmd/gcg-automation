If you pay for Amazon Prime every month, you could be handing Amazon roughly forty extra dollars a year without even realizing it. And if you pay once a year, you might be paying for months you never use. Across the country, Prime members are quietly changing how they pay, and some are cutting their Prime costs significantly. In the next few minutes, I will show you the exact math, the strategy smart shoppers use, and the rules you need to know before you switch anything.

Let's begin with the prices, because everything in this story comes down to two numbers. In the United States, a standard Amazon Prime membership currently costs fourteen dollars and ninety-nine cents per month, or one hundred thirty-nine dollars per year if you pay annually. Those are the prices as of this recording. Amazon has raised Prime prices several times over the years, so always check the current price on your own account before making a decision.

Now, here is where it gets interesting. Take that monthly price, fourteen ninety-nine, and multiply it by twelve months. You get one hundred seventy-nine dollars and eighty-eight cents. Call it about one hundred eighty dollars a year. Compare that to the annual plan at one hundred thirty-nine dollars. The difference is about forty-one dollars. That means if you keep Prime all year long, and you pay month by month, you are paying roughly thirty percent more than someone who pays once a year for exactly the same membership. Same shipping. Same Prime Video. Same everything. Just a different way of paying.

So you might think the answer is obvious. Just switch to annual and save forty dollars. And for some people, that is exactly the right move. But here is the twist that many members are discovering. For a lot of households, the smartest choice is not paying monthly all year, and it is not paying annually either. It is paying monthly, but only during the months when Prime actually earns its keep.

Let me explain.

Think about how you really shop. For many families and retirees, Amazon purchases are not spread evenly across the year. There are busy months and quiet months. The busy times often include the summer, when Amazon runs its big Prime Day sales event, usually in July. And there is the holiday season, from roughly late October through December, when people are buying gifts, decorations, and supplies. Amazon has also held a second members-only sale event in the fall in recent years, often in October. During those stretches, a Prime membership can be very valuable. You get fast free shipping on lots of orders, and you get access to deals that are offered only to members.

But what about February? What about April, or September? For many people, those months might involve one small order, or none at all. Paying fifteen dollars in a month when you place a single order is paying fifteen dollars for one free shipment. That is rarely a good deal.

So here is the strategy, and it is simple. Instead of keeping Prime twelve months a year, some members are signing up for the monthly plan only when they need it. They join a few days before Prime Day, enjoy the deals, and cancel after. They join again around the holidays, do their gift shopping with fast free shipping, and cancel after the new year. In between, they shop without Prime, or they shop elsewhere.

Let's run the numbers. Suppose you keep Prime for just four months out of the year, say July, October, November, and December. Four months at fourteen ninety-nine comes to about sixty dollars. Compare that to one hundred thirty-nine for the annual plan, or one hundred eighty for twelve months of monthly billing. That is a savings of roughly eighty dollars compared to annual, and roughly one hundred twenty dollars compared to paying monthly all year. Even if you keep it for six months, you are at about ninety dollars, still well below the annual price.

The break-even point is easy to figure out. One hundred thirty-nine divided by fourteen ninety-nine is a little over nine. That means if you would keep Prime for nine months or fewer in a year, paying monthly for just those months costs less than the annual plan. If you would keep it for ten months or more, the annual plan is the better deal.

Now, I want to be very clear about something. This is not a trick, and it is not a loophole. Amazon offers a monthly plan, and Amazon allows members to cancel. Using those options the way they are designed is simply being a careful shopper. Also, a quick reminder that this channel is not affiliated with Amazon in any way. We are not paid by them, and we are not telling you what to buy. We just want you to understand your options.

But there are some important rules you need to know before you try this, because if you are not careful, you could end up paying more, not less.

First, let's talk about free trials. Amazon frequently offers a free trial of Prime to people who are not currently members. The standard trial has typically been thirty days, though Amazon sometimes runs different offers. Now, here is the catch. Free trials are generally limited. According to Amazon's terms, you usually cannot get another free trial if you have had a Prime membership or a free trial within the past twelve months. So you cannot simply sign up for a new free trial every time Prime Day rolls around. If you used a trial last summer, you may not be eligible for another one this summer. Amazon decides eligibility, and the offer you see on your account is the one that applies to you.

Second, and this is critical, a free trial typically converts to a paid membership automatically when it ends. If you take a free trial before Prime Day, and you forget to cancel, Amazon will start charging you. Many people have been surprised by this. So if you start a trial, put the end date on your calendar right away, and set a reminder a few days before.

Third, understand what happens when you cancel a monthly membership. Generally, when you end a monthly Prime membership, you keep your benefits until the end of the billing period you already paid for, and then the membership stops. But the exact handling can depend on your situation, and Amazon may offer a refund in some cases. Always read the cancellation screens carefully so you know exactly when your benefits end.

Here is a smart tip. Some members choose to cancel right away after signing up, as long as Amazon's screens confirm that their benefits will continue until the end of the paid month. That way, they cannot forget to cancel later. But you need to read the screen carefully. If the screen says your membership ends immediately, or offers a refund instead, then canceling early might cut off your benefits before you are done shopping. Read every word before you click.

Stay with me, because there is one more piece of this puzzle that could save you even more, and it involves what happens if you already paid for a full year. But first, let's talk about the cancellation process itself, because it has a history you should know about.

For years, many Prime members complained that canceling was confusing. You would click cancel, and then you would see a page reminding you of everything you would lose, and then another page offering you a different plan, and another, until it felt nearly impossible to leave. The Federal Trade Commission took Amazon to court over this, arguing that the company used what are called dark patterns, which are designs that push people into decisions they did not intend, like signing up without clearly realizing it, or making it hard to cancel.

In September of twenty twenty-five, Amazon agreed to settle that case for two and a half billion dollars. That included a one billion dollar civil penalty and one and a half billion dollars in refunds to customers, with eligible customers able to receive up to fifty-one dollars. Amazon did not admit wrongdoing as part of the settlement. The claims process ran in twenty twenty-six, and that window is now closed, so new claims cannot be filed. But the case matters for anyone using the monthly strategy, because a big part of the strategy depends on being able to cancel cleanly. When you cancel, take your time, read every screen, look for the option that actually ends your membership, and do not stop until you see a clear confirmation. Save the confirmation email. And check your bank or card statement the following month to be sure no surprise charge appears.

Now, let's talk about the annual plan, and what happens if you already paid one hundred thirty-nine dollars and realize you do not need Prime for the rest of the year.

According to Amazon's own help pages, if you cancel a paid membership and you have not used any of your Prime benefits since your last payment, you may be eligible for a full refund. If you have used benefits, you may be eligible for a partial refund in some cases, particularly with an annual membership, and the amount generally depends on how much you have used. These rules can change, and Amazon decides the exact amount, so the cancellation screens will tell you what applies to you. The key point is this. If you paid for a full year and you have barely touched your membership, it may be worth going to your Prime settings and seeing what Amazon offers. You might get some of that money back.

But here is a word of caution. If you are on the annual plan and you use Prime regularly throughout the year, the annual plan is probably your best deal. Do not cancel it just because someone told you monthly is smarter. Monthly is only smarter if you would actually go several months without needing Prime.

Now let's talk about what life looks like during the months when you do not have Prime. Because the whole strategy only works if shopping without Prime is still practical. And for many people, it is more practical than they think.

First, free shipping without Prime. Amazon offers free standard shipping to non-members on eligible orders that reach a minimum amount. In the United States, that threshold has generally been thirty-five dollars for eligible items. The threshold has changed in the past and it may change again, so check it at checkout. Standard shipping is slower than Prime shipping. It may take several days, sometimes a week or more. But if you are not in a hurry, that is often perfectly fine.

Here is how smart shoppers handle this. Instead of placing a lot of small orders, they keep a running list. When they think of something they need, they add it to their cart or to a saved list. Once the cart reaches the free shipping threshold, they place one order. That way, they get free shipping without Prime, and they also tend to avoid impulse buys, which can save even more money.

Second, Subscribe and Save. Many people believe you need Prime to use Amazon's Subscribe and Save program, but that is not the case. Subscribe and Save lets you set up regular, automatic deliveries of items you use all the time, like coffee, vitamins, paper products, pet food, or household cleaners. You choose how often you want each item delivered, and you typically receive a discount on eligible items, with bigger discounts sometimes available when you have several subscription items arriving in the same delivery. Subscribe and Save is available to customers whether or not they have Prime, and according to Amazon, eligible Subscribe and Save orders generally ship free. The exact discounts and terms vary by item and can change, so look at the details on the product page. But for people who buy the same things over and over, this program can replace a big part of what they used Prime for.

Third, consider other stores. Many major retailers offer their own free shipping thresholds, store pickup options, or memberships. Comparing prices before you buy is a good habit, whether you have Prime or not.

And here is something to keep in mind as you compare. Amazon's store is not just Amazon. By Amazon's own reporting, third-party sellers, which are independent businesses that sell on Amazon's marketplace, account for roughly sixty percent of the units sold in its store. Prices and shipping options from those sellers vary, and not every item qualifies for free shipping. When you are shopping without Prime, look closely at who the seller is and whether the item is eligible for free shipping before you check out.

There is also one more point about the monthly strategy that you should consider, and it is about streaming. If you watch Prime Video regularly, going without Prime for months at a time means losing access to Prime Video during those months. For some people, that is a deal breaker. For others, it is no big deal. And it is worth knowing that since January of twenty twenty-four, Prime Video has shown ads by default. Members who want to remove those ads have to pay an extra two dollars and ninety-nine cents a month. So if you are already paying for Prime and that ad-free add-on, your total monthly cost is close to eighteen dollars. If Prime Video is a big reason you keep Prime, factor that into your decision. Some people rotate their streaming services too, keeping one or two at a time and switching every few months depending on what they want to watch.

Now, let's talk about households, because how you pay may also depend on who you share with. Amazon used to allow Prime members to share certain shipping benefits with someone outside their household through what was called the Prime Invitee program. That program ended in October of twenty twenty-five. What remains is Amazon Household, which lets two adults living in the same household share many Prime benefits under one membership, along with teens and children under certain rules. So if you and your spouse each have separate Prime memberships, you may be paying twice. Combining under one membership could save you a full membership fee. And if you were relying on a relative's Prime account through invitee sharing, you may now need to decide whether to get your own, and how to pay for it.

There is one more option you should know about, and it can cut your Prime cost dramatically. If you receive certain kinds of government assistance, you may qualify for Prime Access, a discounted membership that costs six dollars and ninety-nine cents a month. That is less than half the standard monthly price. Prime Access is available to people who receive benefits from qualifying programs, including Supplemental Security Income, Medicaid, and SNAP or EBT food benefits, among others. You need to verify your eligibility, and Amazon asks members to re-verify, generally every twelve months. According to Amazon, Prime Access includes the same core Prime benefits. If you qualify, that could be a far better deal than either the standard monthly or the annual plan. We have a separate video covering Prime Access in detail, so be sure to check our channel.

And for those of you who take regular generic medications, there is Amazon's RxPass program, which is available to Prime members for five dollars a month and covers a list of eligible generic medications. There are significant limits, though. RxPass is not offered in every state, and Amazon's published rules have excluded people using certain government health insurance programs, such as Medicare and Medicaid, in many cases. If you rely on RxPass, keep in mind that it requires an active Prime membership. That means the on-and-off monthly strategy may not work for you, and the annual plan could make more sense. Always check the current eligibility details and talk with your pharmacist.

So let's bring this all together. How should you decide which way to pay?

Here is a simple checklist you can use today.

Step one. Go to your Amazon account and open Your Orders. Count how many orders you placed in each month of the past year. Notice which months were busy and which were quiet.

Step two. Count how many months you truly needed Prime. Be honest. A month where you placed one order over thirty-five dollars probably did not need Prime at all.

Step three. If you needed Prime for ten or more months, the annual plan at one hundred thirty-nine dollars is likely your best deal. If you are currently paying monthly, switching to annual could save you around forty dollars a year.

Step four. If you needed Prime for nine months or fewer, consider the monthly plan, but only during the months you need it. Plan around big shopping periods like Prime Day and the holidays, and set clear reminders to cancel.

Step five. If you receive qualifying government benefits, check whether you are eligible for Prime Access at six ninety-nine a month, because that may beat every other option.

Step six. During the months without Prime, use the free shipping threshold for non-members, bundle your orders, and use Subscribe and Save for the items you buy regularly.

And step seven. Whatever you choose, protect yourself. Write down your renewal dates. Read every screen when you sign up or cancel. Save your confirmation emails. Check your statements. And never, ever click a link in a text or email claiming your Prime membership has a problem. Scammers send those messages all the time. Always go to Amazon's website or app directly by typing the address yourself.
