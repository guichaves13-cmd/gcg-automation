When you click "Buy Now" on Amazon, you probably think you're buying from Amazon. Most of the time, you're not. According to Amazon's own figures, roughly six out of every ten items sold on its store come from independent third-party sellers. Some are small American businesses. Many are based overseas. And the one line on the product page that tells you who you're really dealing with is a line most shoppers never read. Stay with me, because by the end of this video you'll know exactly how to check any seller in under a minute, what protection you have if something goes wrong, and the simple checklist that can keep your money safe.

Let's start with the basic question. What is Amazon, really? Most people picture a giant store, a warehouse full of shelves, and a company that buys products and sells them to you. That picture was close to accurate twenty-five years ago. It is not accurate today. Today Amazon is better described as a marketplace, a kind of enormous digital shopping mall, where Amazon owns the building, sets the rules, collects the rent, and also runs some of the stores inside. The rest of the stores are run by other people. Millions of them.

Amazon has said publicly, in its annual shareholder letters and other company statements, that third-party sellers account for around sixty percent of the units sold in its store. That share has grown steadily over the years. Back in the late nineteen nineties, it was essentially zero. By the end of the two thousands, it was a meaningful slice. Today, it is the majority. So when you browse Amazon for a phone charger, a kitchen gadget, a pair of reading glasses, or a bottle of vitamins, the odds are better than even that the product in your cart was listed, priced, and in many cases sourced by somebody who does not work for Amazon at all.

Now, that is not automatically a bad thing. Plenty of those independent sellers are honest, hard-working businesses. Some are family operations in Ohio or Texas. Some are well-known brands that sell directly through their own storefronts on Amazon. Some are retirees themselves, running a small side business from a spare bedroom. The marketplace has given a lot of small sellers access to customers they could never have reached on their own. But it also means the quality, the honesty, and the customer service you get can vary enormously from one listing to the next, even when the product pages look almost identical.

So who are these sellers? Here is where the answer may surprise you. A large and growing share of Amazon's top third-party sellers are based in China. Independent research firms that track the marketplace, such as Marketplace Pulse, have reported for several years that sellers based in mainland China and Hong Kong make up a very large portion of the most active sellers on Amazon's U.S. site, with estimates in recent years often landing around half of the top sellers, depending on how you measure it. Those numbers move up and down, and different analysts count differently, so treat any single figure as an estimate. But the overall trend is not in dispute. Many of the products you buy on Amazon are manufactured in China, and increasingly, they are also sold directly by Chinese companies rather than by an American middleman.

Look just below the price and the "Add to Cart" button. On most product pages, you will see a small block of text that says something like "Ships from" and then a name, and "Sold by" and then a name. On some versions of the page, it may be laid out a little differently, but the information is there. This little block is your window into who you're actually dealing with. Amazon changes its page design from time to time, so it may move around a bit, but it's worth hunting for every single time.

There are three basic combinations you'll see. The first is "Ships from Amazon, Sold by Amazon," or sometimes "Ships from and sold by Amazon dot com." That means Amazon itself is the retailer. Amazon bought the inventory, Amazon is selling it to you, and Amazon is responsible for the sale. This is the closest thing to buying from a traditional store.

The second combination is "Ships from Amazon, Sold by" some other name. Maybe it's a brand name you recognize, maybe it's a string of letters that looks like somebody's cat walked across a keyboard. This means a third-party seller owns the product and has sent it to Amazon's warehouses, using a program called Fulfillment by Amazon, often shortened to FBA. Amazon stores it, packs it, ships it, and handles much of the customer service and returns. But the seller is the one selling it to you. The seller set the price. The seller sourced the product. Amazon is acting as the warehouse and delivery service.

The third combination is "Ships from" a seller and "Sold by" that same seller. That means the third-party seller is doing everything: storing the product, packing it, and shipping it to you directly. Amazon is the marketplace where the sale happened, but the box may come from a warehouse anywhere in the world, delivery times can be longer, and returns may be handled by the seller rather than by Amazon.

Why does this matter so much? Because each of those three situations comes with different risks, different return experiences, and different levels of accountability. When something goes wrong, the first thing you'll want to know is who you need to contact, and that little line tells you.

Now here's something many shoppers have never heard of, and it's one of the biggest reasons counterfeits have been a problem on Amazon over the years. It's called commingled inventory. Let me explain how it works in plain English.

When a third-party seller uses Fulfillment by Amazon, they send their products to Amazon's warehouses. For certain products, Amazon has historically allowed what it calls commingling, sometimes described in seller materials as "stickerless" inventory. That means if five different sellers all send in what's supposed to be the exact same product, with the same barcode, Amazon may store all of those units together in one bin. When you place an order, Amazon may grab whichever unit is closest to you to speed up delivery, regardless of which seller you actually bought from.

In most cases, that works fine, because the products really are identical. But think about what happens if one of those five sellers sends in fakes. Now the fakes are mixed in with the real ones. You could buy from a perfectly honest seller and still receive a counterfeit, because the item that ended up in your box physically came from someone else's shipment. Brands and sellers have complained about this for years, and it has been widely reported in the business press. Amazon has offered tools that let brands and sellers opt out by labeling their own units, and the company has said it has invested heavily in fighting counterfeits, including blocking suspicious listings and pursuing bad actors in court. Amazon has also announced changes to how commingling works over time. But the core lesson for you as a shopper is this: the name in the "Sold by" line doesn't always guarantee where your specific unit came from, especially for small, popular, easy-to-fake items.

What kinds of products are most at risk? Generally, things that are small, popular, and expensive relative to their size. Think phone chargers and cables, memory cards, beauty products, supplements, designer-style accessories, printer ink, certain toys, and batteries. With some of these, a fake isn't just a waste of money. A counterfeit charger can overheat. A fake supplement may not contain what the label says. A knock-off product meant for children may not meet safety standards. For older shoppers buying health-related items in particular, this is worth taking seriously.

If you're still with me, you're already ahead of most shoppers, because in the next few minutes I'm going to walk you through exactly how to check a seller, what Amazon's A-to-z Guarantee really covers, and the step-by-step checklist I'd give to my own parents before they buy anything online. So stay with me.

Let's talk about how to check a seller. It takes less than a minute, and it can save you a great deal of frustration.

Step one. Find the "Sold by" name on the product page. In most layouts, that seller name is a clickable link. Click it. That takes you to the seller's profile page, sometimes called their storefront.

Step two. Look at the seller's feedback rating and the number of ratings. This is different from the product's star reviews. Product reviews tell you what people think of the item. Seller feedback tells you what people think of the business: did it ship on time, was the item as described, did the seller respond to problems. A seller with ninety-eight percent positive feedback over thousands of ratings and several years of history is a very different proposition from a seller with a handful of ratings who appeared last month.

Step three. Look at how long the seller has been around. Many seller profiles show feedback over the past thirty days, ninety days, twelve months, and lifetime. If a seller has almost no lifetime history but suddenly has very low prices on a popular brand-name item, be cautious. That pattern is something consumer advocates have long warned about.

Step four. Scroll down to the detailed seller information. Amazon has required sellers to display certain business information, which can include a business name and an address. This requirement became more consistent after a federal law called the INFORM Consumers Act took effect in 2023, which requires online marketplaces to collect and, for many high-volume sellers, disclose certain information so consumers can tell who they're dealing with. You may see a business name you've never heard of, and an address in another state or another country. That doesn't mean the seller is bad. It simply tells you who you're dealing with and where, which helps you set your expectations for shipping time, customer service, and returns.

Step five. Read the most recent negative feedback, not just the overall score. Sort or scroll to see what unhappy customers are saying, and pay attention to dates. If you see several recent complaints saying "this was fake," "this was used," or "never arrived," take that seriously, even if the overall rating looks fine.

Step six. Compare the seller's price to the price from Amazon itself or from the brand's official storefront, if one exists. A small discount is normal. A price that's dramatically lower than everyone else's on a name-brand item is a classic warning sign.

Now let's talk about what happens if something goes wrong anyway. This is where Amazon's A-to-z Guarantee comes in, and it's one of the most useful protections most shoppers have never fully read.

The A-to-z Guarantee is Amazon's protection program for purchases made from third-party sellers. In broad terms, it's designed to cover you when you buy from a third-party seller and the item doesn't arrive, arrives damaged or defective, is materially different from what was described, or when the seller doesn't honor a return that should have been accepted under Amazon's policies. The exact rules, time limits, and coverage amounts are set by Amazon and can change, so always check the current terms on Amazon's help pages. But the general process looks like this.

First, you're usually expected to contact the seller directly through your Amazon account. Go to "Your Orders," find the item, and look for an option to contact the seller or get help with the order. Explain the problem clearly and politely. Keep everything in writing inside Amazon's messaging system, not over private email or phone, so there's a record Amazon can see.

Second, give the seller a short window to respond. Amazon's policy has generally asked customers to allow the seller a couple of business days to respond before escalating.

Third, if the seller doesn't fix the problem, you can file an A-to-z Guarantee claim from your order page. Amazon reviews the claim and, if it agrees, can issue a refund. Be aware there are time limits on when you can file, measured from the delivery date or the latest estimated delivery date, so don't wait months.

Here are a few important things the A-to-z Guarantee generally does not do. It is not a warranty that a product will last for years. It typically doesn't cover services, and purchases paid for outside Amazon's checkout aren't covered at all. That last point is critical. If a seller ever contacts you and asks you to pay them directly by wire transfer, gift card, payment app, or any method outside of Amazon's own checkout, that is a major red flag. Legitimate Amazon purchases are paid through Amazon. Scammers frequently try to move people off the platform precisely because, once they do, Amazon's protections usually no longer apply.

While we're on the subject of scams, let's be very clear about something that affects older Americans in particular. The Federal Trade Commission has reported for years that business impersonation scams are among the most common frauds reported to the agency, and Amazon is one of the most frequently impersonated companies. You may get a call, a text, or an email claiming to be from Amazon, saying there's a suspicious charge, a problem with your account, or an order you didn't place. The caller may ask you to press a number, install software on your computer, read back a code, or buy gift cards to "fix" the problem. Amazon has said publicly that it will never ask you to pay with gift cards over the phone, and it will never ask you to install software on your computer to fix an order. If you get one of these contacts, hang up or don't reply, and go directly to the Amazon app or type Amazon's web address yourself to check your orders. Never use a phone number or link someone sent you.

Let's go back to sellers for a moment, because there's a deeper story here about why the marketplace looks the way it does. Why would Amazon allow so many third-party sellers onto its store in the first place, competing with Amazon's own retail business? The short answer is that third-party sellers are extremely profitable for Amazon.

When a third-party seller makes a sale, Amazon generally collects a referral fee, which for many product categories is around fifteen percent of the sale price. If the seller uses Fulfillment by Amazon, the seller also pays fulfillment fees for picking, packing, and shipping, plus storage fees for keeping inventory in Amazon's warehouses. Then there's advertising. To get noticed in Amazon's crowded search results, many sellers pay for sponsored listings, and Amazon's advertising business now brings in tens of billions of dollars a year. Amazon's financial reports show that seller services are one of its largest revenue categories.

Add those pieces up and they can take a large bite out of what a seller earns. In fact, in September 2023, the Federal Trade Commission, joined by roughly seventeen states, filed a major antitrust lawsuit against Amazon. Among other things, the complaint alleges that when you add up the fees and costs Amazon effectively requires, Amazon takes close to half of the revenue of many sellers. The complaint also alleges that Amazon punishes sellers who offer lower prices on other websites, for example by making their products harder to find or less likely to win the prominent "Buy Box." Amazon strongly disputes these claims and says its policies protect customers and that sellers choose Amazon because it works for them. Those are allegations, not findings, and the case is scheduled to go to trial in February 2027. But it's worth knowing, because those costs don't simply disappear. In a competitive market, at least some of what sellers pay to Amazon may show up in the prices you pay.

There's another wrinkle that matters to you as a shopper. On many popular products, several sellers offer the exact same item. Amazon picks one of them to be the default seller shown in that main box with the "Add to Cart" and "Buy Now" buttons. This is called the Buy Box, sometimes referred to as the featured offer. When you click "Buy Now" without looking, you're buying from whichever seller currently holds that spot. It might be Amazon. It might be someone else entirely. And it can change from hour to hour. That's another reason to glance at the "Sold by" line every single time, even on a product you've bought before. The seller you bought from last month may not be the seller you're buying from today.

If you want to see who else is selling the same item, look for a link that says something like "Other sellers on Amazon," or "New and used from" a price, or a "Compare other offers" option. Clicking it shows you a list of every seller offering that item, their prices, their shipping times, and their ratings. Sometimes you'll find a slightly higher price from a far more trustworthy seller, or a lower price from a perfectly good one. Either way, you get to choose instead of letting the algorithm choose for you.

Let's also clear up a few common myths.

Myth number one: "If it has the Prime badge, it's sold by Amazon." Not true. The Prime badge mostly tells you about shipping. A product fulfilled by Amazon on behalf of a third-party seller can carry the Prime badge. Some sellers who ship themselves also qualify under a program for sellers who meet Amazon's speed standards. Prime tells you how fast it might arrive. It doesn't tell you who's selling it.

Myth number two: "Amazon's Choice means Amazon checked it." The "Amazon's Choice" label is generally assigned by Amazon's systems based on factors like price, popularity, ratings, and shipping availability. It's a convenience label, not a quality inspection or an endorsement that a product is safe or authentic.

So now let's pull everything together into the practical checklist I promised, especially for shoppers fifty-five and older who want to keep using the convenience of Amazon without getting burned. I'd suggest writing this down or keeping it next to your computer.

Number one. Before you buy, find the "Ships from" and "Sold by" line. Every time. Even on things you've bought before.

Number two. For health products, supplements, medications, baby items, electrical items like chargers and batteries, and anything you'll put on your skin, strongly prefer "Sold by Amazon" or the brand's official store. Counterfeits in these categories can be more than an inconvenience.

Number three. If it's a third-party seller, click the seller's name. Check the feedback score, the number of ratings, and how long the seller has been around. Read a few of the most recent negative comments.

Number four. If a price looks too good to be true on a name brand, assume it might be. Compare it with other sellers using the "other sellers" link, and with the brand's own website or a trusted local store.

Number five. Read the recent written reviews, not just the star average. Look for mentions of "fake," "used," "not as described," or "different from the picture."

Number six. Always pay through Amazon's checkout. Never pay a seller by gift card, wire transfer, cryptocurrency, or a payment app outside of Amazon. If anyone asks you to, stop.

Number seven. Keep all communication with sellers inside your Amazon account messages, so there's a record if you need to file a claim.

Number eight. When an item arrives, inspect it right away. Look at the packaging, labels, spelling, and seals. If something looks off, start a return or contact the seller promptly. Time limits matter.

Number nine. If the seller doesn't fix a legitimate problem, use the A-to-z Guarantee from your order page. Check the current rules on Amazon's help pages, and don't wait too long.

Number ten. Treat any unexpected call, text, or email "from Amazon" with suspicion. Don't click links or call numbers someone sends you. Open the Amazon app yourself or type the address directly to check your account. And remember: no legitimate company needs you to buy gift cards to fix a problem.

Let's step back and look at the bigger picture. Amazon has become, for many Americans, the default place to shop. It's fast, it's convenient, and for a lot of products, it really does offer a good deal. Nothing in this video is meant to tell you to stop shopping there. The point is that the store you see is not quite the store you think you're in. Behind that familiar logo and that smiling arrow is a vast marketplace of millions of independent businesses, from family shops in small-town America to large factories on the other side of the world. Amazon sets the rules, collects the fees, and decides which seller you see first. You, the shopper, are the last line of defense.

The good news is that the tools to protect yourself are already right there on the page. The "Ships from" and "Sold by" line. The seller profile. The feedback history. The "other sellers" link. The A-to-z Guarantee. Most shoppers simply never use them. Now you know they exist, and you know exactly how to use them.
