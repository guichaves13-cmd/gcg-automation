Millions of Americans over fifty-five take at least one prescription every day, and many are paying far more than they need to. Amazon is pushing its five-dollar-a-month prescription plan hard. But pharmacists and consumer advocates keep pointing to several other options that can sometimes cost less, and some of them do not require any membership at all. Stay with us, because by the end of this report, you will know exactly which questions to ask at your next refill.

Let's begin with a simple fact. Prescription drug prices in the United States vary wildly. The same generic medication, in the same dose, can cost a few dollars at one pharmacy and many times that at another pharmacy just down the road. Your insurance copay, the discount card in your wallet, the pharmacy you choose, and even whether you order a thirty-day or ninety-day supply can all change what you pay. That is why pharmacists often say the single most important thing you can do is compare before you fill.

Before we go any further, an important note. Nothing in this report is medical advice. Never stop, switch, or change a medication without talking to your doctor or your pharmacist first. Some medicines should not be switched between manufacturers without guidance, and some people have reasons, such as allergies to certain inactive ingredients, that make a specific version important. The goal here is to help you have a better conversation with the professionals who know your health history. And to be clear, this channel is not affiliated with Amazon or with any pharmacy or company we mention.

With that said, let's look fairly at what Amazon is offering.

Amazon Pharmacy is an online pharmacy that ships prescriptions to your home. You can use it with many insurance plans, or pay without insurance. Amazon also offers a program called RxPass. As of this report, RxPass costs five dollars a month for Prime members. For that flat fee, you can get as many medications as you need from a specific list of generic drugs, shipped to your door at no extra cost. The list covers a range of common generics used for conditions such as high blood pressure, high cholesterol, acid reflux, anxiety, and others. The exact list can change, so check Amazon's website for the current drugs covered.

There are a few important points of fine print. First, you need to be a Prime member, which, as we have covered on this channel, costs fourteen ninety-nine a month or one hundred and thirty-nine dollars a year at current prices. So if you are not already a Prime member, the true cost of RxPass is not just five dollars a month. Second, RxPass covers only drugs on its list. If your medication is not on the list, RxPass does not help with that one. Third, for a long time, people on government insurance like Medicare could not use RxPass. In 2024, Amazon expanded RxPass to include people with Medicare, but availability depends on where you live, and not every state is included. So if you are on Medicare, check eligibility in your state on Amazon's website before you count on it.

Now, who does RxPass make sense for? Pharmacists say it can be a genuinely good deal for someone who is already a Prime member and who takes several medications that are all on the RxPass list. If you take three or four covered generics every month, a flat five dollars for all of them may be hard to beat, and home delivery can be a real convenience if driving to the pharmacy is difficult.

But if you take only one inexpensive generic, or if your medications are not on the list, or if you are not already paying for Prime, the math may look very different. And that is where the alternatives come in.

The first alternative pharmacists and consumer advocates often mention is the Mark Cuban Cost Plus Drug Company, usually just called Cost Plus Drugs. It is an online pharmacy that launched in 2022 with a simple pricing model. As the company describes it, it charges what it pays the manufacturer, plus a fixed markup, plus a pharmacy fee for dispensing, plus shipping. The company publishes its prices on its website, so you can look up a drug and see exactly what you would pay before you order. There is no membership fee to use it.

For many common generics, Cost Plus Drugs prices can be very low, sometimes just a few dollars for a month's supply before shipping. For some medications, the savings compared with what people were paying at a retail counter have been dramatic. Cost Plus Drugs works with a growing number of insurance plans and has partnered with some independent pharmacies, but in many cases, people use it by paying cash, without going through insurance at all.

Here is something important to understand about paying cash. If you have Medicare Part D or other insurance, and you pay out of pocket at a pharmacy without using your insurance, that purchase may not count toward your plan's deductible or your yearly out-of-pocket limit. For people who take inexpensive generics, that may not matter much. But for people with expensive medications, it can matter a great deal. We will come back to this when we talk about the Medicare cap, because it is one of the most important points in this whole report.

Your doctor will need to send your prescription to Cost Plus Drugs, just as they would to any other pharmacy. Many doctors' offices are familiar with it now, but some may not be, so you may need to ask. And since it is mail order, you need to plan ahead. It is not the right choice for a medication you need to start today.

Now let's talk about GoodRx. GoodRx is not a pharmacy. It is a free website and app that shows you discount prices for medications at pharmacies near you, along with free coupons you can show at the counter. You type in your drug name and dose, enter your zip code, and GoodRx lists prices at nearby pharmacies, such as major chains, grocery store pharmacies, and big-box stores. Using the basic coupons does not require a paid membership. GoodRx also offers a paid subscription with deeper discounts at some pharmacies, but you do not need it to use the free coupons.

Pharmacists often say GoodRx is a good starting point simply because it shows you how much prices vary. You might discover that your generic costs a fraction as much at the grocery store pharmacy across town. You might also find that the GoodRx coupon price is lower than your insurance copay. Yes, that happens more often than many people realize, especially for older, inexpensive generics.

But the same rule applies here. When you use a discount coupon instead of your insurance, that purchase generally does not count toward your insurance deductible or out-of-pocket maximum. For some people, that trade-off is worth it. For others, it is not. That is a question to ask your pharmacist directly. Pharmacists can often run a price both ways, with your insurance and with a discount card, and tell you which one is lower. Many will do it if you simply ask.

And here is the point we want you to remember, so stay with us. Coming up, we are going to talk about a change to Medicare drug coverage that could matter more to your budget than any discount card or membership, and that many seniors still have not heard about. But first, let's talk about a pharmacy that many people do not realize they can use.

Costco has pharmacies in many of its warehouses, and they are known among consumer advocates for competitive prices on many generics. Here is the part most people do not know. Under federal and state rules governing pharmacies, Costco generally allows non-members to use its pharmacy. You do not need a Costco membership to fill a prescription there, though you may need to tell the staff at the door that you are going to the pharmacy. Costco members can get additional discounts through Costco's own member prescription program, and Costco accepts many insurance plans. Policies can vary by state and location, so call your local Costco pharmacy first and ask.

Sam's Club pharmacies also serve customers, and Sam's has offered discounts for members on many generics. Again, check with your local store about current rules for non-members. Other big-box and grocery store pharmacies have, at various times, offered low-price generic lists, often for a set price for a thirty-day or ninety-day supply. Those programs change frequently, so ask at the counter what they currently offer.

Now let's get to the big one. The Medicare Part D change.

For years, people on Medicare Part D could face very high out-of-pocket drug costs, with no firm limit on how much they might pay in a single year if they took expensive medications. That changed under the Inflation Reduction Act. Starting in 2025, Medicare Part D has a cap on out-of-pocket drug costs. In 2025, that cap is two thousand dollars a year. In 2026, it rises to two thousand one hundred dollars, because it is adjusted over time. Once you reach that amount in out-of-pocket costs on covered drugs, you generally pay nothing more for covered Part D drugs for the rest of that calendar year.

This is a major change, and pharmacists say it should be part of every Medicare beneficiary's thinking. Remember what we said about paying cash or using a discount card outside your insurance? If you take expensive brand-name medications and you are likely to hit that cap, paying out of pocket outside your plan may slow down your progress toward it. In that situation, using your Part D coverage may protect you better in the long run. On the other hand, if you take only a few inexpensive generics and you will never get anywhere near the cap, a cash price from Cost Plus Drugs, GoodRx, or Costco might be lower than your copay.

There is another related change worth knowing. Medicare now offers a program called the Medicare Prescription Payment Plan, which lets people with Part D spread their out-of-pocket drug costs into monthly payments over the year, instead of paying a large amount all at once at the pharmacy counter. It does not lower your total cost, but it can make large costs easier to manage. You can ask your Part D plan about it.

And if you have limited income and resources, you may qualify for a federal program called Extra Help, also known as the Low-Income Subsidy, which can greatly lower Part D premiums and drug costs. Many people who qualify never apply. You can learn more through Social Security or Medicare, or through your State Health Insurance Assistance Program, often called SHIP, which offers free, unbiased Medicare counseling in every state.

Speaking of which, every fall, Medicare's open enrollment period runs from October fifteenth to December seventh. That is the time when you can switch Part D plans or Medicare Advantage plans for the coming year. Drug plans change their lists of covered drugs, called formularies, and their prices every year. A plan that was a good fit this year might not be next year. Medicare's official plan finder tool at medicare dot gov lets you enter your medications and compare plans in your area. Counselors at your local SHIP office can walk you through it for free. Pharmacists say reviewing your plan every single year is one of the most effective ways to avoid overpaying.

Two more pieces of the Medicare picture are worth knowing. First, under the same law, people with Medicare drug coverage pay no more than thirty-five dollars for a month's supply of each covered insulin product, and there is no deductible for covered insulin. If you or a loved one uses insulin and is paying more than that under a Medicare plan, call the plan and ask why. Second, recommended adult vaccines covered under Part D, such as the shingles vaccine, are now available at no cost to people with Part D coverage. Many seniors used to put off the shingles shot because of the price. Ask your pharmacist whether that still applies to your plan.

There is one more category of help that pharmacists say gets overlooked: patient assistance programs. Many drug manufacturers run programs that provide brand-name medications at low or no cost to people who meet certain income limits. Rules vary widely, and some programs have restrictions for people on Medicare, but they exist for many expensive drugs. Your doctor's office, your pharmacist, or a hospital social worker can often help you find out whether a program exists for your medication. Nonprofit foundations also offer help with copays for certain conditions, though funding comes and goes during the year.

Let's walk through a simple example to see how all of this fits together. This is a made-up scenario, not anyone's real situation, and real prices will differ. Imagine a retiree on Medicare who takes two common generics, one for blood pressure and one for cholesterol, plus one brand-name medication that is expensive. For the two generics, she might compare her Part D copay with the cash price at Cost Plus Drugs, with a GoodRx coupon at her local grocery pharmacy, and, if she is a Prime member in a state where Medicare members can use it, with RxPass. Any of those might come out lowest, and the only way to know is to check. For the expensive brand-name drug, though, she would likely want to keep using her Part D coverage, because those costs count toward the yearly out-of-pocket cap, and once she reaches it, she generally pays nothing more for covered drugs that year. That is the kind of mixed approach pharmacists often describe, and it is exactly why the conversation with your pharmacist matters so much.

Now let's bring all of this together into a practical game plan, the way a careful pharmacist might lay it out.

Step one. Make a complete list of every medication you take, with the name, the dose, and how often you take it. Include anything prescribed by any doctor. This list is useful for your safety, too, because it helps your pharmacist and doctors spot interactions.

Step two. For each medication, find out what you are paying now, including your copay and any premium you pay for your plan.

Step three. Check whether each medication is on the RxPass list, if you are a Prime member, or would consider becoming one. Remember, if you are on Medicare, check eligibility in your state.

Step four. Look up each medication on the Cost Plus Drugs website and on GoodRx. Write down the prices you find, including shipping where it applies.

Step five. Call your local Costco pharmacy, or other pharmacies near you, and ask their cash price. Many will tell you over the phone.

Step six. Take your list to your pharmacist. Ask a simple question: given my insurance and my medications, what is the lowest-cost way for me to fill each of these, and does paying cash for any of them affect my deductible or my out-of-pocket cap? A good pharmacist will appreciate the question.

Step seven. Ask your doctor whether a generic version or a lower-cost alternative exists for any brand-name drug you take. Sometimes there is a generic that works the same way. Sometimes there is not, and your doctor may have good reasons for prescribing what they did. But it is always fair to ask.

Step eight. Ask about ninety-day supplies. For long-term medications, many plans and pharmacies offer lower costs per dose with a three-month supply, and you make fewer trips. Mail-order pharmacies, including your insurance plan's own mail-order service, often specialize in this.

You may find that you end up using more than one option. That is perfectly normal. Some people fill one medication through their Part D plan, another through Cost Plus Drugs, and a third with a discount coupon at the local grocery store. The key is that everyone treating you knows everything you take. Pharmacists stress this point: if you use more than one pharmacy, make sure each pharmacist has your complete medication list, because no single pharmacy's computer can catch interactions with drugs it does not know about.

Let's also talk about safety and scams, because they are a real problem in this area. Only use pharmacies that are licensed in the United States and require a valid prescription. Be very cautious of websites that offer prescription drugs without a prescription, or at prices that seem too good to be true, or that are based overseas. The Food and Drug Administration warns that some of these sites sell counterfeit or contaminated medicines. The National Association of Boards of Pharmacy offers tools to help consumers check whether an online pharmacy is legitimate. And be on guard for phone calls, emails, or text messages claiming to be from Medicare, from your drug plan, or from a pharmacy, asking for your Medicare number or bank information. Medicare generally will not call you out of the blue asking for personal information. If in doubt, hang up and call the number on the back of your card.

Now, a fair word about convenience. One of the real advantages of RxPass, Amazon Pharmacy, and other mail-order services is that you do not have to drive anywhere. For people who no longer drive, live far from a pharmacy, or care for a spouse at home, delivery can be worth paying a little more for. Many local pharmacies also deliver, sometimes for free or for a small fee, so it is worth asking yours. And on the other side, a local pharmacist you know by name, who can answer questions face to face and catch problems early, is something many people value deeply. Price matters, but so does that relationship.

And one practical tip for anyone using mail order of any kind. Set up refill reminders, and order early. Packages can be delayed by weather, holidays, or address problems, and running out of a daily medication is not something you want to risk. Pharmacists commonly suggest reordering when you still have a week or two of medication left. If a delivery is ever late and you are about to run out, call your doctor or a local pharmacist right away. In many cases, they can help you get a short emergency supply to bridge the gap. And keep medications delivered to your porch out of extreme heat or cold whenever possible, since some medicines are sensitive to temperature.

So let's recap. Amazon's RxPass costs five dollars a month for Prime members and covers a specific list of generic drugs, with Medicare eligibility expanded in 2024 but not available everywhere, so check eligibility in your state. It can be an excellent deal if you are already a Prime member and take several covered generics. Mark Cuban Cost Plus Drugs publishes transparent prices with no membership fee, and is often very low for generics. GoodRx is a free tool for comparing pharmacy prices and finding coupons. Costco's pharmacy is generally open to non-members, though you should call ahead. And Medicare Part D now has an out-of-pocket cap, two thousand dollars in 2025 and two thousand one hundred dollars in 2026, which changes the math for anyone on expensive medications. Prices and programs change, so always check current details.

And most important of all, talk to your pharmacist and your doctor before making any change. They are your best allies in getting the right medication at the right price.
