Every price tag at Costco is sending you a message, and most shoppers never learn to read it. The last two digits, a tiny asterisk in the corner, even the date you bought something can decide whether you overpay or quietly get money back. So who really sets these prices, and what rules are they following? Today we investigate the buyers, the markup limits reported by company executives, the hidden price codes, and one policy that could put cash back in your pocket.

Let's start with a simple question that turns out to have a surprisingly interesting answer. When you walk into a Costco warehouse and see a price on a pallet of paper towels, a television, or a bag of coffee, who decided that number?

At most traditional retailers, prices are shaped by a mix of things: what the supplier charges, what competitors are charging, how much the store wants to earn on each item, and sometimes what the store believes shoppers will tolerate. Costco has a different reputation. For decades, the company has been known for a pricing philosophy built around keeping margins low on the products themselves, and earning a large share of its profit somewhere else. We will come back to where that somewhere else is, because it is the key to the whole story.

The people at the center of Costco pricing are its buyers. Costco employs teams of merchandise buyers who are responsible for specific categories, such as food, household goods, electronics, or apparel. These buyers negotiate with suppliers, decide which items make it onto the warehouse floor, and push for the lowest possible cost. And here is something important to understand: Costco carries far fewer different products than a typical supermarket or big-box store. A conventional supermarket can carry tens of thousands of different items. Costco is widely reported to carry only around four thousand or so at a time in a typical warehouse. That smaller selection is intentional.

Why does that matter for prices? Because when Costco picks only one or two versions of a product, say one or two brands of ketchup instead of fifteen, the supplier who wins that spot gets enormous volume. Millions of members across hundreds of warehouses will be buying that one item. That gives Costco buyers tremendous bargaining power. A supplier knows that if it does not offer an excellent price, a competitor might get the spot instead, or Costco might put a Kirkland Signature version there. That pressure is one of the reasons Costco prices on many items are hard to beat.

Now let's talk about the most famous rule in the Costco pricing legend: the markup cap. Over the years, Costco executives have described a policy of limiting how much the company marks up the items it sells. The figures commonly reported are a cap of about fourteen percent on branded products and about fifteen percent on Kirkland Signature products. To understand what that means, imagine Costco pays a supplier one hundred dollars for an item. Under that kind of cap, the price on the shelf would be no more than roughly one hundred fourteen dollars for a branded product.

Compare that to the broader retail world, where markups on certain categories can be much, much higher. Clothing, jewelry, and some packaged goods at traditional stores can carry very large markups, which is part of how those stores pay for advertising, fancy displays, and frequent sales events.

There is a well-known story that has been shared by people familiar with Costco's history, about the company's founding leadership being so committed to low markups that buyers were discouraged from raising prices even when they could. You may have heard versions of that story. What we can say with confidence is that company executives have described this low-markup approach publicly over many years, and it remains one of the defining features of the Costco model.

But here is the part that confuses many shoppers. If Costco caps its markups so tightly, how does it make money at all? Running warehouses costs a fortune. There are employees, utilities, trucks, property, and much more.

The answer is the membership fee. And this is the surprise in today's title. In a real sense, the person who helps set the price you pay at Costco is you, the member, because your annual fee is what allows those low prices to exist. Membership fees account for the majority of Costco's operating profit. Think about that. The company earns relatively little on the products themselves, and the membership card is what makes the business work. Since September 2024, the basic Gold Star membership in the United States costs sixty-five dollars a year, and the Executive membership costs one hundred thirty dollars a year.

So when you pay that fee, you are essentially paying in advance for access to prices that are kept close to cost. Costco has a strong reason to keep those prices low, because if members stop feeling like they are getting a great deal, they stop renewing, and the core of the profit disappears. That is a powerful incentive, and it is very different from a store that makes its money by maximizing what it earns on each individual sale.

Now let's get to the part many of you have been waiting for: the price codes. If you have ever looked closely at Costco price tags, you may have noticed that the endings are not random. Over the years, shoppers, former employees, and consumer writers have widely reported a set of patterns in how Costco prices end. Costco does not advertise these as an official code to members, so think of them as well-documented patterns rather than a guarantee. But they are among the most widely shared shopping tips in America, and they can be genuinely useful.

Let's start with prices ending in point ninety-nine. A price ending in ninety-nine cents is widely reported to be a regular, full price item. For example, a product priced at fourteen ninety-nine. That is the standard price. Nothing special, nothing wrong with it either, but it is not a markdown.

Next, prices ending in point ninety-seven. This one is the favorite of bargain hunters. A price ending in ninety-seven cents is widely reported to signal a clearance or markdown price set at the warehouse level. So if you see something at eleven ninety-seven instead of fourteen ninety-nine, that is often a sign the item has been reduced, possibly because the warehouse wants to move it out. For shoppers, a ninety-seven ending is a signal to take a closer look.

Then there are prices ending in point zero zero, or sometimes point eighty-eight. These are widely reported to indicate a manager's special, a price set locally, sometimes for a returned item, a floor model, a discontinued item, or a product the warehouse wants to clear quickly. These can be some of the best deals in the store, but they also tend to be one-of-a-kind or limited quantity items, so inspect the product carefully before you buy.

And finally, the asterisk. If you see a small asterisk in the upper right corner of a Costco price sign, it is widely reported to mean that the item is not being reordered, at least not at that warehouse for now. Some shoppers call it the death star. It means that once the current stock is gone, it may not come back. If it is a product you love and use regularly, that asterisk is your cue to consider buying an extra one, or to ask a staff member if the item is truly being discontinued. Do not panic buy, but do pay attention.

Stay with me, because the next piece of this investigation may matter more for your wallet than any price code on any tag. It is a policy many members have never even heard of, and it can mean money back after you have already paid.

Costco has had a price adjustment policy that has been widely reported and described by the company. Here is how it has generally worked. If you buy an item and the price at Costco goes down within thirty days of your purchase, you may be able to request a price adjustment for the difference. In other words, if you bought a vacuum cleaner for two hundred dollars and two weeks later Costco puts it on sale for one hundred sixty dollars, you may be able to go to the membership or returns counter, show your receipt or have them look it up through your membership, and get the forty-dollar difference back.

Now, there are important limits, and we want to be careful and accurate here. The policy has applied to price reductions at Costco itself, not to a lower price at a competing store. It is generally tied to a window of about thirty days from the purchase date. And there have been exceptions, for example on certain items, or for price changes that occur because of specific events. Policies can also change, so the honest advice is this: always check the current rules at your warehouse's membership counter, or on Costco's official website, before you assume you qualify. But for members who keep their receipts and pay attention to prices, this policy has been a real and very practical way to save.

Here is a simple habit that works well for many retirees. When you buy a larger item, something over fifty or a hundred dollars, write the date and the price on a small calendar or note on your phone. Then, when you shop again over the next few weeks, glance at the price of that same item. If it has dropped, you know to ask. Some members also check Costco's online listings and the monthly coupon book, sometimes called the savings book, which is mailed or posted with upcoming instant savings. If a product you just bought appears in an upcoming sale, that is your signal.

Now let's look at another weapon in Costco's pricing strategy: loss leaders. A loss leader is a product a store sells at a very low price, sometimes even at a loss, to bring shoppers through the door. The idea is that once you are inside, you will buy other things. Costco has two of the most famous loss leaders in American retail.

The first is the hot dog and soda combo at the food court. That combo has been priced at one dollar and fifty cents since 1985. Let that sink in. Gasoline, groceries, rent, and almost everything else has risen dramatically since the mid-1980s, but the Costco hot dog combo price has not changed. Company leaders have described the price as something they are determined to protect. It has become a symbol of the whole Costco promise, and it is the kind of thing people talk about with their friends. That alone makes it valuable as marketing, even if the company earns little or nothing on each one.

The second famous loss leader is the rotisserie chicken, sold for four dollars and ninety-nine cents. Industry observers have widely described it as a loss leader, priced to bring members in and often placed toward the back of the store, so that shoppers walk past many other products on their way to grab one. Costco went so far as to build its own poultry operation in Nebraska, known as Lincoln Premium Poultry, to help supply chickens and control costs. That is a remarkable investment just to keep the price of one item steady. It tells you how seriously the company takes these symbolic prices.

Here is the shopper's lesson from the loss leaders. They are genuinely good deals, and there is nothing wrong with buying them. But the strategy works because people buy more than they planned once they are in the store. If you go to Costco for a four ninety-nine chicken and walk out with three hundred dollars of things you did not need, the loss leader did its job, and not necessarily for you. A written list is one of the best defenses a shopper has.

Now let's talk about one more factor in who sets prices: the warehouse itself. While core pricing is largely set centrally, individual warehouses have some flexibility for local markdowns, clearance items, and the manager specials we discussed. That is why you may see a different price on the same item at two different locations, especially on clearance goods. It is also why some dedicated bargain hunters like to visit more than one warehouse if they live near several.

And there is another influence that is easy to forget: the supplier and the market. When costs rise for raw materials, shipping, or labor, those increases can work their way into prices even at Costco. The markup cap limits how much Costco adds on top of its cost, but it does not freeze the cost itself. So if coffee beans, eggs, or beef become more expensive across the country, you will likely see that at Costco too. The difference is that Costco is reported to add a smaller margin on top than many stores do.

We are an independent channel looking at publicly reported information. The price code patterns we described are widely shared by shoppers and former employees, but Costco does not officially promise that every tag follows them. Use them as clues, not guarantees.

Let's also clear up a few common misunderstandings about Costco prices, because some of them can cost you money.

The first misunderstanding is that everything at Costco is automatically the cheapest price in town. That is not always true. On many staples, Costco is very hard to beat, especially when you compare the unit price. But a supermarket running a weekly sale, a pharmacy with a coupon, or a discount store with a seasonal promotion can sometimes come in lower on a particular item. The markup cap describes how much Costco adds on top of its own cost. It does not promise that no other store will ever undercut it. Smart shoppers keep a rough mental list of the prices they pay most often, so they can tell a true bargain when they see one.

The second misunderstanding is that bigger always means cheaper. Usually, buying a larger package lowers the cost per unit. But not always, and not if you throw part of it away. A three-pound tub of something that goes bad after you have used half of it has effectively doubled in price. For couples or people living alone, this is one of the most important things to keep in mind. Buying with a friend or a neighbor, and splitting the package, is a simple way to capture the lower unit price without the waste.

The third misunderstanding is about online prices. Costco sells many items on its website as well as in its warehouses, and the prices are not always the same. Online prices can include shipping and handling built in, and some items are available only online or only in stores. If you are comparing, make sure you are comparing the same item in the same channel. Many members find that the warehouse price on everyday groceries is lower than the delivered price, while some large items like appliances or furniture are only practical to buy online.

The fourth misunderstanding is that a price ending in ninety-seven means the item is defective or bad. Usually, a markdown simply means the warehouse wants to clear space, the item is seasonal, the packaging changed, or a newer version is arriving. That is often good news for you. Of course, for manager specials on returned or open-box items, it is wise to inspect carefully, ask whether all the parts are included, and check what the current return policy says for that type of product.

And there is one more pricing tool worth mentioning: the instant savings that appear in Costco's member savings book. These are manufacturer-funded discounts in many cases, meaning the supplier helps pay for the reduced price for a limited time. When you see an item in that book, the discount is usually applied automatically at the register during the promotion period. For a member who was going to buy that item anyway, timing a purchase to match the savings book is one of the easiest ways to save. And remember the price adjustment window. If you bought something shortly before it went into the savings book at a lower price, that is exactly the situation where it may be worth asking at the membership counter, within the current policy rules.

So let's turn all of this into a simple checklist you can take on your next trip.

Number one: look at the unit price, not just the total price. The shelf tags usually break this down. It is the single best way to compare a giant package to a smaller one somewhere else.

Number two: read the ending. A ninety-nine is regular. A ninety-seven is widely reported to be a markdown. A double zero or eighty-eight is widely reported to be a manager special. These are clues that can help you spot deals faster.

Number three: check the corner for an asterisk. If an item you rely on has an asterisk, it may not be coming back. Plan accordingly.

Number four: keep your receipts and watch prices for about thirty days on larger purchases. If the price drops at Costco, ask about a price adjustment under the current policy.

Number five: enjoy the loss leaders, but shop with a list. The hot dog and the chicken are real bargains. The impulse purchases on the way to them are where budgets can get into trouble.

Number six: understand that your membership fee is part of the price. If you only go a few times a year and spend very little, the low prices may not make up for the fee. If you shop regularly, the fee is often what makes the savings possible.

Before we wrap up, let's think about why this matters especially for older shoppers and people on fixed incomes. Many retirees live on Social Security, a pension, savings, or some combination. When prices rise, there is often no raise at work to make up for it. That makes understanding how a store sets prices more than a fun bit of trivia. It is a practical skill. Knowing when an item is marked down, when it may disappear, and when you can request money back can easily add up to real savings over a year.

There is also a broader lesson. Every store has a pricing strategy. Some rely on frequent sales and coupons. Some rely on loyalty programs. Some rely on high markups and big advertising. Costco's strategy is built on limited selection, tough negotiation by buyers, reported markup caps, a handful of famous loss leaders, and, above all, membership fees that bring in the majority of its operating profit. Once you understand the strategy, you can work with it instead of against it.

So who really sets the prices at Costco? It is the buyers who negotiate hard with suppliers. It is a long-standing markup philosophy that company executives have described for years. It is local warehouse managers who mark down clearance items. It is the cost of goods in the wider economy. And, perhaps most surprising of all, it is you, the member, because your annual fee is the engine that makes those low prices possible.
