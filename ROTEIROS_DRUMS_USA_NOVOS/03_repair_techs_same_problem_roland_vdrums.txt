They are a little computer, a bunch of sensors, a pile of cables, and some rubber and mesh that get beaten with sticks every single day. Things wear out. And the things that wear out tend to be the same things, kit after kit.

Let's start with the one I see the most, because it is also the one people notice last.

Problem number one is worn mesh heads.

Mesh heads are one of the big reasons people love V-Drums. They feel closer to a real drum than the old rubber pads, they are quieter, and you can tune the tension to get the rebound you like. Roland's own documentation talks about adjusting head tension and about the heads being replaceable parts. So the idea that a mesh head is a consumable item is not a forum theory. It is built into how the product is designed.

What I see on the bench is that most drummers do not think of a mesh head as something that wears out at all. They think of it like the shell of the drum, something that lasts forever. And it does not. Over time, especially on the snare, the mesh in the main playing zone gets stretched, loses some tension, and sometimes develops a slight dent or a fuzzy patch. In more extreme cases, the weave can actually tear.

Now here is the part that matters. When the head wears, the way it transfers your hit down to the sensor underneath changes. Most Roland mesh pads use a sensor positioned under the head, often with a foam cone or cushion between the mesh and the sensor. If the head has gone soft in one spot, your strikes in that spot may feel different and may trigger differently. Players describe it as the snare suddenly feeling dead, or the dynamics feeling inconsistent, or ghost notes disappearing. A lot of people blame the module first. In my experience, it is very often the head, the cushion under the head, or the tension.

The fix here is usually cheap compared to anything else on this list. A replacement mesh head is generally somewhere in the range of a few tens of dollars depending on the size, and some players use third party heads too. Just know that if you swap to a head that is not designed for that pad, the feel and the triggering can change, so you may need to adjust the module settings afterward.

Problem number two is crosstalk.

If you have ever hit your floor tom and heard a little snare hit come out of the module at the same time, that is crosstalk. Or you hit a crash and the ride makes a tiny noise. It is one of the most common complaints I hear about any electronic kit, not just Roland.

Here is what is confirmed. Roland modules have crosstalk cancellation settings. That is in the manuals. The very fact that the setting exists tells you the company knows vibration can travel through the rack and make one pad's sensor pick up a hit meant for another pad. So crosstalk is not a defect in the sense of something broken. It is a known physical behavior that the module is designed to manage.

What I see in real life is that crosstalk gets worse over time, and the reasons are usually mechanical. A rack clamp loosens a little. A tom arm gets moved during a gig and ends up touching another pad's mount. A cymbal stops being isolated properly. The kit gets set up on a different floor that bounces more. The rubber parts that isolate the pads from the rack get older and harder. And suddenly a kit that was perfectly clean when it was new starts throwing ghost notes all over the place.

The bad move here is to just crank the crosstalk cancellation as high as it will go. That can work, but it can also make your quiet notes disappear, because now the module is ignoring hits that are too soft. Many techs I respect, and plenty of experienced players on forums, will tell you the same thing. Fix the hardware first, then use the software to clean up what is left.

So when a kit comes in with crosstalk, I tighten every clamp, I check that no pad or arm is touching anything it should not, I look at the cymbal isolation, and only then do I go into the module and adjust the trigger settings one pad at a time. Very often, the kit goes back to normal without spending a single dollar on parts.

Problem number three is hi-hat controller calibration.

If there is one thing that makes drummers angry at electronic kits, it is the hi-hat. On an acoustic kit you get this huge range of sounds between fully open and fully closed, and the whole thing just works. On an electronic kit, the module has to figure out how far your foot has pressed the pedal, and turn that into the right sound.

Roland's hi-hat systems, depending on the model, use different approaches. Some use a controller that sits on a real hi-hat stand. Some use a standalone pedal. Some higher end models use digital hi-hat systems. And Roland's manuals describe a calibration or offset procedure for the hi-hat. Again, the fact that the procedure is in the manual tells you this is something the company expects you to do, not just once at setup, but whenever the hi-hat starts behaving strangely.

The symptoms techs see over and over are pretty consistent. The hi-hat does not fully close, so you get a sloppy half-open sound when you want a tight chick. Or the opposite, it reads as closed when you have your foot barely resting on the pedal. Or the splash and foot chick sounds stop triggering the way they used to. Or the hi-hat drifts during a gig, so it feels fine at sound check and weird by the third song.

Here is what is commonly reported, and I will flag it as reported. On setups that use the hi-hat controller on a regular stand, the clutch can slip, the height can change, or the felt and spacing can shift. Any of that changes the distance the module thinks the hi-hat is traveling, and the calibration goes off. On standalone pedal style controllers, players report the sensor inside can wear or get less consistent with time. I have personally seen both situations, but I cannot tell you which is more common, and I am not going to pretend I can.

What I do on the bench is simple. I check the physical setup first, the clutch, the height, the felt, the stand. Then I run the calibration procedure exactly the way the manual for that specific module says to. And then I play it, a lot, with my foot doing all the little things a real drummer does. If it still misbehaves after a proper calibration, that is when I start suspecting the controller itself, the cable, or the input jack.

Which is the perfect lead-in to problem number four.

Problem number four is trigger cables and input jacks.

This is the one that makes me shake my head the most, because it causes so much frustration and it is often the cheapest fix of all.

Every pad on a V-Drums kit is connected to the module by cable. On many Roland kits, the pads plug into a snake, a single connector or a multi-cable harness that runs into the module. On other setups, you have individual quarter inch cables going into individual jacks. Either way, you have a lot of connectors, a lot of bending, and a lot of chances for something to wear.

What techs see again and again is intermittent triggering. A pad works most of the time and then randomly drops out. Or a cymbal choke works one day and not the next. Or a pad that should have two zones only sends one zone. Or you get random crackling hits with nobody touching the kit. And when you wiggle the cable, the problem comes and goes.

The confirmed part here is just basic electronics, not something special about Roland. Cables and connectors that get bent, pulled, stepped on, packed up for gigs, and unpacked again, eventually wear. The internal wires can break right near the plug, where the cable flexes the most. The jack on the pad or on the module can get loose. And dual zone pads use stereo style connections, so if one conductor fails, you can lose just one zone and still have the other, which is extremely confusing if you do not know to look for it.

What is reported by a lot of techs and players is that the cables near the pads, especially on kits that get moved around for gigs, are the first to go. Also reported, and I have seen this myself, is that the jacks on the module itself can get worn on kits that have had a lot of cables plugged and unplugged over the years.

The way I diagnose it is boring but it works. Swap the cable. If the problem moves with the cable, it is the cable. If the problem stays with the pad, it is the pad or the pad's jack. If the problem stays with the input on the module no matter what you plug in, it is the module's input. That simple test saves people from buying a new module when they really just needed a cable that costs less than a pizza.

Problem number five is cracked or worn rubber.

Not every part of a V-Drums kit is mesh. Many Roland cymbal pads use a rubber playing surface. Some lower priced kits use rubber or rubber style surfaces on some pads. Kick pads have their own striking surface. And the rims on many pads have rubber covers too.

Roland's manuals generally talk about caring for the pads, keeping them away from heat and direct sunlight, and not using harsh cleaners. That kind of guidance makes sense for any rubber material, because rubber does age.

What techs and players report is that rubber on cymbal pads, especially on the edge where the choke and the edge zone live, can crack or split over years of hard playing. Rim covers can also wear through on the snare, especially for players who do a lot of rimshots and cross sticks. And kick pads can wear in the exact spot where the beater lands, which is why a lot of players put a patch or a protective pad there.

Now, I want to be careful here. I do not want to say every Roland cymbal cracks. Plenty of them do not, and plenty of kits from many years ago still look fine. But when I see one with a split edge, the story the owner tells me is almost always the same. Hard hitting, lots of gigs, often stored in a hot car or a garage that gets really warm in the summer. Heat and age together are not kind to rubber.

The important thing to know about cracked rubber is that it is not always just a cosmetic issue. If the crack is near a sensor or a switch, like the choke or the edge zone on a cymbal, it can change how that zone triggers. So if your choke stopped working and your cymbal edge is split, those two things might be related.

Problem number six is module firmware and settings.

This one is a little different, because it is not something wearing out. It is software.

Roland has released system updates for many of its modules over the years, and those updates are posted on Roland's own support pages. That is confirmed. The updates sometimes fix bugs, sometimes improve features, and sometimes add new sounds or functions, depending on the model. Roland also typically provides release notes explaining what changed.

What I see on the bench is that a lot of people have never updated their module at all. They bought the kit, set it up, and never looked back. And some of the quirks they complain about may have been addressed in a later update for that specific model. I am not saying every problem gets fixed by an update, far from it. But it is a free thing to check, and it is one of the first things I look at.

Players who use double pedals or play with a lot of force often report that the kick pad starts moving across the floor, or that the beater area wears, or that the pad starts double triggering. Some of that is about the beater, the pedal setup, and the surface the kit is sitting on. Roland's documentation covers setting up the kick pad and adjusting trigger settings. The double triggering issue in particular is very often solved by the beater adjustment and the retrigger settings in the module, rather than by replacing the pad. And a good rug or a drum mat helps a lot with movement. Not glamorous, but it works.

Okay. So now you know the six things techs keep seeing. Let me put all of this into a prevention checklist that you can actually use. I will go through it slowly so you can follow along.

One. Treat mesh heads as consumables. Check the main playing area on your snare and your most used toms every few months. If you see a soft spot, a dent, or fraying, plan on replacing the head. And check the tension, because heads lose tension over time.

Two. Check every rack clamp and every mount a few times a year, and every time you move the kit. Make sure no pad, arm, or cymbal is touching another one. This alone prevents a lot of crosstalk.

Three. Recalibrate your hi-hat whenever it starts feeling off, and every time you move it or change its height. Follow the exact procedure in your module's manual.

Four. Respect your cables. Do not yank them out by the cord. Leave a little slack so they do not pull tight at the connector. Use cable ties or Velcro wraps so they do not get stepped on. If you gig, consider keeping a spare cable or two in your bag. It is one of the cheapest ways to save a show.

Five. Keep your kit out of the heat. Do not leave it in a hot car. Do not store it right next to a heater or in a garage that bakes in the summer. Rubber and electronics both last longer in a normal room temperature environment.

Six. Clean pads the way Roland's manual says to, usually with a soft, dry, or slightly damp cloth, and avoid harsh solvents or chemicals that can damage the rubber or the mesh.

Seven. Check Roland's support page for your module once in a while and see if there is a system update. Read the release notes before you install anything.

Eight. Back up your settings before you change them, and write down any trigger adjustments you make. Future you will thank present you.

Nine. If you use your kit for gigs, check it before every show. Play every pad, every zone, the hi-hat at different positions, and the kick. It takes a couple of minutes and it can save you a lot of embarrassment on stage.

Ten. When something goes wrong, change one thing at a time. Swap one cable. Change one setting. Test. That way you actually know what fixed it.

Now let me give you my honest take on the big question. When should you repair a Roland kit, and when should you replace it?

Let me start with what is almost always worth fixing. Mesh heads, foam cushions, cables, hi-hat calibration, trigger settings, firmware, and rack hardware. These are all either cheap, free, or easy. If your kit has one of these problems, fixing it is almost always better than replacing the whole kit. And honestly, a lot of the kits I see that people think are dying are actually totally fine kits with a worn head, a bad cable, and bad settings.

Then there is the middle zone. A single pad that has failed, like a tom with a dead sensor, or a cymbal pad with a broken edge zone, or a hi-hat controller that will not calibrate no matter what. In many cases, you can replace just that pad or controller. Roland sells many individual pads, and there is also a used market for them. The price depends a lot on the model. A basic tom pad can be fairly affordable, while a high end snare or digital hi-hat can cost a lot more. My advice is to price out the part, compare it to what your whole kit is worth on the used market, and decide from there. If the part costs a small fraction of the kit, replace the part. If the part costs close to half of what the whole kit is worth, take a breath and think about it.

And then there is the module. The module is the brain, and it is usually the most expensive single component. If your module has a dead input, a broken screen, buttons that no longer respond, or it will not power on at all, that is when the repair versus replace math gets serious. Some modules can be repaired by authorized service centers, and that is always worth asking about, especially for higher end modules. But on older or entry level kits, the cost of repairing the module can sometimes be close to the price of a newer, better module, or even a whole new kit.

My personal rule of thumb is this. If the repair fixes the problem and the kit still does what you need it to do, repair it. If the repair costs a big chunk of what the kit is worth and the kit was already holding you back, that is when it might make sense to put that money toward something new. And if you are buying used, go through every single item on that checklist before you hand over any cash. Play every pad, every zone, open and close the hi-hat slowly, check the cables, and look closely at the mesh heads and the cymbal edges.

And one more thing. Do not let anybody, including me, convince you that a whole brand is unreliable because of a few horror stories in a forum. Forums are amazing for troubleshooting, but they are also full of people who only post when something goes wrong. The people whose kits work fine for ten years usually do not write a long post about it. So when you read about a problem, ask yourself, is this something the company confirms, something lots of people report, or something one person had happen once? That is exactly how I try to separate things on this channel.

So, to wrap this up. The problems repair techs keep seeing in Roland V-Drums are not usually mysterious. They are worn mesh heads and cushions, crosstalk from loose hardware, hi-hats that need calibration, cables and jacks that wear out, rubber that cracks with age and heat, and modules that have outdated firmware or messy settings. Most of them are cheap or free to fix if you know what to look for. And most of them can be prevented with a little bit of care.
