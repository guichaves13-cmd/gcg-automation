Epiphone was not born as Gibson's budget brand. It was born as Gibson's enemy. The company traces its roots back to a Greek instrument maker named Anastasios Stathopoulos, who started building instruments in the eighteen hundreds and eventually brought his workshop to New York. His son, Epaminondas, who everyone called Epi, took over the business, and the name Epiphone comes from him. Epi plus phone. Epi's sound.

In the nineteen thirties, Epiphone was one of the most respected guitar makers in America. Their archtop guitars went head to head with Gibson's archtops, and in a lot of jazz clubs in New York, the Epiphone was the guitar the serious players wanted. The two companies were in a genuine arms race. When Gibson made their archtop bodies bigger, Epiphone made theirs bigger still. When one added a feature, the other answered. For a while, it was not obvious who was going to win.

Then Epi died in the early nineteen forties, and the company slowly lost its way. Family disputes, labor problems, a move out of New York. By the mid nineteen fifties, Epiphone was in trouble. And in nineteen fifty seven, Gibson, through its parent company at the time, bought Epiphone. The story goes that Gibson was really after the upright bass business, and the guitar side came along almost as a bonus.

And here is the first surprise. In the late fifties and through the sixties, Epiphone guitars were built in the very same factory as Gibson guitars. Kalamazoo, Michigan. Same building. Same workers. Same wood supply. Same era of craftsmanship that people now pay small fortunes for. The Epiphone Casino, the Epiphone Sheraton, the Epiphone Riviera, the Epiphone Texan. These were Kalamazoo guitars with a different name on the headstock.

That is why a nineteen sixties Epiphone Casino is not a budget guitar. It is a vintage American guitar, built side by side with Gibsons, and if you have ever wondered why one of the most famous bands in history played Casinos instead of Gibsons, now you know. They were not settling. They were playing guitars built by the same hands.

So if somebody tells you an Epiphone is just a cheap copy, ask them which Epiphone. Because for about fifteen years, the answer to who made Epiphone guitars was very simple. Gibson did. In Michigan.

That changed in the early nineteen seventies. Costs in America were going up, competition from Japan was getting serious, and Gibson's owners made a decision that would define the Epiphone name for the next fifty years. They moved Epiphone production overseas.

The first stop was Japan. Through the seventies, Epiphones were built in Japanese factories, and this is where the story gets interesting for anyone who hunts the used market.
