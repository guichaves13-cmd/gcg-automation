Why Clint Walker Walked Off Cheyenne: 8 Secrets Warner Bros. Hid

In the spring of 1958, the star of one of the most popular westerns on American television did something almost nobody did in those days. He walked away.

He was six feet six inches tall. He had shoulders like a barn door and a voice like distant thunder. Every week on ABC, millions of people watched him ride across the screen as Cheyenne Bodie. And one day, he simply stopped showing up for work.

His name was Clint Walker. And the studio that owned him, Warner Bros., did not want the public to know why.

Today we're going to open up eight secrets about Clint Walker and Cheyenne that the studio never put in the fan magazines. One of them involves a fight over money. One of them involves a replacement cowboy most people have forgotten. And secret number one is so incredible that, if you saw it in a movie, you wouldn't believe it. Clint Walker was declared dead, and he got back up.

Secret number eight. Cheyenne Bodie was born Norman.

Clint Walker was born Norman Eugene Walker on May 30, 1927, in Hartford, Illinois, a small town near the Mississippi River, not far from St. Louis. And he wasn't born alone. He had a twin sister, Lucy. There's that word. Twins.

The family did not have much. This was the Great Depression, and young Norman went to work early. He left school as a teenager and took whatever jobs he could find. He worked in a factory. He worked on riverboats. He joined the Merchant Marine near the end of World War Two.

Later, he went west. He worked in the oil fields. He worked construction. In Las Vegas, he worked as a bouncer and as a security man, and, according to the stories, as a deputy sheriff. A man that size had no trouble finding work keeping the peace.

Then he went to Hollywood to try his luck. And somebody decided "Norman Walker" didn't sound like a cowboy. He tried a couple of names. Eventually he became Clint Walker. And Clint Walker sounded like a man who belonged on a horse.

Secret number seven. His big break came in a Bible epic.

Before Cheyenne, Clint Walker had only bit parts. One of them was a little bit of movie trivia that fans love.

He appeared in Cecil B. DeMille's "The Ten Commandments," the giant 1956 epic starring Charlton Heston as Moses. Clint Walker played a Sardinian captain, a guard. You have to look carefully to spot him, but he's there.

Here's the thing. He was so big, so striking, that people at Warner Bros. noticed him around this time. The studio was looking for something brand new: a western made just for television, with a hero who looked larger than life.

They found him. Warner Bros. signed Clint Walker to a contract. And that contract would become the biggest problem of his career.

Secret number six. Cheyenne made television history, and Warner Bros. knew it.

In September of 1955, ABC premiered a program called "Warner Bros. Presents." It was one of the very first times a major Hollywood movie studio made shows specifically for television. Back then, the big studios saw TV as the enemy. Television was stealing their audience.

Warner Bros. decided to join it instead.

And of the shows in that package, the one that broke out was Cheyenne. It's widely remembered as the first hour-long western series made for television. Before Cheyenne, TV westerns were usually thirty minutes long, more like old movie serials for kids. Cheyenne was an hour. It was made with movie studio money, movie studio crews and movie studio locations. It looked like a real western picture, every week, for free, in your living room.

It was a big hit. And it opened the door for all the hour-long westerns that came after it. Wagon Train. Rawhide. Bonanza. The Virginian. A whole golden age of the TV western rolled through the door that Clint Walker kicked open.

Cheyenne Bodie himself was a drifter, a frontier scout who rode from place to place, helping people and getting into trouble. He was strong, but he was gentle. He rarely started a fight. Walker played him with a quiet decency that viewers loved.

And here's a detail that fans of the time remember well: Cheyenne Bodie took his shirt off a lot. The studio knew exactly what it had. A lot of women tuned in for that, too.

Secret number five. He was a star, and he was barely getting paid like one.

Here is the part that the fan magazines didn't print.

In the 1950s, the big studios still worked the old way. They signed actors to long contracts, and the studio controlled almost everything. Clint Walker was the star of one of the biggest shows on television. But he was being paid like a contract player.

And it went further than that. Stars back then made a lot of money from personal appearances: rodeos, fairs, store openings. Fans would pay to see Cheyenne Bodie in person. But under his Warner Bros. contract, the studio took a large share of what he earned from those appearances. According to the accounts of the dispute, it was half.

There were other complaints, too. He wanted more freedom to choose his own work. He wanted a bigger share of the money from records and merchandise. He also wanted some limits on how many hours he had to work. A western like Cheyenne was a hard, physical job, made on a tight television schedule.

Walker asked for changes. Warner Bros. said no.

So in 1958, Clint Walker did what almost no contract player dared to do. He walked out.

Secret number four. Warner Bros. replaced Cheyenne with another cowboy, and hoped you wouldn't notice.

The studio was not about to lose its hit show. So it came up with a solution.

Cheyenne kept going, but without Cheyenne. Warner Bros. brought in a new actor named Ty Hardin, another tall, good-looking young man, to play a new character named Bronco Layne. During the 1958 to 1959 season, episodes of "Bronco" ran in Cheyenne's time slot, under the Cheyenne banner.

Think about that. The show was named for a character who wasn't in it.

Here's a backstage side trip worth knowing. Warner Bros. was doing this kind of thing across its whole western lineup. Around the same time, James Garner, the star of "Maverick," also fought with the studio over his contract. And he eventually took them to court and won his freedom. The late 1950s were a time when the TV stars were finally starting to push back against the old studio system. Clint Walker was one of the first to do it.

For a lot of viewers at home, the switch was confusing. They tuned in for Cheyenne Bodie, and they got somebody else. Warner Bros. did its best to make it seem normal. It wasn't.

Secret number three. He came back, and the studio blinked.

Clint Walker stayed off the show for most of a season. And in the end, Warner Bros. came to the table.

In 1959, he returned to Cheyenne. The reports of the deal say the studio gave ground on some of what he had asked for, including a better arrangement on his personal appearance money and his records. It wasn't everything he wanted. But it was enough.

Cheyenne kept running until 1962, and Bronco went on as its own separate series. In all, Cheyenne produced more than one hundred episodes over seven seasons.

But Clint Walker never forgot what happened. For the rest of his career, he was careful about the contracts he signed and the people he trusted. The big gentle man who played a drifter on TV had learned a hard lesson about Hollywood.

Secret number two. The Dirty Dozen, and the gentle giant.

After Cheyenne, Clint Walker went to the movies. And in 1967, he landed a part in one of the biggest war films of the decade: "The Dirty Dozen."

He played Samson Posey, one of the twelve convicted soldiers sent on a suicide mission behind enemy lines. He was surrounded by tough guys. Lee Marvin. Charles Bronson. Jim Brown. Telly Savalas. Donald Sutherland. And still, the six-foot-six Clint Walker stood out.

Now here's something about the man himself that the studios never sold, because it didn't fit the tough-guy image. Clint Walker was, by every account, one of the gentlest men in Hollywood. He was known for his kindness on set. He didn't drink much. He took care of his health and his body long before that was fashionable. He was interested in spirituality and in the outdoors. People who worked with him described him as polite and soft-spoken.

He had a philosophy that even a strong man should never use his strength unless he had to. And that's exactly how he played Cheyenne Bodie.

And now, secret number one. Clint Walker died on a ski slope, and lived.

In 1971, Clint Walker was skiing at Mammoth Mountain, in California. He fell, and in the accident, the point of a ski pole went into his chest and pierced his heart.

By the time he got to the hospital, the situation was so bad that, according to the accounts of what happened, he was pronounced dead.

But a doctor noticed signs of life. They rushed him into surgery. And somehow, this enormous man, with a wound to his heart, pulled through.

Think about that. Doctors had called it. And Clint Walker got up and walked out of that hospital.

Within a couple of months, he was back to work. He went on acting for years after that. He did movies, he did television, and he even lent his deep voice to an animated film, "Small Soldiers," in 1998, playing a toy soldier commander named Nick Nitro.

And remember the word I asked you to hold on to? Twins. The little boy from Hartford, Illinois, who came into this world with his sister Lucy beside him, lived a full life, a very long life. Clint Walker died on May 21, 2018, in Grass Valley, California. He was ninety years old. Just nine days short of his ninety-first birthday.

The man they declared dead in 1971 lived another forty-seven years.

So that's the real story. Cheyenne Bodie was a boy named Norman who worked on riverboats and in oil fields. A bouncer in Las Vegas. A bit player in The Ten Commandments. The star of the first hour-long western on television. The man who walked out on one of the biggest studios in Hollywood, and made them blink. And a gentle giant who survived a ski pole through the heart.
