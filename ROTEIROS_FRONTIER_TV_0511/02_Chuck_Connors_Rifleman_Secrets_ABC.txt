Chuck Connors Did What? 8 Rifleman Secrets ABC Never Told Fans

The game was delayed while they found a replacement.

Secret number eight. The Rifleman's real name was not Chuck.

Chuck Connors was born Kevin Joseph Connors on April 10, 1921, in Brooklyn, New York. His parents had come to America from Newfoundland, and the family did not have much money. Brooklyn in the 1920s and 1930s was a tough place to grow up, and young Kevin learned early to use his size and his fists.

So where did "Chuck" come from? The story told over the years is that, when he played first base as a young man, he would yell to the pitcher, "Chuck it to me, chuck it to me!" And the nickname stuck. Kevin Connors became Chuck Connors, and he was Chuck for the rest of his life.

Now, picture Lucas McCain. Calm, quiet, a widower raising his boy on a ranch in New Mexico Territory. The man behind that character was a loud, funny, competitive kid from the streets of Brooklyn who never sat still. ABC didn't sell that. They sold the quiet rancher.

Secret number seven. He was a pro in two sports before Hollywood ever called.

This is where the story gets almost hard to believe.

Chuck Connors was a gifted athlete, good enough to earn a scholarship to Seton Hall. Then World War Two came, and like millions of young men, he served in the Army. After the war, he went straight into professional sports.

In basketball, he played for the Boston Celtics in their very first seasons, 1946 to 1948. That's where the shattered backboard comes in. On that opening night in Boston, the story goes, he was taking practice shots, and the rim and glass gave way. It's remembered as one of the first backboards ever broken in professional basketball.

But basketball wasn't his first love. Baseball was. And Connors made it to the major leagues, too. He appeared in one game for the Brooklyn Dodgers in 1949, his hometown team. Then in 1951, he played in sixty-six games at first base for the Chicago Cubs.

So the man who played The Rifleman is one of the very few people who ever played both in the NBA's early days and in Major League Baseball. Most TV cowboys learned to ride a horse for the job. Chuck Connors had already lived a whole career as a professional athlete before he ever said a word on camera.

Secret number six. Baseball is what made him an actor.

After the Cubs, Connors was sent to play for the Los Angeles Angels, which back then was a minor league team in the Pacific Coast League. And Los Angeles in the early 1950s was full of people from the movie business.

Connors was a natural showman. He would entertain the crowd. He recited poetry. He told stories. He clowned around. And people in Hollywood noticed.

His first movie role came in 1952 in "Pat and Mike," a comedy starring Spencer Tracy and Katharine Hepburn. He played a small part as a police captain. Think about that. His very first time in front of a movie camera, he was in a scene with two of the biggest legends in Hollywood history.

After that, he gave up baseball for acting. He worked steadily through the 1950s, often playing tough guys and heavies. And then in 1957, he played a role that a lot of families remember even if they don't remember his name. He was in Walt Disney's "Old Yeller," as Burn Sanderson, the man who comes looking for his dog.

Families who cried their eyes out over Old Yeller had already met the man who would become The Rifleman, and most of them never put the two together.

Secret number five. The Rifleman began as a story by Sam Peckinpah.

Here's something even many big western fans don't know. The man who wrote the original Rifleman story was Sam Peckinpah. Yes, the same Sam Peckinpah who later became famous for violent, hard-edged movies like "The Wild Bunch."

The story first appeared as an episode of another anthology series, Dick Powell's Zane Grey Theatre, in 1958. It was called "The Sharpshooter." It was so well received that it became a series of its own on ABC, beginning in the fall of 1958.

Peckinpah wrote and directed some of the early episodes. And you can feel his touch in those first seasons. The Rifleman could be surprisingly tough for a family show. People died. Lucas McCain fired that rifle a lot. In fact, people have counted, and the show had one of the highest body counts of any western on TV at the time.

But it also had something else Peckinpah cared about: the relationship between a father and a son. That's the heart of The Rifleman. Every episode, no matter how many bad guys came through North Fork, came back to Lucas teaching his boy Mark how to be a good man.

There's a story that Connors was not the first choice for the part, and that he wasn't sure he wanted it. What helped convince him, according to the accounts, was meeting the young actor who would play his son. Which brings us to that name I asked you to remember.

Secret number four. Mark McCain was a real boy who became a real son.

Mark McCain was played by Johnny Crawford. When The Rifleman started, he was only about twelve years old. He had already worked as one of the Mouseketeers on Disney's Mickey Mouse Club.

And something happened between Chuck Connors and Johnny Crawford that went far beyond the camera. Over five seasons, they spent more time together on that set than many real fathers and sons spend together in a year. Crawford talked for the rest of his life about how much Connors meant to him, and how he looked up to him. Johnny Crawford was even nominated for an Emmy Award for his work as Mark, when he was still a boy.

The Rifleman ran on ABC from 1958 to 1963, for five seasons and one hundred sixty-eight episodes. Every week, millions of kids watched a father who listened to his son, who explained things, who apologized when he was wrong. A lot of men who grew up in that era have said that Lucas McCain was the father they wished they'd had.

And the warmth they saw on screen was real. That's something ABC could never have manufactured.

Secret number three. That famous rifle was a modified 1892 Winchester.

Let's talk about the star of the show that wasn't a person. The rifle.

Lucas McCain's rifle was a Winchester Model 1892, a lever-action rifle. And it had been modified so that he could fire it very, very fast. It had a large ring for the lever, and a special screw in the trigger guard. When Connors worked the lever, that screw would trip the trigger, so the rifle could fire as quickly as he could cycle it.

That's how you got that iconic opening, with Lucas McCain striding down the street firing shot after shot. It was a gimmick that made the show stand out from every other western on television. Other cowboys had six-shooters. Lucas McCain had a rifle that fired like nothing else.

Here's a fun little detail. Historians of the Old West like to point out that The Rifleman is set in the 1880s, but the Model 1892 Winchester didn't come out until 1892. So technically, Lucas McCain was firing a rifle from the future. But nobody watching on a Tuesday night in 1959 was counting.

Now a quick side trip, because there's a great behind-the-scenes story here. Chuck Connors practiced with that rifle constantly. He was an athlete, after all, and he approached it like an athlete. He wanted the move to look natural, smooth and real. The way he spun and worked that lever became his trademark, and fans would ask him to show it for the rest of his life.

Secret number two. The Rifleman had dinner with the leader of the Soviet Union.

This is the one that makes people say, "Wait, what?"

In the early 1970s, in the middle of the Cold War, Leonid Brezhnev, the leader of the Soviet Union, came to the United States for a summit with President Richard Nixon. That was in June of 1973.

And it turned out that Brezhnev was a fan of American westerns. He was a big fan of Chuck Connors in particular. The Rifleman was one of the few American shows that had been shown in the Soviet Union.

So at a reception in California, during that visit, Chuck Connors and Leonid Brezhnev met. Photographers caught the moment. In the most famous photo, the big American cowboy and the Soviet leader are hugging, and Brezhnev is grinning. Connors even gave him a gift, a pair of Colt revolvers.

Think about that. In the middle of the Cold War, when both countries had missiles pointed at each other, a TV cowboy from Brooklyn had the leader of the Soviet Union hugging him and laughing. Connors later visited the Soviet Union, too.

ABC never could have scripted that one.

And now, secret number one. Chuck Connors was so good, he got nominated for playing the worst man in America.

After The Rifleman ended, Connors could have spent the rest of his life playing the good guy. He did play another hero for a while, in a western called "Branded" on NBC, in 1965 and 1966, as a cavalry officer wrongly accused of cowardice.

But in 1977, he made a choice that shocked a lot of his fans.

He joined the cast of "Roots," the historic ABC miniseries about slavery in America, based on the book by Alex Haley. Roots was one of the most-watched programs in the history of American television. And Chuck Connors played Tom Moore, a cruel slave owner.

This was the man who had spent five years as the most loving father on television. And now he was playing one of the most hateful men on the screen. He was so convincing that he was nominated for an Emmy Award for it.

Why would he do it? Because Chuck Connors was never just a cowboy. He was a working actor who wanted to show what he could do. And he was willing to take a role that made people uncomfortable, in a story that America needed to hear.

Chuck Connors died on November 10, 1992, in Los Angeles. He was seventy-one years old. He had been a smoker for many years, and the cause was lung cancer.

And remember the name I asked you to hold on to? Mark. Johnny Crawford, the boy who played Mark McCain, stayed loyal to his TV father for the rest of his life. He spoke about Chuck Connors with love at reunions and in interviews for decades. Johnny Crawford died in 2021. And to the very end, he still talked about "Pa."

So that's the real story. Lucas McCain was a loud kid from Brooklyn named Kevin. A Boston Celtic who broke a backboard on opening night, eighty years ago today. A Chicago Cub. A Disney actor. A man who hugged the leader of the Soviet Union. And an actor brave enough to play a villain in Roots after years as television's best dad.

Not bad for a guy who just wanted somebody to chuck him the ball.
