8 Michael Landon Secrets NBC Kept Hidden From Bonanza Fans

On the night of September 12, 1959, millions of American families sat down in front of their television sets and met the Cartwrights for the very first time. A big ranch called the Ponderosa. A wise father. Three sons who could not have been more different from each other. And the youngest one, Little Joe, was played by a skinny twenty-two-year-old kid with a big smile and a head full of dark curly hair.

His name on the screen was Michael Landon.

But that was not the name he was born with. And that was only the first thing most Bonanza fans never knew about him.

For fourteen seasons, NBC sold Little Joe Cartwright as the happy-go-lucky heartthrob of the Ponderosa. What the network did not put in the press releases was the boy behind the character: a kid who grew up afraid, ashamed and alone, who almost had a completely different life, and who quietly became one of the most powerful men in all of television.

Today we're going to walk through eight of those secrets, one at a time. Some of them are funny. Some of them are sad. And the last one, secret number one, changed the way you remember every single episode of Bonanza you ever watched.

Secret number eight. Little Joe was not born Michael Landon.

Michael Landon was born Eugene Maurice Orowitz on October 31, 1936. Yes, Halloween. He was born in Forest Hills, in Queens, New York, and when he was still a little boy his family moved to Collingswood, New Jersey, a small town just across the river from Philadelphia.

His father, Eli Orowitz, was Jewish, and he worked in movie theaters and in publicity. His mother was a Catholic woman who had once been a dancer and a comedienne on the stage. Her stage name was Peggy O'Neill. There's that name.

Now, in a small town in the 1940s, a mixed family like that did not always have an easy time. Young Eugene was a thin, shy boy, and by his own later accounts he was teased and picked on. He did not feel like he fit anywhere.

When he started out as an actor in the 1950s, he needed a new name. The story most often told is that he picked "Landon" out of a telephone book. Whether that's exactly how it happened or not, the point is the same: the boy from Collingswood, New Jersey, built a new man from scratch. Eugene Orowitz disappeared, and Michael Landon walked onto the set.

Millions of fans who wrote letters to "Little Joe" had no idea that the man reading those letters had grown up under another name entirely.

Secret number seven. His first big dream had nothing to do with acting.

Before Hollywood, Michael Landon wanted to be an athlete. And not just any athlete. He threw the javelin.

In high school in Collingswood, the skinny kid who everybody overlooked found out that he could throw that spear farther than almost anybody around. According to the accounts of his life, he set a national high school record in the javelin, and that arm earned him an athletic scholarship to the University of Southern California.

Here's the part that sounds almost like a Bonanza episode. Landon later said that he believed his long hair gave him his strength, a little like the story of Samson in the Bible. He kept it long, and he believed in it.

Then, at USC, he got hurt. He tore the ligaments in his arm, and his throwing career was over. Just like that, the dream was gone. He left school, and he took whatever work he could find. He worked in a warehouse. He did odd jobs. A young man who had built his whole idea of himself around one talent suddenly had nothing.

It was a friend, by most accounts, who talked him into trying acting classes. And the rest, as they say, is television history. But think about this the next time you watch Little Joe jump onto a horse or win a fistfight. The man you're watching was an athlete who lost his first dream before he was twenty years old.

Secret number six. Before he was a Cartwright, he was a werewolf.

This is one that a lot of Bonanza fans still don't know. Two years before NBC made him a cowboy, Michael Landon starred in a low-budget horror movie with one of the most famous titles of the decade.

"I Was a Teenage Werewolf," from 1957.

Yes, really. The future Little Joe Cartwright played a troubled high school student named Tony who gets turned into a hairy, snarling monster. It was made quickly and cheaply, and it made a whole lot of money at drive-in theaters all over America.

For a young actor, it was a break. It put his face in front of teenagers everywhere. But it also was the kind of picture that could have trapped him in B-movies for the rest of his career. Lots of young actors who started in movies like that never got out.

Landon got out. He did guest roles on television westerns, the shows that were everywhere in the late 1950s. And then came the role that would change his life. When NBC and producer David Dortort put together a new western about a father and his three sons, Landon got the part of the youngest son, Joseph Cartwright.

From teenage werewolf to Little Joe in about two years. NBC was certainly not going to put that old horror picture on the posters for its big new family show.

Secret number five. Bonanza was NBC's color sales pitch, and Little Joe was part of the package.

Here is something most people at home never thought about. In 1959, almost nobody in America owned a color television set. They were expensive, and there wasn't much to watch in color anyway.

NBC was owned by RCA, and RCA made color television sets. So NBC had a very big reason to want a show that looked beautiful in color.

Bonanza was one of the first westerns broadcast in color, and it is widely remembered as the first regularly scheduled weekly television series filmed in color. Think about what that meant. The wide-open skies. The green trees around Lake Tahoe. The Cartwrights in their famous outfits, which, by the way, they wore in almost every episode. That was partly practical. If the men always wore the same clothes, the production could reuse footage of them riding without the costumes giving away the trick.

The show did not start out as a giant hit. In its early seasons, the ratings were not great. But NBC kept it on the air, largely because it was such a good showcase for color television. Then, in 1961, the show moved to Sunday nights, and everything changed. Bonanza became a monster. For several seasons in the mid-1960s, it was the number one show in all of America.

So when you remember Little Joe's bright green jacket and Hoss's big hat against the blue Nevada sky, remember this: part of the reason it all looked so good is that a television company wanted to sell you a new TV set.

Secret number four. The boy who wet the bed.

This one is personal, and Michael Landon told it himself.

When he was a boy in Collingswood, he had a problem that a lot of children have but almost nobody talked about back then. He wet the bed. And it went on for years, well past the age when most children stop.

According to Landon, his mother did not handle it with kindness. He said she would hang his wet sheets out of the window, where the other kids in the neighborhood could see them. So every day after school, he would run home as fast as he could, trying to get to the sheets and pull them down before anybody saw.

Some people who have told his story believe that all that running is part of how a skinny little boy turned into a young athlete.

Now here's what makes this a secret worth knowing. Years later, when Michael Landon had the power to make whatever he wanted, he turned that childhood pain into a TV movie. It was called "The Loneliest Runner," and it aired on NBC in 1976. He wrote it and directed it. It tells the story of a boy who wets the bed, whose mother hangs the sheets out the window, and who runs home every day to take them down. And that boy grows up to be an Olympic athlete.

He was telling the whole country his secret, and most people never realized it was his own life on the screen.

And remember Peggy? This is where she comes back. The relationship between Michael and his mother was, by all of his own accounts, very difficult. He described her as unpredictable, dramatic, and at times frightening. The man who would later become television's most famous gentle father figure, Charles Ingalls on Little House on the Prairie, grew up without much gentleness at home.

Secret number three. Little Joe quietly took over the writing room.

Here's something NBC didn't exactly advertise to the folks at home. As the years went on, Michael Landon was not just acting on Bonanza. He was learning everything about how the show was made.

He watched the directors. He watched the writers. He asked questions. And in the 1960s, he started writing episodes of Bonanza himself. Then he started directing them, too.

That was unusual. Most actors on a hit show were happy to show up, say their lines and go home. Landon wanted to know how the whole machine worked. Some people on the show thought he was too ambitious. Some found him difficult. But nobody could say he wasn't talented. By the later seasons, a number of the episodes fans remember most were written or directed by the youngest Cartwright.

Now let's take a little side trip, because there's a backstage story here that matters.

The Bonanza family did not stay the same. Pernell Roberts, who played the oldest brother Adam, was never happy on the show. He felt the scripts were too simple, and he left after the 1964 and 1965 season. Not many actors walked away from the number one show in America. He did.

So by the late 1960s, the show leaned more and more on three men: Lorne Greene as Ben, Dan Blocker as Hoss, and Michael Landon as Little Joe.

And those three became close. Especially Landon and Blocker. Big Dan Blocker, the gentle giant, was like a real older brother to him on that set. Keep that in mind. Because that friendship leads straight into the next secret.

Secret number two. The day the Ponderosa lost its heart.

In May of 1972, Dan Blocker went into the hospital for what was supposed to be a fairly routine operation, to have his gallbladder removed. A few days later, he died from a blood clot in his lung. He was only forty-three years old.

The cast was devastated. And the show had a terrible problem. Hoss Cartwright was not just a character. For millions of viewers, Hoss was the heart of Bonanza.

The producers decided not to replace him with another actor. Instead, Hoss was simply gone, and the show kept going without him.

Michael Landon, who had lost his closest friend on the set, wrote and directed a two-part episode for the next season called "Forever." It's a story in which Little Joe falls in love and gets married, and then loses his new wife in a terrible way. A lot of fans and critics believe the grief you see on Little Joe's face in that story was not all acting.

But without Hoss, the magic was fading. NBC moved Bonanza to a different night, the ratings dropped, and the network canceled the show. The final episode aired in January of 1973, after fourteen seasons and more than four hundred episodes. At that point, it was one of the longest-running westerns in the history of American television, second only to Gunsmoke.

Most fans at home never knew how fast it ended. One season the Cartwrights were family. The next, they were gone.

And now, secret number one. The real power on NBC was Little Joe.

When Bonanza ended, a lot of people figured that would be the last they heard from Michael Landon. He was a TV cowboy, and the age of the TV western was ending.

They were wrong.

The little boy from Collingswood who ran home to pull the sheets off the window had spent fourteen years learning how to make television. And now he used every bit of it.

In 1974, just one year after the Cartwrights rode off for the last time, NBC premiered "Little House on the Prairie," based on the books by Laura Ingalls Wilder. Michael Landon wasn't just the star, playing Charles Ingalls. He was the executive producer, and he wrote and directed many of the episodes himself. Little House ran for nine seasons, until 1983, and it became one of the most beloved family shows ever made.

Then, in 1984, he did it again. "Highway to Heaven," also on NBC, where he played an angel named Jonathan Smith, sent to earth to help people. He created it, he produced it, he wrote and directed episodes, and it ran for five seasons.

Think about that. For close to thirty years, almost without a break, Michael Landon was on NBC. Three hit series. Fourteen years on Bonanza, nine on Little House, five on Highway to Heaven. Very few people in the history of television ever had a run like that on one network.

The skinny kid nobody noticed became the man NBC trusted with its family audience, decade after decade.

And here is the part that hurts. In April of 1991, Michael Landon announced that he had pancreatic cancer. He went on television and talked about it openly, and he talked about it with humor and courage, the way he had handled so much of his life. He appeared on The Tonight Show with Johnny Carson and joked about it, because he wanted people to see that he was still Michael.

He died on July 1, 1991, at his home in Malibu, California. He was just fifty-four years old.

Fifty-four. Younger than many of the people watching this video right now.

So that's the real story NBC never put in the publicity photos. Little Joe Cartwright was a boy named Eugene who was ashamed of his own bed sheets. A young man who lost his first dream to an injury. A B-movie werewolf who became the youngest son of the Ponderosa. A grieving friend who wrote his sadness into the scripts. And, in the end, one of the most powerful creators in the history of television.

Every time you see that smile on Little Joe's face, now you know what it cost to get there.
