Picture a pair of speakers on a shelf in a front room in Stockport. They're small, black, a bit scuffed at the corners, and the drivers sit in that odd, upside-down order the brand was famous for, with the tweeter underneath the woofer instead of on top. The owner bought them second-hand in 1991 for about eighty quid. He's told everyone who'll listen that they're proper British engineering, made in Huntingdon, designed by people who cared. And last month he decided to treat himself to the new version, the reissue, the one with the same badge on the front. He went to look up where it was made. And what he found genuinely stopped him in his tracks.

Because the honest answer to "who really makes Mission speakers now?" is not the answer most British owners expect. It isn't a simple story of a British firm being bought and gutted, either. It's stranger than that, and in some ways it's better than that, and in some ways it's worse. By the end of this video you'll know exactly who owns the name, where the boxes are actually built, where the engineering still happens, and, most usefully of all, which years of Mission you should hunt for on the second-hand market and which ones you should quietly walk past.

But to get there, we have to go back to a small industrial unit in Cambridgeshire in the late nineteen-seventies, and to a man most people in the hi-fi world have never heard of.

Mission was founded in 1977 by Farad Azima. He wasn't a grey-suited executive and he wasn't a garden-shed tinkerer. He was an engineer and an entrepreneur with a very particular idea: that British loudspeakers had got a bit comfortable. A lot of the big names of the time were making speakers that sounded polite, warm, a little bit woolly. Lovely with a string quartet, less lovely with anything that actually moved. Azima wanted speakers that were tighter, quicker, more precise, and he wanted to do it at a price a normal working person could afford.

The company set up in Huntingdon, and that word, Huntingdon, matters for the rest of this story, so hold onto it.

The first product that really put Mission on the map was the 770. It arrived in the late seventies, a fairly big standmount, and it had a reputation almost immediately. Reviewers liked it, dealers liked it, and crucially, people who heard it in a shop liked it. It used a polypropylene bass driver at a time when plenty of rivals were still using paper or Bextrene cones, and it had a clarity in the midrange that made voices sound like they were actually in the room. If you were a student in 1980 with a decent turntable and a part-time job, the 770 was the speaker you dreamed about while you were actually buying something cheaper.

And then came the speaker that most of you watching will actually remember, because there's a decent chance one of your parents owned it, or you did. The Mission 700. Small, affordable, and genuinely fun. It was the kind of speaker that turned up in bedrooms, in student flats, in the back room of a pub that played records on a Thursday. It made Mission a household name in a way the 770 never could, because the 700 was the one people could actually afford.

From there, the range exploded. The 70 series, the 700 series, the 760, the 761, the 780, the 731, the 751 and 752 and 753, and later the M-series. And with them came that design signature I mentioned at the very start, the inverted driver layout, tweeter at the bottom, bass driver on top. Mission's argument was about timing, getting the sound from both drivers to arrive at your ears in the right order when the speaker sat on a typical stand and you sat in a typical chair. Some people thought it was genius. Some people thought it was a gimmick. Everybody recognised it instantly. You could walk into a shop in Glasgow or Plymouth and spot a Mission from across the room.

Now here's the first open loop, and I'll come back to it properly in a few minutes. Mission didn't just make speakers. In the early eighties it made something else, something that would go on to outlive Mission's independence entirely and become a respected British brand in its own right. Most people who own one of these products have absolutely no idea it started life inside the Mission building. Keep that in your head, because it changes how you look at everything that happened next.

Let me introduce you to a man I'll call Derek. Derek's a composite, I'll be honest about that, he's built from three or four bench technicians I've spoken to and read about over the years, but every detail about him is real somewhere. Derek runs a little repair bench in a lock-up just outside Leeds. He's been fixing hi-fi since the early eighties, and he reckons he's had more Missions across his bench than any other British speaker brand.

Derek has a phrase for the old Huntingdon stuff. He calls it "honest kit". What he means is that when you take the back off an early Mission, you can see what they were thinking. The crossovers are sensible. The cabinets are built to a price but not built cheaply. The drivers were often made or heavily specified in-house. And when something goes wrong, which, after forty years, it will, it usually goes wrong in a way you can fix. Foam surrounds perish, capacitors drift, a tweeter gets blown at a party in 1994. All fixable. Derek's seen pairs of 700s come in that were bought for forty pounds at a car boot, and go home sounding better than they did when they were new.

So that's the golden era. A founder with a clear idea, a factory in Huntingdon, drivers developed by people who were obsessed with them, and a range that went from student budget to serious audiophile. Hold that picture in your head, because what happened next is where it all gets complicated.

Through the late eighties and into the nineties, the British hi-fi industry went through a brutal patch. Cheap, competent kit from Japan had already reshaped the amplifier and cassette deck market. Speakers had held out longer, because they're big, heavy and expensive to ship, and because British buyers had a real loyalty to British sound. But margins were getting squeezed everywhere. Retailers were consolidating. Lots of the smaller British brands found themselves either selling up, merging, or simply vanishing.

Mission was not immune to any of that. And in the late nineteen-nineties the company changed hands. Mission was bought by the Verity Group, which already owned Wharfedale. So for a while, Mission and Wharfedale sat under the same British corporate roof. That alone surprised a lot of owners, because in the shops these brands had felt like rivals. Then, in the following years, those audio brands were sold on again, and they ended up under the ownership of a company called IAG, the International Audio Group.

And this is the moment where our friend in Stockport looked up from his phone and said something I'm not going to repeat on a family channel.

IAG is headquartered in Shenzhen, in southern China. It's a large audio manufacturer with its own factories. And under its umbrella today sit some of the most famous names in British hi-fi history. Wharfedale. Quad. Mission. Plus several other brands you may well recognise, including some that have been relaunched in recent years. If you've watched our video on who really makes Wharfedale now, some of this will sound very familiar, because the answer for Wharfedale and the answer for Mission is, to a large extent, the same company.

Right. Now, it would be very easy at this point to do the lazy version of this story. British brand bought by Chinese company, quality falls off a cliff, the end. Lots of videos do exactly that, and they get a lot of views doing it. But it isn't what actually happened, and if you're going to spend your own money on a pair of Missions, new or used, you deserve the real version.

Let's go back to that word I told you to hold onto. Huntingdon.

When IAG took on these British brands, it didn't simply close everything in the UK and move the lot to China. It kept a research and development presence in Huntingdon. Over the years, a lot of the acoustic engineering for Wharfedale, Quad and Mission has been associated with that UK R&D base. So the people voicing the speakers, the people deciding what the crossover does and how the cabinet should be braced and how the thing should actually sound in a British living room, have in many cases been working in Cambridgeshire, not far from where Farad Azima started the company back in 1977.

The manufacturing, though, the physical building of the boxes, the drivers, the assembly, the packing, is largely done in IAG's own factories in China.

So the honest answer to the title question is this. Mission speakers today are generally designed and voiced with significant input from engineers in Britain, and they are built in China by a large Chinese-owned audio group that also owns several of Mission's old British rivals.

Is that shocking? Well, it depends what you thought you were buying. If you've been telling people for twenty years that your modern Missions were hand-built in a British shed, then yes, I'm sorry, that's a shock. If you thought the brand had been reduced to a sticker with nothing British left in it at all, then the reality is actually a bit more reassuring than you feared.

But there's a twist in here, and it's the bit that most owners genuinely don't know. Remember the open loop from earlier? The other product that came out of the Mission building?

In the early eighties, Mission started making electronics. Amplifiers. And one of those amplifiers had a little half-width case, a very distinctive look, and a sound that punched way above its size. It was called the Cyrus. The Cyrus One and Cyrus Two were Mission products. They were sold as Mission Cyrus. And over time, Cyrus was spun out and became its own independent British company, still based in Cambridgeshire, still making that famous half-width box.

So here's the irony. When people talk about Mission being bought by a Chinese group, they often point to Cyrus as the "proper British" alternative. And Cyrus is, broadly speaking, a British-owned company that's continued making electronics with a strong UK identity. But Cyrus is Mission's child. The two separated decades ago, and they took completely different paths. One child stayed home. The other got adopted. If you've got a Cyrus amp driving a pair of old Missions, you've effectively reunited a family that hasn't spoken properly since the early nineties.

Let me bring in our second character. I'll call her Priya. Priya runs an independent hi-fi shop in Bristol, again a composite, but built from real conversations with real retailers. Priya has sold Mission on and off over the years, and she's had customers walk in with exactly the reaction our man in Stockport had.

She told me the conversation usually goes the same way. A customer, typically a bloke in his fifties, sometimes his sixties, comes in wanting "a proper British speaker". He sees something with a British badge at a reasonable price, gets excited, then asks where it's made. And when she tells him, honestly, that it's designed with UK input and built in China, his face drops. And then she does something clever. She plays it.

Because the thing is, factory location tells you a lot less about how a speaker sounds than people think. What matters is who designed it, what budget they had, what parts they were allowed to use, and how strict the quality control is. A well-run factory in Shenzhen building to a careful UK design can absolutely outperform a sloppy British workshop. And a well-designed speaker built cheaply in the wrong place can be a disappointment wherever the logo says it came from.

Priya's rule, which I think is a sensible one, is this. Judge the speaker, not the postcode. But be honest with yourself about what you're paying for. If the price is high because of "British heritage", and the heritage now mostly lives in the badge and the design office, then you need to decide whether that's worth it to you.

Which brings us to the big one. The reissue.

In recent years, Mission brought back the 770. The name that started it all. A modern take on that big standmount from the late seventies, with a retro look, wood finishes, and a price tag that tends to sit in the region of a few thousand pounds, roughly in the five grand area for a pair, though prices vary with finish and dealer, so check before you buy. Reviews have generally been very positive. People have praised its scale, its warmth, its sense of fun. It plays very nicely into the current love for heritage designs, the same wave that's brought back retro-looking Wharfedales and others.

And here's where our friend in Stockport finally found his answer. The new 770 is not a Huntingdon-built speaker from 1978. It's a modern product from IAG's Mission brand, voiced with British engineering involvement, built in the group's Chinese manufacturing operation. It looks like the old one. It's inspired by the old one. It wears the same number. But it's not the same company that made his eighty-quid 700s, and it's not the same factory.

So let me put it another way. If you buy a new 770 because you love how it sounds, brilliant. If you buy it because you think you're buying a little piece of 1978 Huntingdon, you might want to look at the second-hand market first. Which brings me to the part of this video I promised you at the start, and I think it's the most useful bit.

Which eras of Mission should you actually buy used?

Let's go back to Derek's bench in Leeds, because Derek has opinions, and frankly they're better informed than most magazine reviews.

Era one. The original Huntingdon years, roughly from 1977 through the eighties. This is the 770, the early 700, the 70 series, the 760 and 761 and 780 and so on. Derek's view is simple. If you find these in good condition, and the price is right, they're a lovely buy. The caveat is age. Many of these speakers are now well over forty years old. Foam surrounds on the bass drivers can rot and crumble. Crossover capacitors can drift. So budget for a refoam and possibly a recap. A good tech will charge you somewhere between fifty and a hundred and fifty pounds for that kind of work depending on the pair, and then you've got a speaker that, in Derek's words, "will see you out".

A pair of 700s at a car boot or on a local marketplace for forty to eighty pounds is, honestly, one of the best-value bits of British hi-fi history you can still buy. Original 770s are rarer and you'll pay more, often a few hundred pounds depending on condition, and sometimes more if a collector's interested. But they're the real thing.

Era two. The late eighties and the nineties. This is where you find a lot of the 760 series and the 730s and 750s, the 731, 751, 752, 753, and models like the 780 and its variants. Derek rates a lot of this stuff very highly. Many people think the 750 series in particular was some of Mission's best value ever, solid cabinets, good drivers, that famous inverted layout, and prices on the used market that can be surprisingly low. Again, check the surrounds, check the tweeters, listen before you buy if you can. But if you want classic Mission sound without paying silly money, this is the sweet spot.

Era three. The transition years, the late nineties into the two-thousands, when the company changed hands and then changed hands again. This is the era where Derek gets a bit cautious. Not because everything from this time is bad, there are some very good speakers in here, but because it's the era where things were changing fast. Ownership changed. Manufacturing moved. Ranges were refreshed and renamed. Quality can vary from model to model. His advice is to research the specific model rather than trusting the badge. Read old reviews from the time. Look at owner forums. Don't assume a speaker from 2004 is built the same way as one from 1989 just because the logo's the same.

Era four. Modern IAG-era Mission. The newer ranges, the reissues, the current lineup. Here's the funny thing. Some of the modern Missions are genuinely very good speakers, and on the second-hand market they can actually represent decent value, because a lot of buyers have turned their noses up at them purely because of where they're built. That's the sort of prejudice that can work in your favour if you're shopping used. If you can find a lightly used modern pair at a big discount from someone who's upgrading, and you like how they sound, there's no reason to turn your nose up at them.

But here's the warning, and this is what Priya in Bristol drums into her customers. Don't pay a heritage premium for a modern speaker on the used market. If a seller is listing a fairly recent pair of Missions and leaning hard on "classic British brand since 1977", remember what you now know. The badge has history. The box in front of you may not.

Let me tell you about our third character, a collector I'll call Malcolm. Malcolm lives near Huntingdon, as it happens, which I think is lovely, and he collects Mission. Not modern Mission, old Mission. He's got pairs of 770s, 700s, a couple of 780s and some of the oddities from the early eighties, plus a little Mission Cyrus amp that he refuses to let anyone else touch.

I asked Malcolm, or rather, the collectors Malcolm is built from, what they thought about the brand today. And the answer was more nuanced than you'd think. Malcolm doesn't hate the modern brand. He's actually glad the name survived. Lots of British hi-fi names from that era simply disappeared, and he'd rather the Mission name live on than vanish completely. What he dislikes is the way the history gets used to sell new products without people being told the full story.

His line was, "I don't mind them building it in China. I mind them letting people think it's still 1978." And I think that sums up how a lot of long-time Mission fans feel. It's not anger at a factory. It's frustration at a fog. When you buy something with a British name on it, a lot of people genuinely want to know what's British about it. And with Mission today, the answer is the history, the name, and a meaningful chunk of the design and voicing work. The building isn't.

So let's go back to our man in Stockport, the one with the eighty-quid 700s and the itchy credit card. What did he do?

He didn't buy the reissue. Not because he thought it was bad, he went and heard a pair and admitted they sounded lovely.
