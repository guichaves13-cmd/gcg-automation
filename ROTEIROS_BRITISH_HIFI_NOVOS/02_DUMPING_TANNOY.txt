Picture a terraced house in Leeds on a wet Tuesday night. In the back room, under a dust sheet, there's a pair of speakers the size of small wardrobes. Walnut cabinets, gold-coloured trim, a badge with a name on it that, for about seventy years, meant something very particular to British music lovers. Tannoy.

The man who owns them is selling. Not because they've broken. Not because he's skint. He's selling because of something he learned a few weeks ago, something he says he wishes he'd known before he spent the best part of a decade's savings on them.

And here's the odd thing. He isn't the only one. Ask around the independent shops, the bench techs, the lads who run the second-hand listings, and you'll hear versions of the same story. Tannoys are coming up for sale. Some are sitting there for months. Some are going for prices that would have been unthinkable a few years ago. And others, the right ones, are being snapped up within hours by people who know exactly what they're looking for.

So what's going on? Why are thousands of British listeners quietly walking away from one of the most famous speaker names this country ever produced? The answer isn't what most people expect. It's not one thing. It's three things stacked on top of each other, and the third one is the one that genuinely surprised me. Stick with me, because by the end of this you'll know whether you should sell, hold, or buy, and you'll know which Tannoys are about to become very hard to find.

Let's start where Tannoy started, because you can't understand why people are leaving unless you understand why they fell in love in the first place.

Tannoy's roots go back to the 1920s in London. The name itself came from a rectifier technology, tantalum and an alloy, and for a long while the word Tannoy meant public address systems more than it meant hi-fi. If you're of a certain age, you'll remember someone saying "announcement over the Tannoy" at a railway station or a holiday camp. The brand became a verb. That's how deep it went into British life.

But for hi-fi people, Tannoy means one thing above all else. The Dual Concentric.

Here's the idea, and it's a lovely one. In a normal speaker, the tweeter sits above the woofer. High frequencies come from one point, low frequencies from another. Your ears and brain stitch it together, and mostly it works. Tannoy's idea, which appeared in the years after the Second World War, was to put the tweeter right in the middle of the bass driver, firing through the throat of it, so that the whole sound comes from one point in space. A single source. Like a real instrument. Like a real voice.

If you've ever sat in front of a pair of big old Tannoys playing a solo cello or a well-recorded jazz vocal, you'll know what that does. The image locks in. The singer stands there, between the speakers, with a kind of solidity that's hard to describe until you've heard it. It's why studios used them for decades. It's why collectors in Japan have paid serious money for vintage British Tannoys. It's why, to this day, there are blokes in sheds across the Midlands rebuilding old Monitor Golds and Monitor Reds with the care of a watchmaker.

I spoke, in a roundabout way, to the sort of person who lives in that world. Let's call him Malcolm. He's a bench tech in Leeds, been repairing amplifiers and speakers since the days when you could still buy valves from a shop on the high street. Malcolm's composite of a few people I've heard from over the years, but every word of what he describes is the kind of thing you'll hear in any workshop in the country.

Malcolm says the older Tannoys come to him like old friends. Surrounds gone crispy, crossovers with tired capacitors, cabinets that have been knocked about by three house moves. And he says something I found striking. "The old ones," he says, "you can fix. Everything's there. Everything makes sense. You open them up and you can see somebody cared."

Keep that phrase in your head. Somebody cared. We're going to come back to it.

Now, the first of the three reasons people are dumping Tannoy is the simplest, and it's the one most people already suspect. It's about who owns the name.

For most of the late twentieth century, Tannoy was a British company making speakers in Britain. In the 1970s, manufacturing moved north to Coatbridge, just outside Glasgow, and for decades Coatbridge was Tannoy. Engineers, cabinet makers, people who'd worked there twenty or thirty years. When you bought a big Prestige Tannoy, a Kensington or a Canterbury or the enormous Westminster, you were buying something that had been built and finished by hand in Scotland.

Over the years, the company changed hands, as lots of British brands did. It became part of a Danish-led group alongside other pro audio names. And then, in 2015, that group was bought by what was then called the Music Group, the company built by Uli Behringer. Today it trades as Music Tribe.

Now, I want to be fair here, because this is where a lot of YouTube videos get lazy. Behringer is a name that makes some hi-fi purists wrinkle their noses, because it's associated with affordable pro audio gear. Mixers, interfaces, synths, stuff you'll find in a thousand bedroom studios. But affordable isn't the same as bad, and Music Tribe is a big, serious company that owns a lot of well-known brands. Plenty of musicians would tell you Behringer has done more to put decent kit in the hands of ordinary people than almost anyone.

So the takeover on its own isn't the shocking reason. Plenty of British brands have foreign owners now. Wharfedale. Mission. Quad. You know the story. If you've watched our videos on KEF and Wharfedale, you'll know that ownership on its own doesn't kill a brand.

What matters is what happens next. And with Tannoy, what happened next is that the centre of gravity shifted.

Over the years after the takeover, much of the manufacturing that had been associated with Coatbridge was scaled back, and production of more of the range moved to Music Tribe's facilities overseas. The details of exactly what was made where, and when, are the sort of thing you'll find argued about endlessly on forums, and I'm not going to pretend to have a definitive timeline. But the direction of travel is clear enough. The Scotland part of the Tannoy story got smaller.

And for a certain kind of British buyer, that matters enormously. Not for snobbery. For a very practical reason. Malcolm's reason. Somebody cared.

Let me introduce you to someone else. Her name, for our purposes, is Jo, and she runs an independent hi-fi shop in Bristol. Small place. Listening room at the back, a sofa that's seen better days, a kettle that never stops. Jo has sold Tannoys for years. And she told me something that I think captures the mood perfectly.

"People don't come in asking whether it's made in Scotland," she says. "They come in, they listen, and then, after they've fallen for it, they ask. And when the answer gets complicated, you can see their face change."

That's the moment. The face changing. Because a big Tannoy isn't a cheap impulse buy. The Prestige models run into the thousands, the tens of thousands at the very top. When you spend that kind of money, you're not just buying a sound. You're buying a story. And if the story starts to wobble, the whole thing wobbles.

So. Reason number one, ownership and where things are made. That explains some of the sellers. But it doesn't explain all of them. Because a lot of the people dumping Tannoys right now aren't selling new ones. They're selling ones they bought years ago, some built in Scotland, some even before the takeover. So why would they get rid of those?

That's reason number two. And it's about the size of British homes.

Let's be blunt. The classic Tannoy, the one people dream about, is huge. A Westminster Royal is a piece of furniture. A Canterbury is the size of a fridge-freezer. Even the more modest Prestige models, the Stirling, the Turnberry, the Cheviot from the Legacy range, are proper big boxes. They're designed around big drivers, ten, twelve, fifteen inches, sitting in serious cabinets with horn-loading or big ported enclosures. They're designed to move air.

And here's the thing. They need room to do it. Not just floor space. They need distance from the walls, distance from the listener, a room that lets the bass breathe without turning into a muddy boom.

Now think about where a lot of British people live. A two-up two-down. A flat in a converted Victorian house. A new-build where the living room is open-plan and doubles as a kitchen and an office and a place to dry the washing. The average British living room has been getting smaller for decades. Lots of us are in rooms where a pair of Canterburys would be like parking a Range Rover in a garden shed.

Malcolm, our bench tech, sees this constantly. "Half the Tannoys I get asked to collect," he says, "it's because someone's moving house. They had a big Edwardian semi, kids have gone, they're downsizing to a flat, and the speakers just won't go. Or they will, but the wife says they won't." He laughs, but he's not really joking.

And this has a knock-on effect on the used market that I didn't fully appreciate until I started looking. Because when lots of people are downsizing at once, lots of big speakers hit the market at once. And the pool of buyers with the space for them is shrinking.

Jo in Bristol sees the other side. "I've had customers come in with photos of their Tannoys on their phone," she says, "asking if I'll take them in part exchange. And honestly, sometimes I have to say no. Not because they're not good. Because I know I'll have them in the shop for six months. I haven't got the space either."

Think about that. Even the shops haven't got the space.

So reason two is physical. Big speakers, small rooms, a shrinking pool of buyers. And a falling price that makes some owners panic and sell before it falls further, which of course makes it fall further. It's a little spiral.

But I promised you a third reason, and I said it was the one that surprised me. Here it is.

The third reason people are dumping Tannoy is that the range itself has become confusing. And confusion, in hi-fi, is poison.

Let me explain. For years, Tannoy had a clear hierarchy in people's minds. At the top, the Prestige range. The big, hand-finished, dual concentric speakers in walnut cabinets with the gold trim. These were the heritage pieces. Below them, ranges aimed at more ordinary budgets, the Mercury, the Revolution, the Eclipse, the sort of thing you'd find on a shelf in a high street retailer. Sensible speakers. Perfectly fine. But not the dream.

And here's the issue. A lot of buyers, especially newer ones, didn't really know the difference. They saw the name Tannoy, remembered their dad's old monitors or a studio they'd been in, and assumed every Tannoy had the magic. Then they got a pair of the cheaper floorstanders home, many of which didn't use the dual concentric at all, and they thought, hang on. This is nice. But it's just a speaker. Where's the thing everyone talks about?

Jo puts it beautifully. "The name was doing too much work," she says. "It was carrying products that couldn't live up to it."

That's what brand dilution looks like in real life. It's not a dramatic collapse. It's thousands of small disappointments, spread across years, each one chipping a bit off the reputation. And each disappointed owner becomes a seller.

Now combine all three reasons. The ownership story wobbles. The big ones won't fit in modern rooms. And the cheaper ones don't deliver the thing the name promises. Put them together and you get exactly what we're seeing. Owners deciding, one by one, that it's time to let go.

But here's the twist, and this is the part I want you to really pay attention to if you own a pair, or if you've ever fancied owning a pair.

Not all Tannoys are equal on the used market. Far from it. And while some models are falling, others are holding up remarkably well, and a few are quietly becoming collectable.

Let's go through it.

First, the vintage stuff. The old Monitor Silvers, Reds and Golds, the drivers in the Lancaster and the Chatsworth cabinets, the classic Arden and Berkeley from the late seventies. These have a following that never went away. A lot of it is driven by collectors overseas, in Japan especially, where British dual concentrics have a near-legendary status. Malcolm tells me that when a properly original pair comes through his workshop, with the drivers untouched and the cabinets in decent nick, there's usually someone waiting for them. "Those don't go cheap," he says. "And they don't hang about."

Then there's the question of the Scottish-built Prestige models. Here, condition and provenance matter enormously. A pair with documentation, original boxes, a known history, built while the Coatbridge operation was in full swing, tends to attract a different kind of buyer from a pair with no story at all. I'm not going to tell you that's a guaranteed premium, because the market doesn't work like that, but the people who care about this really, really care.

And then there's the stuff in the middle. Mid-range floorstanders from the more affordable lines, especially ones that didn't use the dual concentric. Those are the ones struggling most. Not because they're bad speakers, but because there are so many good alternatives at that price, and a buyer looking at a used pair for a few hundred quid will often go for something smaller, newer, or simply with a stronger story.

Let me tell you about a collector. Call him Derek. Retired, lives in Shropshire, has a room in his bungalow that his family call the shrine. Derek owns three pairs of Tannoys at any given moment, and he's been buying and selling them since the eighties.

Derek told me something that stuck with me. "When everyone's selling," he says, "that's when I'm looking. But I'm only looking for one thing. The concentric. If it hasn't got the concentric, I don't care what the badge says."

That's the lesson in a sentence. The badge isn't the magic. The driver is.

Right. What should you do?

Let's split this up, because the answer depends entirely on which owner you are.

If you own a vintage pair, the old Monitor Golds, the Arden, the Berkeley, anything with a proper old dual concentric in it, my honest advice is: don't panic. Don't let a wave of other people selling push you into dumping them cheap. These are the ones with the strongest following and the most durable value. If they need work, get them looked at by someone who knows Tannoys specifically. The surrounds, the crossover components, the cabinet itself. A good bench tech can transform a tired pair. Malcolm's line on this is: "Restore, don't replace. Original parts where you can, sympathetic parts where you can't, and for God's sake don't let anyone drill holes in the cabinet."

If you own a Scottish-built Prestige pair, look after the paperwork. Find the receipts, find the boxes, photograph the serial plates. If you ever do sell, provenance is your friend. And think hard before selling into a falling market. If you've got the room and you love them, you'll never find something quite like them again for the same money.

If you own a newer Prestige pair, made after the takeover, here's the honest truth. Lots of owners say they sound wonderful. The dual concentric principle is still there, the cabinets are still serious, and there's no shame in owning them. The question is resale. If you bought them to keep, keep them and enjoy them. If you bought them partly as an investment, I'd temper your expectations.

And if you own a pair from the cheaper lines, especially non-concentric floorstanders, and you're not in love with them, this is probably a fine time to sell, or to trade them in towards something that suits your room better. Don't expect a fortune. Sell them to someone who'll enjoy them and move on.

Now, what if you're a buyer? What if you've always wanted a pair of Tannoys and you're watching this thinking, hang on, if everyone's selling, is this my chance?

Possibly, yes. But go in with your eyes open.

One. Measure your room. Properly. Not just the floor, the distances. Where will they sit? How far from the back wall? How far from you? A big Tannoy jammed into a corner of a small room will boom and bloat, and you'll end up hating the thing you dreamed about. Jo in Bristol says she's talked more people out of big Tannoys than into them, purely on room size. "I'd rather they buy the right speaker from me than the wrong one from anyone," she says.

Two. Check for the concentric. If the dual concentric is what you want, and it should be, make sure the model you're buying actually has one. Don't trust the badge.

Three. Listen before you buy. Tannoys have a character. Big, open, very dynamic, often very efficient, which means they go loud with very little power. Some people adore that. Some people find it a bit forward, a bit much. Better to find out in someone's front room than after you've hired a van.

Four. Budget for a check-up. Any used speaker that's more than a couple of decades old may need attention. Surrounds, crossovers, connections. Factor in a visit to a bench tech, and you'll sleep better.

So let's go back to the man in Leeds. The one with the speakers under the dust sheet.

I said he'd learned something a few weeks ago that made him decide to sell. Here's what it was. He'd always assumed that his pair had been built in Scotland. That's what he'd told his mates. That's part of what he'd paid for. And then he looked up the serial number, and the date, and he realised he couldn't be sure. The story he'd been telling himself, the story that made those speakers special, had a question mark over it.

And you know what? The speakers sounded exactly the same the day after he found out as they did the day before. Not a single note changed.

That, really, is the shocking reason. It's not that Tannoy has suddenly gone bad. It's that, for a lot of British listeners, the name was never really about the sound alone. It was about the story. A British company, a Scottish factory, a clever bit of post-war engineering, a sound that filled cathedrals and studios and your dad's front room. When parts of that story changed, when the factory got smaller, when the cheaper ranges diluted the name, when the rooms we live in got too small for the cabinets, people started to look at their speakers differently. And some of them decided the story wasn't worth the space any more.

But here's what I'd say to you, if you're sitting there with a pair of Tannoys tonight, wondering whether to join the sellers.

Turn them on. Put on something you love. A voice you know. A record you've played a hundred times. Sit in the middle, close your eyes, and just listen.

If the singer's standing there in the room with you, if you feel that solid, single-point image that made people fall in love with this brand in the first place, then forget about the story for a minute. You've got the thing that mattered. Keep it.

And if you don't hear it, if you've been holding on because of the badge and not because of the sound, then maybe it's time. Pass them on to someone like Derek, who's waiting with a measuring tape and a cleared corner. They'll love them. And you'll have the space, and the money, for something that suits the life you're actually living now.
