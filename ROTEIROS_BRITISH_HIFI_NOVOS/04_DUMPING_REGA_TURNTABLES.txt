Let's start where Rega started, because you can't understand why people are leaving unless you understand why they arrived in the first place.

Rega was founded in 1973 in Essex, and the name everyone associates with it is Roy Gandy. For decades the company has been based around Southend, and it has always had this slightly stubborn, very British personality. No glossy marble plinths. No gold-plated nonsense. Just a light, rigid platter, a simple belt drive, and a tonearm that, frankly, became one of the most copied designs in the history of hi-fi. The idea was always the same: get rid of anything that doesn't need to be there. Low mass, high rigidity, keep it simple, keep it affordable.

And it worked. The Planar 3, and its predecessors, became the turntable every British student, every first-time buyer, every bloke with a decent record collection and a modest budget aspired to. Go into any hi-fi shop from Glasgow to Plymouth in the late eighties or the nineties and you'd find a Rega on demo. It was the default. It was the safe recommendation. If you asked a salesman what to buy and he didn't say Rega, you'd wonder what he was getting a commission on.

Then vinyl died. Or at least, it went to sleep. CDs took over, then MP3s, then streaming. And for a while, Rega was one of the companies that just kept going, kept making turntables for the faithful while the rest of the world threw their records in the loft.

And then came the revival. From around 2008 onwards, vinyl came roaring back. Record Store Day, Urban Outfitters selling suitcase players, teenagers buying Taylor Swift on coloured vinyl. And who was perfectly placed to catch all those people when they realised a suitcase player sounds like a biscuit tin? Rega. The Planar 1 became the gateway drug. The Planar 3 became the "proper" deck. Sales went up and up.

So when I tell you people are now dumping them in their thousands, you'd think something must have gone badly wrong. And that's what a lot of comment sections online seem to assume. You'll see people saying Rega quality has slipped. You'll see people saying belt drive is outdated. You'll see people saying the arm is overrated.

Let me be really clear right now: from everything I've seen, heard and been told by people who repair these things for a living, that is not the main reason. Not even close. But there is a reason. Actually, there are several, and they add up to something much bigger than one brand.

Up in Leeds there's a bench technician I'll call Martin. Martin has been servicing turntables for about thirty years. Lenco, Thorens, Linn, Technics, Rega, the lot.

And Martin says the Regas that come across his bench now fall into two very different groups.

The first group, he says, are the old ones. The Planar 2s and Planar 3s from the seventies and eighties. People bring them in because they've inherited them, or found them in a parent's house, and they want them working again. Usually it's a new belt, maybe a stylus, maybe a bit of a clean on the motor. And then, he says, nine times out of ten, the owner sells it. Not because it's bad. Because they don't actually want a turntable. They want the money, or they want the space, or they think it's worth a fortune because it's "vintage". That's a big chunk of the Regas flooding onto eBay and Facebook Marketplace right now: inherited decks being cashed in.

The second group is the interesting one. The second group is people who bought new. People who walked into a shop in, say, 2019 or 2020 or 2021, during the lockdown boom, and spent three, four, five hundred pounds on a Planar 1, or seven, eight hundred, more, on a Planar 3. And now, a few years later, they're letting go.

And Martin says when he asks them why, he hears the same handful of answers again and again. I want to go through them one by one, because each one is a little piece of the shocking reason.

The first answer is money. And not the money for the turntable. The money for the records.

Do you remember when a new LP was twelve, thirteen, fifteen quid? Go and look now. A standard new release on vinyl is regularly north of thirty pounds. A deluxe edition, a double album, a coloured variant, you can easily be looking at forty pounds or more. Reissues of classic albums, records your dad bought for a fiver in 1979, are sitting on the shelves in HMV at thirty-odd pounds.

Now think about the person who bought a Planar 1 during lockdown. They were furloughed, maybe. They had a bit of spare cash, nowhere to spend it, and they fell in love with the ritual. Sliding the record out, putting the needle down, sitting with an album from start to finish. Lovely. But then life came back. Energy bills went through the roof. Mortgage rates went up. Food went up. And suddenly that thirty-five-pound record on a Saturday afternoon doesn't feel like a treat any more. It feels like a luxury you can't justify.

So they stop buying. And a turntable with no new records is a piece of furniture. And a piece of furniture worth a few hundred pounds on the second-hand market is very easy to sell.

That's the first piece. Hold onto it.

The second answer, and this one makes Martin genuinely angry, is pressing quality.

Here's the thing nobody tells you when you get into vinyl. The turntable is only half the story. If the record itself is warped, off-centre, noisy, full of clicks and pops straight out of the shrink-wrap, it doesn't matter whether you're playing it on a Planar 1 or a fifty-thousand-pound deck. It's going to sound bad.

And during the vinyl boom, the pressing plants were overwhelmed. Demand went through the roof, and there just weren't enough presses in the world. Lead times stretched to months. And quality control, according to a lot of people who buy a lot of records, suffered. You'll hear collectors talk about dished records, warped records, records with non-fill, which is when the vinyl doesn't properly fill the grooves and you get this horrible gritty, swishing sound.

Now, imagine you're new to this. You've spent your money on a nice British turntable. You buy a brand-new record for thirty-two pounds. You put it on, and it crackles. It wobbles. The pitch wows on the first track because the record isn't flat. What do you blame?

You blame the turntable. Of course you do. You don't know any better. You think, I spent all this money and it sounds worse than Spotify. And a few of those experiences later, the Rega goes on Marketplace with the description "excellent condition, hardly used".

Martin told me about a young woman, I'll call her Priya, who brought in her Planar 3 convinced it was faulty. Every record she played had this rhythmic swish. He checked the speed, checked the belt, checked the arm, checked the cartridge alignment. All perfect. Then he looked at her records. Four of the six she'd brought in were visibly warped. She'd been keeping them stacked flat on top of a radiator shelf. The deck was fine. The records weren't. But by the time he'd explained it, she'd already decided vinyl wasn't for her.

The third answer is that people are going back. Back to CD, and back to streaming. And this is the part that surprised me most.

For years, the CD was the most uncool object on Earth. Charity shops couldn't give them away. But something funny has happened. Second-hand CDs are cheap. Like, really cheap. You can walk into a charity shop in Sheffield or Norwich and buy a stack of classic albums for a pound or two each. And a CD, played on a decent player through a decent amp, sounds clean, consistent, no clicks, no warps, no faff.

Meanwhile, streaming has got better. Lossless and high-resolution streaming is now part of mainstream services. You can sit on the sofa with your phone and play almost any album ever recorded, in quality that, to be honest, most people can't distinguish from a well-pressed record on a mid-priced turntable.

The fourth group are people who are dumping their Rega because they love Rega.

I know. Sounds daft. Let me explain.

There's a condition in hi-fi that everyone in the hobby knows about. It's called upgrade-itis. And it goes like this. You buy a Planar 1. It's brilliant. It's the best thing you've ever heard. For about six months. Then you start reading forums. You start watching reviews. You start hearing words like "transparency" and "soundstage" and "timing". And you start wondering what a Planar 3 would sound like. So you sell the Planar 1 and buy a Planar 3.

And it's lovely. For about a year. And then you hear about the Planar 6. Or the Planar 8. Or the Planar 10. And you hear people whispering, almost reverently, about the Naia, Rega's flagship, a turntable with a price tag that runs into the tens of thousands of pounds, a deck that represents everything the company knows about low mass and rigidity taken to an absolutely extreme degree.

Now, very few people actually buy a Naia. But the existence of the Naia does something to the whole range. It's like a ladder. Every rung has a reason to step up. And every time somebody steps up, the old deck goes on the second-hand market.

Dave, back in Bristol, put it perfectly. He said, "Half the Regas I take in part-exchange, the customer walks out with another Rega. They're not dumping the brand. They're climbing it."

And this is why the numbers look so dramatic. If you search the second-hand listings, you will see Rega turntables everywhere. But a big chunk of those aren't refugees. They're people moving up the ladder. The flood of used Regas isn't a sign that the brand is failing. In a strange way, it's a sign of how many people bought into it in the first place.

But hang on, because there's a twist inside the twist. Because not everyone climbing the ladder stays on it.

Some people, when they get the upgrade itch, look sideways. They look at direct-drive decks, the kind DJs have used for decades, because direct drive has had a huge resurgence among hi-fi buyers. They look at decks from other British makers. They look at exotic stuff from Germany or Japan. And part of what drives them away is a set of myths about Rega that have been floating around for years. So let's tackle those, because I think they're genuinely costing people money.

Myth number one: belt drive is old-fashioned and unstable.

You'll hear this a lot from people who've just bought a direct-drive turntable and want to justify it. The argument is that a belt stretches, it slips, the speed isn't as accurate. Now, there's a grain of truth in it. A worn belt absolutely will cause speed problems. A belt that's been sitting for twenty years in a hot loft will be useless. But a belt in good condition on a well-designed deck is not some fragile compromise. Belt drive exists for a reason: it isolates the platter from motor vibration. Plenty of very expensive, very highly regarded turntables use belts. Martin's view is simple. "If your Rega sounds wobbly, change the belt. It costs less than a takeaway. Don't sell the deck."

Myth number two: you have to upgrade the arm.

There's a whole cottage industry of modifications for Rega arms. Counterweights, rewiring, bearings, all sorts. Some of it is perfectly sensible. Some of it, according to the people who do this professionally, is snake oil wrapped in machined aluminium. The Rega arm design has been used, licensed and imitated across the industry for decades because it's very good at what it does. If you've got a Planar 3 and you're thinking of selling it because someone on a forum told you the arm is holding it back, my honest suggestion is to try a better cartridge first. In a lot of systems, the cartridge makes a far bigger difference than people expect.

Myth number three: you need a fancy platform, clamp, mat and isolation feet before it sounds right.

Rega has historically been quite blunt about this: their decks are designed to sit on a light, rigid surface. Not a giant heavy slab. And a lot of the accessories people pile on are, at best, a matter of personal taste. Graham, the collector, laughed when I mentioned this. "I've had people come round with a bag of accessories worth more than the deck. And then they wonder why it doesn't sound like it did in the shop."

People are dumping Rega turntables because records have become eye-wateringly expensive. Because a lot of new pressings are, frankly, not good enough, and beginners blame their decks. Because upgrade-itis pushes people up the ladder, and every step leaves a used Rega behind. And because a handful of myths make people think their perfectly good turntable is holding them back.

And here's the shocking reason. The thing tying it all together.

Most of the people dumping their Regas aren't dumping them because Rega got worse. They're dumping them because vinyl itself has changed. It's not a story about a British company failing. It's a story about a British company being so successful that it's become the visible symptom of something much bigger. When the tide goes out, you see whose boat was the biggest on the beach.
