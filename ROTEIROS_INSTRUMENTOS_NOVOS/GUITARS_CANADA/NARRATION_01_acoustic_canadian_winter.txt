An acoustic guitar is basically a box made of thin slices of wood. The top on a typical steel-string is somewhere around two and a half to three millimetres thick. Wood is hygroscopic. That's a fancy way of saying it trades moisture with the air around it. When the air is humid, the wood takes on water and swells. When the air is dry, the wood gives up water and shrinks. And critically, wood moves much more across the grain than along it. A spruce top shrinks side to side, across its width, far more than it shrinks from neck to tail.

Now picture that top glued to a set of braces that run across it, and glued all the way around its edges to the sides. When it tries to shrink and it can't, the stress has to go somewhere. Eventually, the wood lets go along the grain. That's your crack.

Guitar factories control this carefully. Most major acoustic makers build in climate-controlled rooms, typically somewhere in the range of forty-five to fifty-five percent relative humidity. That's the zone you'll hear almost every luthier recommend for storing an acoustic. Your guitar was born in that zone. It was glued, fitted and set up in that zone.
