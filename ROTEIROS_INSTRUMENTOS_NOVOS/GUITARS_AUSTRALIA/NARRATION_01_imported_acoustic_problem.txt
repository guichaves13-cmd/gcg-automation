So let's start with where your guitar actually comes from.

If you buy a new acoustic in Australia today, the odds are very good it was built somewhere in Asia. That's not an insult. Some of the best value instruments ever made come out of factories in China, Indonesia, Vietnam and Taiwan. Yamaha, a company founded in Hamamatsu, Japan, back in 1887, builds a big chunk of its acoustic range in factories in China and Indonesia. Cort, the Korean company, has long run large production in Indonesia and has built guitars for plenty of other brands over the years. Epiphone, owned by Gibson, has its acoustics made in Asia. Fender's entry and mid-level acoustics are built overseas too. Even companies famous for American-made guitars run factories outside the US. Taylor builds in El Cajon, California, but also in Tecate, just over the border in Mexico. Martin builds in Nazareth, Pennsylvania, and also in Navojoa, Mexico.

Now compare that with the guitars actually built here. Maton has been making guitars in Melbourne since 1946, founded by Bill May, and its factory is in Box Hill in the city's east. Cole Clark started in Melbourne in the early two thousands and builds in the city's outer east. Those are Australian guitars, built from wood that has spent its working life in Australian air. Hold that thought, because it's going to matter a lot more than you think.

Here's the first clue. When a tech in Brisbane and a tech in Adelaide describe the same imported guitar, they often describe it differently. The Brisbane guitar might feel a bit puffy, a bit sluggish, the action creeping up in summer. The Adelaide guitar has sharp fret ends, a dip near the soundhole, maybe a hairline crack beside the fingerboard. Same model, same factory, same price. Different city, different symptoms. And yet, every tech will tell you, it's the same underlying problem.

So what is it? Let's look at the first symptom up close, because it's the one you can feel in your hands right now.

Those sharp metal edges on the side of the neck have a name in the trade. It's called fret sprout. A fret is a strip of nickel silver, or sometimes stainless steel, pressed into a slot in the fingerboard. When the guitar is built, the fret ends are trimmed and filed flush with the edge of the board, so the side of the neck feels smooth. Then the guitar leaves the factory. And over the following months, the wood of the fingerboard shrinks across its width. Wood shrinks a lot across the grain and barely at all along it. So the fingerboard gets a tiny bit narrower, but the metal frets don't. The ends of the frets end up poking out past the wood. You run your hand up the neck and it feels like a little saw.

The common fingerboard woods on budget and mid-priced imports, things like rosewood, ovangkol, laurel, walnut, and various engineered or roasted woods, all move with moisture. Ebony does too. It's not about one timber being bad. It's about what happens to any timber when it moves from one environment into a very different one.

And that's your first hint about the one problem. But fret sprout is just the bit you can feel. The part that actually costs money is hiding further up the neck, at a tiny piece of bone or plastic most players never look at.

Right. The nut.

The nut is that little white strip at the top of the fingerboard, where the strings cross over from the headstock onto the neck. On a lot of factory guitars it's plastic, or a synthetic material. On better guitars it's bone, or sometimes a material like TUSQ, made by Graph Tech. Each string sits in a slot cut into the nut. And the depth of those slots is one of the most important measurements on the whole instrument, because it decides how hard you have to press to play a note in the first few frets.

Here's where the factory comes in. On a production line, nut slots are often cut on the high side. There's a sensible reason. A slot that's too deep makes the open string buzz, and you can't fix that easily without replacing the nut. A slot that's a bit high just makes the guitar a little harder to play near the headstock. So when you're building thousands of guitars, many of which will sit in shipping containers and warehouses before anyone touches them, high is the safe choice. High means fewer returns for buzzing. The final adjustment is supposed to happen at the shop, in a setup, before the guitar is sold.

And this is a big part of what techs keep seeing. A guitar comes in that feels stiff and tiring to play, and the player assumes it's a cheap guitar, or that they're a weak player. Then the tech files the nut slots down a fraction, maybe the thickness of a sheet of paper or two, and suddenly that six-hundred-dollar guitar plays like something that cost twice as much. Barre chords at the first fret stop hurting. Intonation in the open position improves, because you're not pushing the string sharp every time you press it down.

But why are those slots still high when the guitar reaches the customer? That's the uncomfortable part. Plenty of guitar retailers in Australia do excellent setups. But a lot of guitars, especially ones bought online and shipped straight from a warehouse, never get a proper setup at all. They're played exactly as they left the factory, which might have been a city like Guangzhou, or a factory in Java, or anywhere in between, many weeks and thousands of kilometres earlier.

And the nut is only half of it. There's another adjustment that changes with the seasons, and it's the reason your guitar can feel great in July and awful in January. That's the truss rod and the neck relief.

Inside nearly every steel-string acoustic neck there's an adjustable metal rod called a truss rod. Gibson patented an adjustable truss rod in the early nineteen twenties, and in one form or another it's been in steel-string guitars ever since. Its job is to counteract the pull of the strings, which on a typical set of light acoustic strings adds up to well over fifty kilograms of tension pulling the neck forward. The rod lets a tech set a tiny amount of forward bow in the neck, called relief, so the strings can vibrate without hitting the frets.

Now, here's what techs see on imported guitars over and over. When the wood dries out, the neck and fingerboard can change shape slightly, and the relief changes. When the air gets humid, the wood swells and the relief changes again, often the other way. A guitar that was adjusted perfectly in a humid factory can end up with too little relief in one season and too much in another, once it's living somewhere with very different air. Buzzing in winter, high action in summer, or the reverse. Players blame the strings, the pick, their technique. The real cause is the wood quietly adjusting itself to a new home.

So we've got three symptoms now. Sprouting frets. High, uncomfortable nut slots that were never corrected. And a neck that won't stay put through the seasons. They all point the same way. But the biggest symptom, the one that sends guitars to the bin, is still coming, and it lives in the body of the guitar.

Let's talk about the top.

The top of an acoustic, the soundboard, is a thin plate of wood, usually spruce or cedar, sometimes mahogany or koa or, on cheaper models, laminate. On a solid-top guitar it might be under three millimetres thick. It's braced underneath with thin strips of wood in a pattern, often the X-bracing pattern associated with Martin, which has been around since the eighteen hundreds. That top has to resist the strings trying to rotate the bridge forward, every second of every day, for decades.

Solid wood tops sound wonderful, and they're one of the main things you're paying for as you move up from entry level. But a solid top is also the part of the guitar most sensitive to moisture. When a solid top dries out quickly, it shrinks. If it shrinks faster than the frame around it allows, it can crack. Those cracks often appear right beside the fingerboard extension, or running from the bridge towards the tail of the guitar, along the grain. Techs see this constantly in dry places and dry seasons, and in homes with heating or air conditioning running all day.

Go the other way, into heavy humidity, and the top can swell. Under string tension a swollen top tends to bulge up behind the bridge and sink a little in front of it. That rotation changes the geometry of the whole guitar. The strings sit higher above the frets, the action climbs, and the guitar gets harder to play every month. Techs call this a belly behind the bridge, and while a little movement is normal, a lot of it is a sign the guitar has been living in conditions it wasn't prepared for.

And this is where Australia makes things worse than almost anywhere else.

Think about the range of climates a single model might have to survive here. Darwin in the wet season is tropical, hot and heavy with moisture. Brisbane summers are humid and sticky. Then you go inland to Alice Springs, or out to Kalgoorlie, and the air is bone dry for much of the year. Perth has long dry summers. Melbourne can swing from a dry northerly blasting in from the interior to a cool damp southerly change in the same afternoon. Hobart is cool and often damp. Canberra has cold, dry winters where people run heaters for months.

A guitar is a box made of thin wood held together with glue and string tension. It doesn't care what badge is on the headstock. It cares what the air around it is doing. And an Australian guitar may have to deal with more variety than a guitar sold almost anywhere in Europe.

Which brings us to the reveal I promised you at the start. Let me lay it out cleanly.

The one problem repair techs keep seeing in imported acoustics isn't a bad factory, or a bad brand, or cheap wood. It's this. The guitar was built and settled in one climate, and it's being played in a completely different one, and nobody adjusted it for the move.

That's it. That's the root. The fret sprout is the fingerboard shrinking into a drier home. The high nut slots and the bad setup are the factory's safe defaults that were meant to be corrected for local conditions and never were. The truss rod drifting season to season is the neck renegotiating with Australian weather. The cracked top in Canberra and the bellied top in Cairns are the same wood reacting to two different extremes.

Wood has something called equilibrium moisture content. Over time, a piece of timber takes on a moisture level that matches the air around it. Good guitar factories control the humidity in their wood stores and building rooms, so the wood is stable when it's glued up. Many large factories do exactly that. But the target they choose suits their region and their biggest markets, and after the guitar leaves, it might spend weeks in a shipping container crossing the equator, sit in a warehouse in western Sydney through a hot dry summer, and then go home to a flat in Darwin. Every one of those steps shifts the wood a little. And the guitar arrives in your hands still carrying the setup it was given back in the factory.

Here's the part almost no one in a guitar shop will say out loud. A guitar built in Box Hill, by Maton, or in Melbourne's east by Cole Clark, has spent its build life in Australian air. That doesn't make it immune, and those guitars still need setups and still crack if you leave them in a hot car. But it's one of the reasons local players often talk about Australian-built guitars feeling settled and stable here. They were born in the conditions they live in.

Now, I'm not telling you imported guitars are bad. Far from it. A Yamaha FG or FS model, or one of the lower-priced Taylor or Martin Mexican-built models, or a solid-top guitar from Cort or Eastman, can be a seriously good instrument. Eastman, by the way, builds in China and has built a strong reputation among players and repairers for its solid-wood instruments. The point isn't where it was made. The point is that a guitar from anywhere needs to be adjusted for where it actually lives. And a huge number of them never are.

Now let's get practical. Here's the five-minute home check I promised. You'll need a capo, a plastic card like an old gift card or a credit card, a good light, and ideally a cheap digital hygrometer, which you can find for well under twenty dollars at most hardware or electronics stores.

Step one. Fret ends. Close your eyes and slowly slide your fretting hand up and down the edge of the fingerboard, on both sides, from the first fret to the body. If you feel little sharp points catching your skin, you've got fret sprout. That tells you the fingerboard has dried and shrunk since the guitar was built. It's not a disaster. A tech can file and dress the fret ends, and it's usually one of the more affordable bench jobs. But it tells you the guitar is drier now than it was in the factory, and that's useful information for everything that follows.

Step two. The nut. Put your capo on at the third fret. Now look at the gap between the bottom of each string and the top of the first fret. You want a very small gap, just enough to slide a thin piece of paper under the string and see it move. If you can see daylight clearly under every string at the first fret with the capo on the third, the nut slots are probably high. Another way to feel it: play a basic F barre chord at the first fret, then play the same shape up at the third fret. If the first-fret chord feels dramatically harder and sounds sharper than the one higher up, the nut is a likely suspect. That's a classic factory setup that was never corrected.

Step three. Neck relief. Hold down the low E string at the first fret with one hand, and at the fret where the neck meets the body with the other hand, usually the fourteenth fret on a steel-string acoustic. Now look at the gap between the string and the top of the frets around the seventh or eighth fret. With a normal setup, there's a tiny gap there, around the thickness of a business card. If the string is touching the frets in the middle, the neck is too straight or even bowed backwards, and you'll get buzzing. If the gap looks big, like you could slide a coin through it, there's too much relief and your action will feel high. Either way, that's a truss rod adjustment, which is quick for a tech. And unless you know exactly what you're doing, that's the one I'd leave to them.

Step four. The top. Lay the guitar flat, under a light, at eye level, and look across the top from the side, towards the bridge. Behind the bridge, between the bridge and the tail end of the guitar, you'll often see a slight rise. A small amount is normal for a guitar under tension. But if it looks like a hill, and in front of the bridge the top dips down towards the soundhole, the top has rotated. Then check the edges of the fingerboard extension where it sits on the top, and run your eyes along the grain lines of the top near the bridge, looking for fine dark lines that don't follow the pattern of the wood. That's how hairline cracks usually show up. Catch a crack early and a repair is much simpler than when it's been left to grow.

Step five. The action at the twelfth fret. Take a ruler with millimetre markings and measure the gap between the bottom of the low E string and the top of the twelfth fret. Plenty of techs aim for somewhere around two and a half millimetres on the bass side and a bit under two on the treble side for a comfortable steel-string acoustic, depending on how hard you play. If you're well over three millimetres on the low E, the guitar is going to fight you. Now, here's the important part, which leads straight into the bonus I promised.

Look down at the bridge and find the saddle, the white strip the strings sit on. Check how much of it is sticking up above the bridge. This is the bonus check, the one most players never do, and it's the one techs use to decide whether a guitar is worth saving.

The usual first fix for high action on an acoustic is to lower the saddle by sanding material off its base. Simple, cheap, quick. But you can only do that if there's enough saddle sticking up above the bridge to remove. If your action is high, and there's already very little saddle showing, just a sliver above the wood, there's nowhere left to go. And if somebody has already shaved the bridge itself down to get the action lower, that's another warning sign. At that point, the proper fix for a top that's rotated, or a neck angle that's changed, is often a neck reset, where the neck is removed and refitted at a new angle. On a lot of guitars that's one of the bigger jobs a tech does, and it's priced accordingly, sometimes running into the hundreds of dollars.

So here's the rule of thumb that techs use, and you can use it too. If your guitar has high action, plenty of saddle showing, and no cracks, it's almost always worth a setup. Fret ends dressed, nut slots lowered, truss rod adjusted, saddle lowered. On a decent imported guitar, that setup can be the best money you ever spend on it. If your guitar has high action, almost no saddle left, a big belly behind the bridge, and a cheap laminate build that cost two hundred dollars, you're often looking at a repair that costs more than the guitar. That's the moment to put the money towards something better.

And one more thing on the bonus. Remember that hygrometer? Put it in the room where your guitar lives, or in the case with it, and watch it for a week. Many repairers suggest keeping acoustic guitars somewhere roughly in the forty to fifty-five percent relative humidity range. If you're in Canberra in July with the heater going and the hygrometer is showing thirty percent, that's exactly the kind of dry environment that causes sprouting frets and cracked tops. A soundhole or case humidifier, the kind made by companies like D'Addario or Planet Waves, or Boveda packs, can help. If you're in Darwin in the build-up and it's sitting up in the seventies or eighties, the opposite problem applies. Air conditioning, a dehumidifier in the room, or keeping the guitar in its case with moisture-absorbing packs can all help bring it back towards a steady level.

Here's the big takeaway. Stability matters as much as the number itself. Guitars hate big, fast swings. The thing that kills a lot of tops in this country isn't one hot day. It's going from a humid lounge room straight into a car boot on a forty-degree afternoon, or from a cool, damp gig in Hobart to a dry hotel room with the heater cranked. Keep the guitar in its case when you're travelling. Never leave it in a parked car in summer. Let it sit in its case for a bit before you open it after a big temperature change. Simple habits, and they're the habits techs wish every customer had.
