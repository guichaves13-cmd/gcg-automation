Now let's talk about the fingerboard. Most Gibson electrics use rosewood fingerboards, and higher-end models like the Les Paul Custom have traditionally used ebony. Gibson has, at various points over the years, also used other woods, like richlite on certain models, and baked maple for a period. But rosewood is the classic Gibson fingerboard.

Rosewood is a natural material. It takes on moisture and gives it up depending on the air around it. In a humid summer in Tennessee or Florida, the wood swells a little. In a dry winter in Minnesota or Colorado, with the heat running indoors, the wood gives up moisture and shrinks. The frets, though, are metal. They don't shrink. So when the fingerboard dries out, it gets slightly narrower, and the ends of the frets start sticking out past the edge of the neck. Techs call this fret sprout. You feel it when you slide your hand up the neck and it catches on sharp little metal edges.

Now here's a detail that's very specific to Gibson. On many Gibson models with bound fingerboards, the frets don't run over the binding. Instead, the binding is left a little higher at the end of each fret, forming what people call binding nibs. It's a beautiful, traditional detail, and it takes skill to do. But when the wood shrinks and the binding ages, those nibs can crack, chip, or separate. And when it's time for a refret, a guitar with nibs takes more time and more skill to do correctly, which often means a higher bill at the shop. A full refret on a bound Gibson neck can easily run into the hundreds of dollars depending on where you live.

The fix for most dry-board problems is preventive. Keep the guitar in its case in winter. Use a humidifier pack. Oil the fingerboard lightly with a proper fingerboard conditioner once or twice a year. Simple. But a lot of people don't, especially with a guitar that hangs on a wall all year.

Now, why does the nut matter so much on a Gibson in particular? Go back to that seventeen-degree headstock angle. If the nut slot isn't cut exactly right, if it's too narrow, or too rough, or the angle of the slot doesn't follow the path the string takes toward the tuner, the string gets pinched. It binds. The tension on the fingerboard side and the tension on the headstock side aren't equal anymore. Then you bend a note, or you play hard, and the string suddenly slips through the slot. Why does this happen so often on Gibsons, if it's such a well-known problem? There are a few reasons, and none of them are secrets in the repair world.

First, nut slot cutting is precise handwork. Even in a modern factory, with computer-controlled machines cutting bodies and necks, the nut is often finished by a person with specialized files. Every slot has to have the right width, the right depth, and the right angle. A slot that is perfect at the factory with one gauge of strings can also start binding when the owner switches to a heavier set. Third, the design itself. Again, the sharp break angle and the three-a-side string path put more pressure on the nut than on a straight-pull, flat-headstock design. Now, the at-home check I promised. Get your guitar and tune it carefully. You'll want a clip-on tuner or a pedal tuner. Here's what to do, step by step.

Step one. Tune your G string to pitch. Then, with one hand, press gently down on the string between the nut and the tuner, on the headstock side. Don't pull hard. Just press down a little. Then let go and check the tuner. If the note jumps, especially if it jumps sharp and stays sharp, the string was hung up in the nut slot.

Step two. Tune the G string again. Now play a note at the fifth fret, and bend it up a whole step, nice and slowly, and let it come back down. Listen carefully close to the nut. Do you hear a little ping, or a tick, as the string comes back? Then check your open G on the tuner again. If it's not where you left it, that's another sign the slot is binding.

Step three. Detune the G string down about a half step, then tune it back up to pitch slowly. Watch the tuner as you approach the note. If the needle jumps in steps instead of moving smoothly, if it sits flat and then suddenly hops sharp, the string is sticking in the slot as tension increases.

If your guitar passes all three tests, great. Your nut is doing its job. If it fails, here's the simplest first step you can do yourself. Take a regular pencil, a soft one is best, loosen the string, lift it out of the slot, and rub the pencil tip into the slot so it leaves graphite behind. Put the string back, retune, and do the three tests again. If it improves, the slot was binding. If it helps but doesn't fully fix it, take it to a qualified tech for proper nut work. Don't start filing your nut yourself unless you know what you're doing and have the right files. A nut that's cut too deep has to be replaced, and that costs more than doing it right the first time.
