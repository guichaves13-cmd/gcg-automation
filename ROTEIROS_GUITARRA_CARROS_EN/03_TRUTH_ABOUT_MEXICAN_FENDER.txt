Walk into almost any big guitar store in America and look at the Fender wall. It is usually the brightest wall in the building. Rows of Stratocasters and Telecasters, sunburst, black, sea foam green, every one of them hanging at the same angle, every one of them with a little white price tag dangling from the tuner. Now look closely at those tags. Some of them say around eight hundred dollars. Some of them say around two thousand. And the guitars attached to them, from six feet away, look almost identical.

So here is the question nobody behind the counter is in a hurry to answer. What exactly is the difference between a Mexican-made Fender and an American one? Not the marketing difference. The real one. The one you can measure with a ruler, hear through an amp, and feel when you try to sell the thing three years later.

I have spent a lot of years with these guitars on my bench. I have set them up, refretted them, rewired them, and talked a lot of customers out of bad decisions and, honestly, a few into good ones. And today I am going to walk you through the truth about Mexican Fenders the way a car channel would walk you through the truth about a dealership. The factory story. The sales tactics. What is the same, what is different, and what happens to your money after you walk out the door. And stay with me until the end, because there is one thing about which guitar the store would actually prefer you to buy, and it is probably not the one you think.

Let us start on the sales floor, because that is where most people make this decision, and that is where the information is the least neutral.

Car people know this game well. You walk onto a lot looking at the mid-level trim, and within five minutes someone is showing you the top trim. Better seats, nicer wheels, and a monthly payment that is only, quote, a little more. Guitar stores are not that different. A customer walks in looking at a Player Stratocaster made in Mexico. The salesperson is friendly, knows their stuff, and very quickly says something like, this one is great, but if you are serious, you should really try the American.

Now, I want to be fair here. Most guitar salespeople I have met genuinely love guitars, and a lot of them are working musicians. They are not villains. But they work on a floor where the bigger ticket usually looks better on the end-of-month numbers, and in a lot of stores there are commission structures, sales targets, or manufacturer promotions sitting in the background. I am not going to tell you how any specific store pays its staff, because I do not know, and it varies. But you should walk in knowing that the person handing you a guitar has incentives, the same way a car salesman has incentives. That is not a scandal. It is just retail.

And here is the line you will hear more than any other. The American one is better wood, better hardware, better everything. Hold on to that sentence, because by the end of this video we are going to take it apart piece by piece, and some of it is true, and some of it is very much not.

To understand any of this, you have to understand where these guitars actually come from. And that story starts in the eighties, at a moment when Fender was almost a dead brand.

In the early nineteen eighties, Fender was owned by CBS, and by most accounts the quality had slipped badly. Players were buying vintage pre-CBS guitars and Japanese copies instead of new Fenders. In nineteen eighty five, a group of employees led by Bill Schultz bought the company from CBS. The deal famously did not include the old Fullerton factory. So the new Fender had a name, a lot of history, and almost no American production for a while. For a period, a large share of what was sold as Fender was actually being built in Japan.

The new owners needed a way to build affordable guitars without depending entirely on Japan, where costs were rising along with the yen. And the answer was just south of the border. Fender set up operations in Ensenada, Baja California, in Mexico, around nineteen eighty seven. Over the following years, Ensenada grew into one of the most important guitar factories in the world, sitting only a few hours' drive from Fender's American factory in Corona, California.


The early Mexican Fenders, from the late eighties and the nineties, had a mixed reputation. Thinner finishes on some, cheaper electronics on others, and a lot of them had what techs used to call the Mexican setup, which was basically no setup at all. If you bought a nineties Mexican Strat, there was a good chance the first thing you did was take it to someone like me.

Quick question before we go further. If you own a Mexican Fender, what year is it? Go look at the serial number on the headstock or the neck plate and put the year in the comments. I am genuinely curious how many of you are playing nineties guitars versus newer Player series ones, because those are almost different instruments.

And then, in two thousand eight, something happened that most players have never heard of.

In early two thousand eight, there was a fire at the Ensenada factory. Reports from the time describe significant damage to the facility, and production was disrupted while the plant was rebuilt. I am not going to throw around numbers about losses, because I have not seen figures I would trust. But a fire in the middle of the factory that builds a huge portion of a company's affordable guitars is not a small event, and people who follow Fender history often point to the rebuild as a turning point. When a factory gets rebuilt, it often gets modernized. New equipment, new processes, a chance to fix what was not working.

Here is a story that sums up the change for me. A few years ago a customer, let us call him Danny from Bakersfield, brought me two Telecasters. One was a mid-nineties Mexican Tele he had bought used in college. The other was a Mexican Player Tele he had bought new for his son. He wanted me to set up the old one so it played like the new one. And when I put them side by side on the bench, the difference was not subtle. The newer guitar had cleaner fret ends, a more even finish around the neck pocket, and it was closer to playable straight out of the box. Same country, same city, same brand. Very different eras.

So when someone tells you Mexican Fenders are the cheap ones, the first question is, which Mexican Fender? Because just like with cars, the model year matters.

Now let us talk about the guitar most people are actually comparing today. The Player series.

Fender launched the Player series in two thousand eighteen as the replacement for the old Mexican Standard line, and in twenty twenty four it rolled out the Player II series. These are the Mexican guitars that hang on that bright wall at around the eight hundred to nine hundred dollar mark, depending on the model and the year. On the other side, the American guitars, currently the American Professional II line and the American Performer and Vintage lines, sit higher, often well above that.

So the store's story is simple. One is the Mexican one. One is the American one. Pay more, get more. But let us actually go through the guitar the way a mechanic goes through a car, part by part, and see where that story holds up.

Start with the wood. This is where the salesperson usually leads, and it is where the story is weakest. Both factories use the same broad families of wood for the classic models. Alder or similar woods for a lot of the bodies, maple necks, rosewood or maple or pau ferro fingerboards depending on the model and year. Fender has also used other body woods on some Player II models. Is there grading? Probably. A big company with two factories is going to send its nicest material to its most expensive product. But a Strat body is not an acoustic soundboard. The tone of a solid body electric is dominated by pickups, electronics, setup and your amp far more than by the body wood. Ask ten techs and you will get ten opinions on tonewood, but very few of them will tell you that the wood alone is worth the price gap.

Next, the neck. This is where you can feel real differences, and it is worth paying attention. The American Professional II necks have a rolled edge treatment on the fingerboard and the Deep C profile, plus narrow tall frets. The Player series uses a Modern C profile and medium jumbo frets, and on Player II, Fender also introduced rolled fingerboard edges, which used to be one of the selling points of the higher lines. That is a classic example of a feature trickling down, the same way a feature that used to be on the luxury trim of a car ends up on the base model five years later.

Then the fretwork. And here I want to be careful, because this is where I see the most variation from guitar to guitar. In my experience, American Fenders tend to be more consistent on fret leveling and fret end dressing. Not perfect, but more consistent. On Mexican guitars, I have seen beautiful fretwork, and I have seen guitars that needed a level and a crown to get the action down where the customer wanted it. That is not a scandal either. It is what happens when you build a guitar to a lower price. Less time per guitar means more variation. If you buy a Mexican Fender, the single best thing you can do is play the actual guitar you are buying, not one like it, and budget for a professional setup.

The finish. American Fenders typically use thinner finishes on certain lines, and some vintage-style models use nitrocellulose. Player series guitars use polyester or polyurethane finishes, which are thicker and tougher. Does that change the tone? People will argue about that forever. Does it change how the guitar ages? Yes. A thick poly finish will look much the same in twenty years. A thin nitro finish will check, wear and fade. Some people pay extra for that. Some people would rather their guitar not change at all.

Hardware. This is a mix. Tuners and bridges on the American guitars are generally better, and things like the two-point tremolo with bent steel saddles on the American Pro II feel a little more refined. But the Player series hardware is perfectly functional, and the Player II upgraded some parts compared to the first generation. I will be honest, when I swap tuners on a Mexican Strat, it is usually because the customer wants locking tuners, not because the stock ones are failing.

And the electronics. The American guitars get Fender's higher-end pickups, the V-Mod II sets on the American Pro II, and better pots and switches in general. The Player and Player II guitars use Alnico pickups that are, honestly, pretty good for the money. Some players love them. Others swap them out right away. Which brings us to something we will come back to later, which I call the upgrade path myth. Hold that thought.

Here is another bench story. A woman named Karen from Tulsa brought in a Player Strat because she thought something was wrong with it. She had played an American Pro II in a store and felt that hers sounded thin. We plugged both into the same amp, which is the one test almost nobody does in a store. And the truth was, the biggest difference was the pickup height on hers. The pickups were set low out of the factory. We raised them, adjusted the action, put on fresh strings, and her face changed. Was it an American Pro II? No. But the gap she heard in the store was mostly setup, not geography.

Okay. Now let us talk about the part the dealer really does not want to discuss. The resale market.

On car channels, this is always the big reveal. You buy a car, and the second you drive it off the lot, it loses a chunk of its value. Guitars work the same way, just a little gentler. And the way this plays out between Mexican and American Fenders is not what most people expect.

If you look at used listings on the big marketplaces, you will usually find used Mexican Player series guitars selling for a noticeable chunk under new price. And used American Professional guitars also sell for a lot less than new. I am not going to give you an exact percentage, because it depends on the model, the condition, the color, and the month. But when you look at the dollar amount, the American guitars typically lose more money in absolute terms, simply because they started higher. Losing a few hundred dollars on an eight hundred dollar guitar hurts. Losing several hundred more on a two thousand dollar guitar hurts more.

Now, I want to be fair to the American side. Some American models, especially limited runs, special colors, and older vintage-correct guitars, can hold their value very well, and some go up over time. There is also a segment of the used market that only wants American, and those buyers will pay for it. But if you are buying a standard production guitar as a player, not a collector, you should go in knowing that the extra money you spend on day one is not all coming back when you sell.

If you are on the buying side, a used Player Strat in good shape can be one of the best value guitars out there. Find one that has already been set up, has good frets, and you are getting most of the experience for a fraction of the new American price.

Here is a third story. A guy named Marcus from Columbus came in a while back with an American Strat he had bought new. He had saved for it, he loved it, and then life happened and he needed to sell it. He brought it to me to get it in shape for selling, and when he started looking at what people were actually paying, he was shocked. Not because it was worth nothing. Because the gap between what he had paid and what the market would pay was more than the price of a brand new Mexican Strat. He looked at me and said, so I basically paid a Player Strat for the privilege of owning it for three years. And that is not a reason to never buy an American guitar. But it is a reason to buy it with your eyes open.

Now let us deal with the myth that keeps this whole system running. The upgrade path.

The upgrade path goes like this. You start with a Squier, which by the way we covered in another video, you move up to a Mexican Fender, and one day, when you are serious, you buy the American. It is the same ladder the car industry built decades ago. Start with the economy model, move up to the mid-size, end with the luxury badge. Every step is a sale. And at every step, the store benefits.

The myth is not that the ladder exists. The myth is that you have to climb it to get a better playing guitar. Here is what a lot of techs, myself included, will tell you. A well-chosen Mexican Fender, with a professional setup, maybe a fret level if it needs it, and a pickup swap if you want a specific sound, can get you remarkably close to the experience of an American guitar for less money than the gap between them. You are not getting an American guitar. The neck will feel a little different, the finish will be different, and the name on the back of the headstock will say Mexico. But the experience of playing it, the thing that actually matters on stage or in your bedroom, can be closer than the price tag suggests.

There is a catch, and I want to be honest about it. Upgrades do not add resale value the way you think. If you spend a few hundred dollars on pickups and hardware on a Mexican Strat, the used market usually will not pay you back for those parts. So upgrade for yourself, not as an investment. Keep the original parts in a bag in the case, because some buyers want a stock guitar, and you can swap them back if you sell.

And there is a second catch. Not every Mexican Fender is a good candidate. If the neck is twisted, if the frets are badly worn, if the neck pocket is sloppy, no amount of upgrades is going to turn it into a great guitar. Start with a good one. That is the whole trick. And the only way to find a good one is to play it.

So, let us go back to that sentence from the sales floor. The American one is better wood, better hardware, better everything. Here is how I would grade it. Better hardware and electronics, mostly true. Better fretwork and consistency, generally true in my experience, but not guaranteed. Better neck details, true on some features, though Player II closed part of that gap. Better wood, the weakest part of the claim, and the part that matters least on a solid body electric. Better resale, depends what you mean, because in raw dollars the American guitar usually loses more. Better everything, no.

And now, the payoff. The thing I promised at the start. Which guitar does the dealer actually prefer you to buy?

You would think the answer is obvious. The expensive one. Bigger ticket, bigger commission, better day. And on the floor, in the moment, that is often how it feels. But if you talk to people who have worked in music retail, the answer gets more interesting, because a store does not live on the guitar alone.

In general, the margins on new guitars in music retail are known to be pretty thin compared to a lot of other products. Guitars are competitive, prices are easy to compare online, and manufacturers have pricing policies that limit how much a dealer can discount. I am not going to quote a margin figure, because they vary and I do not have numbers I would stand behind. But it is widely understood in the industry that the accessories, the cases, the cables, the strings, the pedals, the extended protection plans, and the setup services are where a lot of the healthier margins live.

So what does the dealer really want you to buy? Not just the guitar. The guitar plus everything around it. And here is the twist. When you walk out with a Mexican Fender, you have more budget left over, and the store knows that budget often ends up spent at the same counter. A Player Strat, a hard case, a strap, a tuner, a pack of strings, a couple of cables, maybe a pedal or a small amp. Add it up, and the total can look a lot like the price of the American guitar on its own. Except more of that money is sitting in the higher-margin stuff.

That is why you will sometimes see the salesperson who tried to sell you up suddenly become very happy to sell you the Mexican guitar, as long as it comes with the bundle. In the car world, it is the same thing. The dealer does not just make money on the car. They make money on the financing, the warranty, the floor mats, and the paint protection. The badge on the hood gets you in the door. The add-ons pay the bills.

So here is my honest advice, from the bench. If you want a Fender, and your budget is real, a Mexican Player or Player II guitar is a legitimate instrument, built in a Fender-owned factory with a long history, and it is not a lesser version of you as a player. Play the actual guitar before you buy it. Look at the fret ends, sight down the neck, plug it into the same amp as the American one if you can. Skip the add-ons you do not need. Put part of the money you saved into a good professional setup. And if one day you want an American Fender, buy it because you love that specific guitar, not because somebody told you it was the next step on a ladder.

And if you have the patience, look at the used market first. That is where the Mexican Fender really shines, because somebody else already took the first hit on the price.
