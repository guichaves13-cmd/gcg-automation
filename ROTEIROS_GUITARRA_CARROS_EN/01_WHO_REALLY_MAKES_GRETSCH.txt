Let us start at the beginning, because the beginning is older than most people realize.

Gretsch was founded in Brooklyn, New York, in eighteen eighty three, by a young German immigrant named Friedrich Gretsch. He was in his twenties. He did not start out making electric guitars, obviously, because there were no electric guitars. He made banjos, tambourines, and drums. And here is a detail that tends to surprise guitar players. For a big part of its history, Gretsch was at least as famous for its drums as for its guitars. If you are a drummer watching this, you already knew that, and you have probably been waiting for a guitar channel to admit it.

Friedrich died young, only about a dozen years after starting the company, and his son Fred took over as a teenager. Fred Gretsch Senior is the one who turned a small Brooklyn workshop into a real manufacturer. The company eventually moved into a big multi-story building in Brooklyn, near the Williamsburg Bridge. That building became famous in its own right. For decades, if you said Gretsch, you meant that building in Brooklyn.

By the nineteen fifties, Gretsch guitars had become something special. This is the era of the White Falcon, the Duo Jet, the Country Gentleman, and the six one twenty, the famous orange hollow body that a lot of people picture when they hear the name. A big part of that story is Chet Atkins, who partnered with Gretsch on signature models. Then came rockabilly, then came the early rock and roll players, and then, in the sixties, came a certain British band whose lead guitarist played a Gretsch on television in front of an enormous American audience. After that, demand went through the roof.

So for roughly the first eighty years, the answer to who made Gretsch guitars was easy. The Gretsch family did. In Brooklyn.

And then it all came apart.

In nineteen sixty seven, the Gretsch family sold the company to Baldwin, the piano maker. This is the part of the story that Gretsch fans tend to skip over, and I understand why. Within a few years, Baldwin moved guitar production out of Brooklyn. Production went to Arkansas, to a town called Booneville. The legendary Brooklyn building stopped being the heart of the company.

Now, I want to be fair here, because the internet loves to say every guitar made after a corporate buyout is garbage. That is not true, and I have had Baldwin-era Gretsch guitars on my bench that played beautifully. But the reputation of that era is mixed. Owners and repair people have talked for years about inconsistent quality, design changes that did not age well, and a company that seemed to be drifting. There were also, as I understand it, a couple of factory fires reported during that period, which certainly did not help. By the very early nineteen eighties, Baldwin stopped making Gretsch guitars altogether.

Think about that for a second. One of the most famous names in American guitars, the brand on the stage at the birth of rock and roll, and for a few years in the early eighties, nobody was building them at all.

In nineteen eighty five, a member of the family bought the name back. Fred Gretsch, often called Fred the Third, the great-grandson of the founder, reacquired the company. Brooklyn was long gone as a guitar plant, Arkansas was closed, and building a high end guitar factory in the United States from scratch in the late eighties would have been enormously expensive. So when Gretsch guitars came back, around nineteen eighty nine, they did not come back from Brooklyn.

They came back from Japan.

The factory most widely associated with them is a company called Terada, a long established Japanese maker that, from what is commonly reported, had built hollow body and archtop instruments for a number of brands. Around two thousand two, two thousand three, Gretsch made a deal with Fender. So when people ask, does Fender own Gretsch, the honest answer is, it is complicated. The family has kept the name.
