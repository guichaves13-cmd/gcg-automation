It is a Tuesday afternoon in a strip mall outside Columbus, Ohio. The sign over the door is still lit. Inside, there is a wall of electric guitars, maybe two hundred of them, hanging in perfect rows under bright lights. There is a drum room in the back. There is a kid trying out a distortion pedal way too loud. And there are exactly three customers in the whole building, and two of them are just killing time before a dentist appointment next door.

If you have been inside a big-box guitar store lately, you have probably felt it. Something is off. Fewer people. Fewer staff. The guitar you wanted is "available to ship" but not actually on the wall. And the one guy who knew how to do a proper setup is not there anymore.

The big-box guitar store, the giant chain store that a lot of us grew up in, is in trouble. Not overnight. Not with one big explosion. It is a slow fade, the same way the big car dealerships and the department stores started fading before anyone said it out loud. Today I am going to count down the six real reasons it is happening. And at the end, I am going to tell you what this means for the next guitar you buy, because there is one change coming that almost nobody is talking about, and it is going to hit your wallet whether you shop in stores or not.

Let me tell you where I am coming from. I have spent a long time on the bench. Setups, fret work, wiring, the boring stuff that makes a guitar actually play. I have worked with independent shops, I have done work that came out of chain stores, and I have seen thousands of guitars that came out of a box from a warehouse. So I am not here to bash anybody. I am here to tell you what I see from behind the counter, and what it means for you.

Before we start, one quick fact so nobody thinks I am making things up. In late 2020, Guitar Center, the biggest musical instrument retailer in the United States, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection. That is public record. It restructured its debt and came out the other side within a few weeks, and the stores stayed open. But think about that for a second. The biggest name in the business, a company that a lot of people thought was untouchable, needed a court to help it survive. And then, in 2024, Sam Ash, a family music chain that goes back to the 1920s, announced it was closing its stores. When the two most recognizable store names in American guitar retail go through that kind of turbulence within a few years of each other, that is not a coincidence. That is a pattern.

So let us get into it.

Reason number six. The store is no longer where you discover guitars.

Think about how you found your first guitar. If you are over forty, the answer is probably a catalog, a friend, or a store. You walked in, you saw it on the wall, you picked it up, and something clicked. The store was the showroom, the library, and the museum all in one.

Now think about how a sixteen-year-old finds a guitar today. They watch a video. They see a player they love using a certain model. They read comments. They watch a demo, then another demo, then a shootout, then a teardown. By the time they are ready to buy, they already know exactly what they want, what color, what pickups, and what it should cost. The store is no longer where the decision gets made. At best, it is where the decision gets confirmed.

The car world went through exactly the same thing. People used to drive from dealership to dealership on a Saturday, kicking tires. Now they spend three weeks on their phone, and they show up at the dealer with a printout, ready to argue about the price. The showroom lost its power the moment the research moved online. Guitar stores are living the same story about ten years later.

And here is the part that hurts the big stores more than the small ones. A small independent shop was never really selling discovery. It was selling relationship. The owner knows your name, knows what you play, and calls you when something cool comes in on trade. The big box was selling selection. Two hundred guitars on a wall. And selection is precisely the thing the internet does better than any building ever could.

Reason number five. The direct sellers and the online giants.

Here is a story. A customer of mine, I will call him Dale, a retired electrician from just outside Knoxville, Tennessee, came in last year with a brand new guitar he had ordered online. He did not buy it from a store at all. He bought it directly from a smaller brand's website. Shipped to his door. He told me something that stuck with me. He said, "I used to drive forty-five minutes to the big store, and then they did not have the one I wanted anyway. Now I click a button, and it shows up on Thursday."

That is the whole problem in two sentences.

Online retailers, including some very large ones that started out selling mostly by phone and website, have built a reputation on things that used to belong to the store. Fast shipping. Easy returns. Detailed photos of the actual guitar, sometimes with the serial number and the exact weight. Some of them inspect guitars before they ship. That last part matters, and we will come back to it, because it is connected to the biggest reason of all.

And then you have the direct-to-consumer brands. Companies that skip the store completely, design guitars, have them built overseas, and sell them straight from their own website at prices that a store with rent, salaries, and electric bills simply cannot match. The car industry has a version of this too. Some newer car brands sell direct, no dealership, price on the website, delivered to your driveway. Dealers hated it. Big-box guitar stores feel the same pressure.

Now, I want to be fair. Buying direct is not perfect. If something goes wrong, you are shipping a guitar back across the country in a box, and boxes get dropped. But for a lot of players, especially experienced ones who know what they want, the trade-off is worth it.

Reason number four. The used market moved into your pocket.

Back in the day, if you wanted a used guitar, you had three choices. The classified ads in the newspaper, the pawn shop, or the used rack at the big store. And that used rack was a big deal for the chain stores, because they could take your old guitar on trade for a low number, clean it up, and sell it for a much higher number. That margin paid a lot of bills.

Then the used market moved onto your phone. Reverb became a huge marketplace for gear. Facebook Marketplace turned every neighborhood into a used guitar shop. Local selling apps made it easy to sell a guitar to somebody fifteen minutes away for cash. Suddenly players realized they could sell their own gear for a lot more than the trade-in offer, and buyers realized they could find the same guitar for less than the store price.

That is the same thing that happened to used car lots when online car marketplaces took off. Once everyone can see every price at once, the middleman's margin shrinks. And for the big-box guitar store, the used department was not a side hustle. For a lot of locations, it was a serious part of the business.

I had a customer, a young woman named Priya from Sacramento, who traded in a guitar at a chain store a few years ago and later found out she could have sold it privately for close to twice the trade-in amount. She was not angry at the store. They have to make money. But she told me she would never trade in again. And when enough Priyas make that same decision, the used rack gets thinner, and the store loses one of its best profit centers.

Now, there is a twist here, and it connects to one of those open questions I mentioned at the start. The used market is great for prices, but it has a hidden cost that most players do not see until they are standing at my bench. Hold that thought, because I will come back to it near the end.

Reason number three. Tariffs and the price squeeze.

This is the one that has been all over the news in 2025 and 2026, and it is affecting the whole industry. A huge number of guitars, amps, pedals, strings, and parts are manufactured in Asia, in places like China, Indonesia, Vietnam, and Korea. Even guitars assembled in the United States often use parts, hardware, or electronics that come from somewhere else. When import tariffs go up, costs go up somewhere in that chain.

I am going to be careful here, because the tariff situation has changed several times and the exact rates depend on the country, the product, and the month. I am not going to throw out a number I cannot back up. But what is clear is that several major brands have announced price increases over the last couple of years, and many of them have pointed to costs like tariffs, shipping, and materials.

Now here is why that hits a big-box store harder than a small shop or an online seller. A big store has to keep a massive amount of inventory physically sitting on the wall. Every guitar on that wall is money that is not moving. When prices go up, that inventory gets more expensive to replace, and customers become more cautious about spending. Fewer impulse buys. More people waiting for a sale. More people going used.

It is exactly what car dealers went through when new car prices jumped. The lots were full of expensive inventory, the interest costs on that inventory kept piling up, and customers walked in, saw the sticker, and walked back out to look at used cars. Holding inventory is a game you win when sales are fast, and lose when sales slow down.

Reason number two. The staff got thinner, and the experts left.

Walk into a big guitar store on a weekday, and count the employees. Then ask one of them a technical question. Something real. Like, "What is the difference in neck feel between these two models?" or "Can you check the relief on this neck before I buy it?"

Sometimes you will get an amazing answer. There are still incredibly knowledgeable people working in chain stores, people who have been playing for thirty years and know their stuff. I have met a lot of them. But more and more often, players tell me they get someone who is friendly, polite, and has no idea. Or they cannot find anyone at all.

That is not a moral failing. It is economics. When a retail chain is under pressure, the first places it looks to cut are labor hours and experienced, higher-paid staff. That is true in electronics stores, in bookstores, in car dealerships, in everything. But a guitar store is not a toaster store. The knowledge of the people working there was a big part of what you were paying for. And when that knowledge walks out the door, the store becomes just a warehouse with nicer lighting.

A guy named Marcus, a session player I have worked with in Nashville, told me something that sums it up. He said, "I used to go to the big store to talk to guitar people. Now I go to the big store when I need strings fast on a Sunday." That is a completely different business. And strings on a Sunday do not pay the rent on a forty-thousand-square-foot building.

Reason number one. The death of the setup.

This is the big one. This is the open question I promised you at the very beginning, and it is the reason I think the fading of the big-box store is going to matter more to your playing than to your wallet.

Here is something most players do not realize. Almost every production guitar, from the cheapest to the expensive ones, needs a setup to play its best. A setup means adjusting the truss rod for the right neck relief, setting the string height at the bridge, checking the nut slots, setting the intonation, sometimes leveling a high fret, and adjusting the pickup height. When it is done well, a modest guitar can play like a much more expensive one. When it is not done at all, an expensive guitar can feel like a piece of lumber.

Guitars travel from the factory to warehouses to stores. They go through temperature changes, humidity changes, and months in a box. Wood moves. Necks shift. A guitar that left the factory perfect can arrive needing work.

In the old model, the store was supposed to catch that. A good store had a tech, sometimes a great tech, who would go through guitars before they hit the wall, or at least before they went out the door. You picked up a guitar in the store, and if it had been set up, it felt great, and you bought it. The setup was part of the magic, even if you did not know it was happening.

Now think about what happens when the store fades. Online buyers get the guitar straight from a box. Direct-to-consumer buyers get it straight from a box. Used buyers get it from a stranger who may have never had it set up in their life. In every one of those cases, nobody is standing between the factory and your hands making sure it plays right.

That is why I see so many players at my bench who think they bought a bad guitar. Most of the time, they did not. They bought a guitar that nobody ever set up. I had a customer, a high school kid named Tyler from Mesa, Arizona, whose dad bought him a perfectly good guitar online. Tyler was ready to quit because it hurt his fingers and would not stay in tune. Forty-five minutes on my bench, new strings, lower action, a nut slot cleaned up, and the kid was grinning. His dad looked at me and said, "So the guitar was fine the whole time?" Yes. It was fine the whole time. It just never had anybody take care of it.

When the store with the tech disappears, the setup does not disappear. It just becomes your problem. And that is the thing nobody in the industry likes to say out loud.

So, what does all of this mean for you and your next guitar? Let us talk about it practically, because this is where you can actually save money and get a better instrument.

First, budget for a setup. Every single time. When you look at a guitar price, mentally add the cost of a professional setup at a good local shop. Prices vary by area and by what the guitar needs, so call a few shops and ask. Think of it like the destination fee on a car. It is part of the real price, whether the listing shows it or not. A guitar that costs a bit less but needs a setup might still be the better deal, and a guitar that costs a bit more but has been inspected and set up before shipping might actually be the smarter buy.

Second, look carefully at the return policy before you buy. This is more important now than ever. If you are buying online, find out how many days you get, who pays return shipping, and whether there is a restocking fee. Some sellers are very generous. Some are not. A guitar is a physical thing that needs to feel right in your hands, and if you cannot touch it before you buy it, the return policy is your safety net. Read it like you would read the warranty on a used car.

Third, understand what is happening to used prices. When new prices go up, used prices often follow, because people who cannot afford new shift to used, and demand goes up. That means a guitar you own right now may be worth more than you think, and a used guitar you want may cost more than it did a few years ago. Before you sell, check recent sold prices on the major marketplaces, not just asking prices. Asking prices are dreams. Sold prices are reality. And before you buy used, do the same thing, so you know when a deal is really a deal.

Fourth, when buying used, inspect like a mechanic. Bring a tuner. Check that every fret plays clean up the neck on every string. Look down the neck from the headstock for twists. Check that the truss rod still has room to adjust, if the seller will let you. Plug it in and wiggle the output jack and turn every knob. Look at the frets for deep grooves. If it is a private sale, meet somewhere safe, and do not be shy about walking away. The car buyers have a rule for this. Never fall in love on the lot. Same thing here.

Fifth, find your local independent tech and your local independent shop, and treat them like gold. Here is the irony of this whole story. As the big-box stores fade, the small independent shops with a great tech are becoming more valuable, not less. They are the ones who will do your setup, fix the guitar you bought online, help you check out a used one, and tell you the truth. A lot of them are struggling too, and the best thing you can do for your playing is to keep them in business. Buy your strings there. Buy your cables there. Get your setups done there.

Sixth, and this is the one I really want you to remember. Do not judge a guitar until it has been set up. If you buy something online or used, and it feels wrong, do not assume the guitar is junk and do not assume you are a bad player. Get it on a bench first. I would bet a lot of guitars sitting in closets all over America right now are perfectly good instruments that just need forty-five minutes of attention.

Now, let me close the last loop, because I told you at the beginning there was one change coming that would hit your wallet no matter where you shop. Here it is.

As the big stores shrink, the cost of the setup, the inspection, and the expertise is moving from the store to you. That is the change. It used to be hidden in the price of the guitar at the store. Now it is a separate line item, and a lot of players are not budgeting for it. The ones who do budget for it, who build a relationship with a good tech, who buy carefully, and who check return policies, are going to get better guitars for less money than ever before. The ones who do not are going to keep buying guitars that feel wrong and blaming the guitar.

In other words, the big-box store dying is not the end of buying great guitars. It is the end of somebody else doing the thinking for you. And honestly, for players who are willing to learn a little, that is not bad news. It is a huge opportunity.

Will the big chain stores disappear completely? I do not think so, at least not anytime soon. Just like car dealerships did not vanish when online car sales took off, I think the big stores will shrink, change, and maybe become something different. Smaller locations. More focus on lessons, rentals, repairs, and the things you cannot download. The ones that bring back great techs and great experts might actually come out stronger. The ones that do not will keep fading.

But the days of walking into a huge store, grabbing any guitar off a wall of two hundred, and assuming it has been checked and set up for you, those days are going away. And the sooner you adjust, the better your next guitar is going to be.
