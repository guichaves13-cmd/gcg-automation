On the night of February 28, 1983, something happened in America that had never happened before and has rarely happened since. Streets went quiet. Restaurants emptied out. In some cities, water departments reportedly noticed a sudden surge as millions of people waited for the commercial break to use the bathroom at the same time. More than one hundred million Americans were watching the same television show, saying goodbye to a group of Army doctors and nurses in a war that had ended thirty years earlier. That show was M*A*S*H. For eleven seasons it made us laugh, it made us cry, and it made us think. But behind the scenes, there were battles CBS fought with its own creators, cast members who were kept in the dark, and decisions that still stir up arguments among fans today. Secret number eight. The haunting theme song was written by a fourteen-year-old boy, and it made him more money than his famous father made directing the movie.

Almost everyone can hum the gentle, melancholy melody that opened every episode of M*A*S*H, with those helicopters coming over the hills. But very few TV viewers ever heard the words, because the series used only the instrumental version. The song is called "Suicide Is Painless," and it was written for the original 1970 film M*A*S*H, directed by Robert Altman.

Here's the story. Altman wanted a song for a scene in the movie, and he wanted the lyrics to be deliberately silly and awkward. He reportedly tried to write them himself but said he was too old to write something that dumb. So he handed the job to his fourteen-year-old son, Mike Altman. Mike came back with the lyrics quickly, composer Johnny Mandel wrote the music, and the song ended up in the film.

When the television series became a massive hit and used the melody week after week for eleven years, the royalties kept rolling in. Robert Altman later said on The Tonight Show with Johnny Carson that his son had earned far more from the song than he had been paid to direct the film. Various accounts have put Mike's earnings well over a million dollars over the years, while Altman reportedly received about seventy-five thousand dollars to direct the movie. The father was famously unhappy with the TV series, which he felt had softened his antiwar vision, but he had to admit the show was very good for his son's bank account.

Secret number seven. The original M*A*S*H was based on a real surgeon's experiences, and the author hid behind a pen name.

The 4077th never existed, but the world it came from was very real. The story began with a 1968 novel called MASH: A Novel About Three Army Doctors, written by Richard Hooker. That name was a pseudonym. The real author was Dr. H. Richard Hornberger, a surgeon who had served in the 8055th Mobile Army Surgical Hospital during the Korean War. He wrote the book with help from sportswriter W. C. Heinz, and it drew on the wild, exhausting, and often heartbreaking life of surgeons working just miles from the front lines.

Here's the irony. Dr. Hornberger was, by most accounts, fairly conservative in his outlook, and he reportedly did not care much for the way the television series turned his characters, especially Hawkeye Pierce, into outspoken liberal voices against war. Hawkeye was, in fact, partly modeled on Hornberger himself. The doctor reportedly felt Alan Alda's Hawkeye was much more of a preacher than the man he had written about.

When producer Gene Reynolds and writer Larry Gelbart developed the series for CBS in 1972, they went out of their way to make it feel authentic. Reynolds and Gelbart interviewed real doctors and nurses who had served in Korea, and many of the stories that showed up in episodes were inspired by those conversations. Medical details were checked carefully. That's part of why the show felt so real. Underneath the jokes about Major Burns and the Swamp's homemade gin still, there was actual history, gathered from people who had lived it.

Secret number six. CBS insisted on a laugh track, and the creators of M*A*S*H fought it for the entire run of the show.

This is one of the most famous behind-the-scenes battles in TV history. Larry Gelbart and Gene Reynolds believed that a show about war, about surgeons trying to save young soldiers' lives, should not have canned laughter telling the audience when to laugh. In the early seventies, though, the networks believed comedies needed a laugh track to work. CBS insisted.

So the creators negotiated. The compromise was that the laugh track would not be used during scenes in the operating room. When the doctors were in surgery, there would be no artificial laughter, no matter how funny the dialogue was. If you watch closely, you'll notice that the O.R. scenes have a completely different feel. The jokes are still there, but they land in silence, mixed with the sounds of surgical instruments and quiet voices. It's eerie and powerful.

Gelbart said many times over the years that he regretted the laugh track and considered it a wound on the show. As the series went on, the laughter was used more sparingly, and a handful of episodes aired with no laugh track at all, including the experimental episode "The Interview," filmed in black and white as a mock news documentary, and the series finale. Here's a fun fact for fans: when M*A*S*H aired in the United Kingdom, the BBC broadcast it without a laugh track. And later DVD releases in the United States included an option to watch many episodes without one. If you've never seen the show that way, try it sometime. It feels like an entirely different, more serious drama.

Secret number five. M*A*S*H nearly flopped in its first season, and it was saved by a lucky time slot.

Today, M*A*S*H is remembered as one of the most successful shows ever made. But when it premiered on CBS on September 17, 1972, it struggled. It was scheduled on Sunday nights against tough competition, and its ratings were weak. By the end of the first season, it was nowhere near the top of the charts, and there was real uncertainty about whether CBS would bring it back.

What saved it was a schedule change. For season two, CBS moved M*A*S*H to Saturday nights, right after the biggest show on television, All in the Family. That Saturday lineup became legendary. All in the Family, M*A*S*H, The Mary Tyler Moore Show, The Bob Newhart Show, and The Carol Burnett Show, all on one night. It is often called one of the greatest single nights of programming in television history.

With All in the Family as its lead-in, M*A*S*H found a huge audience almost overnight. It jumped into the top five for that second season, and it remained a top ten show for most of the rest of its run. Think about how close we came to losing it. If CBS had given up after a single season, there would have been no Radar's teddy bear, no Klinger's dresses, no Colonel Potter's horse Sophie, and no farewell that united the country. A schedule decision made by network executives may have been the single most important moment in the show's history.

Secret number four. Klinger was only supposed to appear in one episode, and Jamie Farr had actually served in the Army in Korea.

Corporal Maxwell Q. Klinger, the soldier from Toledo, Ohio, who wore women's clothing in a never-ending attempt to get a Section Eight discharge, was one of the most beloved characters on the show. But he was never meant to stick around. Klinger first appeared in the first season episode "Chief Surgeon Who?" and he was conceived as a one-time joke. The original idea reportedly even had the character as effeminate, but Jamie Farr and the producers decided he would play Klinger straight, as a regular guy desperate to get home, which made the joke funnier and more sympathetic.

Audiences loved him, and the producers kept bringing him back. Eventually Klinger became a full cast member, and after Radar left in season eight, Klinger took over as company clerk and finally put the dresses away.

What many viewers never knew is that Jamie Farr, like Klinger, really was from Toledo, Ohio, and that he had actually served in the U.S. Army. He was drafted in the 1950s and served in Korea and Japan after the fighting had ended, and he reportedly entertained troops on a USO tour with comedian Red Skelton. Farr also proudly worked Toledo references into the show, from the Toledo Mud Hens minor league baseball team to Tony Packo's Cafe, a Hungarian restaurant that became famous after Klinger kept mentioning it. Fans still visit Tony Packo's today because of M*A*S*H. And in a lovely full-circle detail, Klinger's Toledo Mud Hens cap became one of the most recognizable props from the series.

Secret number three. The actors behind some of the show's most lovable characters had secrets of their own, and the cameras were carefully positioned to protect them.

Gary Burghoff, who played Corporal Walter "Radar" O'Reilly, holds a special place in M*A*S*H history. He was one of the very few actors from the 1970 Robert Altman film to carry his role over to the television series. Radar, with his teddy bear, his grape Nehi soda, and his uncanny ability to hear helicopters before anyone else, was a fan favorite from the start.

What viewers didn't notice was that Burghoff was born with a condition affecting his left hand, which is smaller, with shortened fingers. It's often identified as Poland syndrome. Throughout the series, Burghoff and the directors worked to keep that hand out of the spotlight. Watch carefully and you'll see Radar often holding a clipboard, a cup, or some other object in his left hand, or keeping it in a pocket or just out of frame. It was handled so naturally that most of the audience never noticed.

Burghoff left the series during the eighth season, in 1979. He was reportedly exhausted and wanted to spend more time with his family. His farewell episode, "Goodbye, Radar," was a two-parter, and at the end, Hawkeye finds Radar's teddy bear left on his bunk. It remains one of the most touching moments in the series.

And Radar wasn't the only one keeping things hidden. Loretta Swit, who played Major Margaret "Hot Lips" Houlihan, fought for years to deepen her character, and the nickname itself became a sore spot. The writers eventually let Margaret grow from a punchline into a strong, respected officer, and by the later seasons the nickname was rarely used. Swit earned two Emmy Awards for her performance. It was a quiet victory behind the scenes, and a sign of how much the show grew over eleven years.

That growth showed up in small ways, too. Early on, Margaret was mostly the target of the Swamp's pranks, paired with Frank Burns in a secret romance that everybody in camp knew about. Over time, the writers gave her a marriage to Lieutenant Colonel Donald Penobscott, a painful divorce, and a series of scenes that revealed how lonely it was to be a career woman in the Army of the 1950s. By the finale, when Margaret and Hawkeye share a long goodbye kiss, it felt earned. Swit has said in interviews that she was grateful the writers listened to her, and that the character's journey was one of the things she was proudest of in her career.

Secret number two. Alan Alda became the creative heart of the show, and he made history in a way no actor ever had.

When M*A*S*H started, Alan Alda was a stage and film actor who wasn't widely known. He was the son of Robert Alda, a Broadway and film star. By the end of the series, Alan Alda wasn't just the star of M*A*S*H, he was one of its most important writers and directors.

Over the run of the show, Alda wrote or co-wrote around nineteen episodes and directed more than thirty. He also became deeply involved in shaping the show's voice, especially after Larry Gelbart left following the fourth season and Gene Reynolds stepped back later. Alda made history by becoming the first person to win Emmy Awards for acting, writing, and directing, all for the same series. Over the years he earned five Emmys for his work on M*A*S*H.

And here's a sweet detail. Alda's father, Robert Alda, actually guest starred on the show. He appeared in a couple of episodes as Major Anthony Borelli, a visiting doctor. Alan's half-brother, Antony Alda, also appeared in an episode. For a show that so often explored the bonds of family under pressure, it was lovely to see a real father and son working side by side.

There's another side of Alda's M*A*S*H years that fans rarely heard about. While the show was filmed in Los Angeles, Alda's wife Arlene and their three daughters lived in New Jersey. Rather than uproot his family, Alda reportedly flew back home across the country nearly every weekend for the eleven years the show was in production. Think about that, the man playing the ultimate wisecracking bachelor surgeon was, in real life, a devoted family man commuting thousands of miles to be at the dinner table with his wife and kids. Alan and Arlene Alda married in 1957 and are still married today, one of the longest marriages in Hollywood.

Alda also cared deeply about how the show portrayed war. He has said in interviews that he wanted Hawkeye's humor to come from pain, a way of surviving the unbearable. As a writer and director he pushed episodes that explored the toll of combat on the human mind. One of his most acclaimed scripts, "Dear Sigmund," told through the eyes of the visiting psychiatrist Sidney Freedman, and the finale itself, which he co-wrote and directed, dealt with Hawkeye's breakdown after a traumatic event on a bus. These were bold choices for a half-hour comedy. M*A*S*H was not glorifying war. It was showing what war does to ordinary people.

Secret number one. When Colonel Henry Blake died, the cast didn't know it was going to happen until minutes before they filmed the scene, and CBS was flooded with angry letters.

This is the most famous secret in M*A*S*H history, and it's still one of the most shocking moments ever broadcast in an American sitcom. McLean Stevenson played Lieutenant Colonel Henry Blake, the lovable, bumbling commanding officer of the 4077th. By the end of the third season, Stevenson had decided to leave the show. He wanted to be a star in his own right, and he reportedly was unhappy being part of an ensemble where Alan Alda's Hawkeye got most of the attention.

Larry Gelbart and Gene Reynolds decided that Henry's departure would not be a typical happy sitcom goodbye. In the episode "Abyssinia, Henry," which aired on March 18, 1975, Henry Blake receives his discharge and is going home. There's a party. There are tearful farewells. Henry kisses Margaret goodbye, he waves, his helicopter lifts off, and it seems like a sweet ending.

But the producers had a secret. The final page of the script was held back from the cast. Just minutes before filming the last scene, Gelbart and Reynolds handed it to the actors. In it, Radar walks into the operating room, his voice shaking, and announces that Henry Blake's plane was shot down over the Sea of Japan, and that there were no survivors. The surgeons stand in stunned silence, and they keep operating, because the wounded keep coming.

The cast was genuinely shocked. Some of the actors, including Gary Burghoff and Loretta Swit, have said over the years that the emotion you see on their faces was very real. McLean Stevenson himself reportedly knew only shortly before. The producers wanted to make a point. In war, people don't always get to go home. Young men, and good men, die suddenly and for no reason.

The audience reaction was enormous. CBS and the producers received a flood of letters, many of them angry, some heartbroken. Some viewers felt betrayed that a comedy would do something so painful. Others wrote that it was the most honest moment they had ever seen on television. Reynolds and Gelbart stood by the decision, and today it's widely seen as a turning point, the moment M*A*S*H proved a sitcom could be a serious work of art.

That same season, Wayne Rogers, who played Trapper John McIntyre, also left after reportedly disputing his contract, and the next season brought in Harry Morgan as Colonel Sherman T. Potter and Mike Farrell as B. J. Hunnicutt. The show reinvented itself, and many fans would say it only got better.

Here's a little detail that sharp-eyed fans love. Harry Morgan wasn't new to the 4077th when he arrived as Colonel Potter. Back in season three, in 1974, he had guest starred in the episode "The General Flipped at Dawn," playing a completely different character, the unhinged Major General Bartford Hamilton Steele. The producers were so impressed by him that they brought him back as the steady, horse-loving career officer from Hannibal, Missouri, who became the camp's father figure. Morgan, who had already been a familiar face on Dragnet, played Potter for the rest of the series and won an Emmy for the role in 1980.

And the arrival of Major Charles Emerson Winchester the Third in season six, played by David Ogden Stiers, after Larry Linville decided to leave as Frank Burns, gave the show a worthy rival for Hawkeye. Winchester was a snob, but he was a brilliant surgeon and, as the years went on, a man with a surprisingly tender heart, especially in his love of classical music. Linville reportedly felt he had taken Frank Burns as far as he could go after five seasons, and the writers replaced a cartoonish foil with a smarter one.

And that brings us full circle to that unforgettable night in 1983. The finale, "Goodbye, Farewell and Amen," ran two and a half hours. It drew an estimated one hundred and six million viewers, with a 60.2 rating and a 77 share, meaning about three out of every four televisions turned on in America were tuned to it. For twenty-seven years it remained the most watched broadcast in American history, until Super Bowl XLIV finally passed it in 2010. It is still the most watched episode of any scripted series ever aired in the United States.

There was even a twist of fate behind the scenes. In October 1982, a brush fire swept through the Fox Ranch in Malibu Creek State Park, where the outdoor scenes were filmed, and destroyed much of the set. The writers wove the fire right into the finale, giving the 4077th a final crisis before the war ended. And the image that closes the series is one you'll never forget, B. J. leaving a message spelled out in stones on the ground for Hawkeye to see from his departing helicopter: GOODBYE.

The 4077th lasted on television for eleven years, more than three times longer than the Korean War itself. That fact alone tells you how much it meant to the people who watched it. Many of us grew up with these characters, and some of us grew old with them. They felt like our friends, our family, and sometimes, our conscience.
