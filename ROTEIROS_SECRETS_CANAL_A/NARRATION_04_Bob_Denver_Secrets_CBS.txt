On the morning of November 22, 1963, a small boat sailed out of a harbor in Hawaii carrying a film crew and a young actor in a red shirt and a white sailor's hat. They were shooting the pilot for a new CBS comedy about seven castaways on an uncharted island. Then word came over the radio from Dallas. President John F. Kennedy had been shot. Production stopped, the harbor was reportedly closed, and the boat that would become the S.S. Minnow was forced to return to shore. That young actor was Bob Denver, and the show was Gilligan's Island. It would become one of the most rerun programs in television history, yet the man who played Gilligan reportedly saw very little of the fortune it made. Today we are counting down eight secrets about Bob Denver that CBS never told Gilligan's Island fans. And our number one secret has nothing to do with coconuts or lagoons. It is about the role Bob Denver considered the most important of his life. So sit right back, and you'll hear a tale.

Robert Osborne Denver was born on January 9, 1935, in New Rochelle, New York. While he was still young, his family moved to Brownwood, Texas, and that is where he spent much of his childhood. He later attended Loyola University in Los Angeles, which today is Loyola Marymount University. He studied there, played sports, and got involved in campus theater almost by accident. He was not chasing stardom. He was a regular young man trying to figure out what to do with his life. And that leads us to our first secret.

Secret number eight. Before he was a television star, Bob Denver was a mailman and a schoolteacher.

After graduating from Loyola in 1957, Bob did not go straight into show business. He took a job with the post office, delivering mail. He also worked as a teacher, coaching and teaching physical education and math at a Catholic elementary school in the Los Angeles area. By his own account, he enjoyed working with kids, and he might have stayed in teaching if not for a lucky break.

That break came, according to the stories he later told, through a family connection who encouraged him to try for a role in a new television series that the studio was producing for CBS. The show was The Many Loves of Dobie Gillis, based on the stories of humorist Max Shulman. Bob auditioned for the part of Dobie's best friend, a goateed, bongo-playing beatnik named Maynard G. Krebs. He got it. And just like that, the mailman from Texas became one of the most recognizable faces on American television.

Maynard was a sensation. He wore a scruffy sweatshirt, he talked in jazzy beatnik slang, and every time someone mentioned the word "work," he would shriek in horror. "Work?!" became a national catchphrase. Teenagers across America imitated him. Dobie Gillis ran on CBS from 1959 to 1963, with Dwayne Hickman as Dobie, and it was one of the first network shows to center on the lives of teenagers. It also featured a young Warren Beatty and a teenage Tuesday Weld in early roles. Bob Denver had been plucked from a classroom into the spotlight.

Secret number seven. Uncle Sam tried to take Bob Denver away from his hit show, and the writers had to draft Maynard too.

In 1960, during the run of Dobie Gillis, Bob Denver received a draft notice. America was in the middle of the Cold War, and the peacetime draft was still in effect. The producers were worried. Maynard was one of the most popular characters on the show. So the writers did something clever. They wrote the draft into the story. In the series, Maynard was drafted into the Army and shipped off, and for a stretch of episodes, the writers brought in a new character, Maynard's cousin Jerome Krebs, to fill the gap.

But here is the twist. Bob Denver was rejected by the Army. According to accounts of his life, he had suffered a neck injury years earlier, and at his physical, he was found unfit for service. So he came back to the show, and Maynard was written back in, cheerfully returning from his military adventure. The whole thing became a fun piece of behind-the-scenes trivia, and it showed how valuable Bob had already become. The network was willing to rearrange its storylines rather than lose him.

When Dobie Gillis ended in 1963, Bob was already well known, but he was also already being typecast. Audiences saw him as the lovable, goofy sidekick. That image would follow him straight onto a deserted island.

Secret number six. Bob Denver was not the first choice to play Gilligan.

Gilligan's Island was created by Sherwood Schwartz, a veteran comedy writer who had won an Emmy writing for The Red Skelton Show. Schwartz had a big idea. He wanted to put a group of people from completely different walks of life together in one place, a kind of small society, and see how they got along. A millionaire and his wife. A movie star. A professor. A sweet farm girl from Kansas. A ship's captain and his bumbling first mate. Stranded on an island.

For the role of Gilligan, the bumbling first mate, Schwartz and the network reportedly first wanted Jerry Van Dyke, the younger brother of Dick Van Dyke. But according to well-known accounts, Jerry turned it down. He reportedly chose instead to star in a different sitcom, My Mother the Car, a show in which his late mother is reincarnated as an antique automobile. My Mother the Car premiered in 1965 and lasted one season. It is frequently listed among the worst television shows ever made. Jerry Van Dyke would later find great success decades later on the sitcom Coach, but he openly joked over the years about the choice he made.

With Jerry out, the part went to Bob Denver. Sherwood Schwartz later said he could not imagine anyone else as Gilligan. Bob brought a sweetness to the character that kept him from becoming annoying. Gilligan wrecked every rescue plan, fell into every trap, and broke every invention the Professor built, and yet audiences never got tired of him, because Bob made him so kindhearted. Gilligan meant well. He just could not help himself.

And Bob was paired with Alan Hale Jr. as the Skipper. Alan Hale was the son of the famous character actor Alan Hale Sr., who had appeared alongside Errol Flynn in many swashbucklers. The two men became close friends, and their chemistry, the big blustering Skipper and his skinny little buddy, became the heart of the show. The Skipper would swat Gilligan with his hat, and America laughed every single time.

Secret number five. The day the pilot was being filmed, America changed forever, and the ship got its name from a government official.

As we mentioned at the start, the pilot for Gilligan's Island was filmed on location in Hawaii in November of 1963, mostly on the island of Kauai. The famous opening shots of the S.S. Minnow sailing out of the harbor were filmed in Honolulu. According to Sherwood Schwartz's own account in his book about the show, production was underway on November 22, 1963, when the news came that President Kennedy had been assassinated. Filming was halted, and the crew needed to get their boat back in. When you watch the opening credits and see the Minnow heading out to sea, you may be looking at footage from one of the darkest days in American history.

Now, about the name of that boat. The Minnow was named, reportedly with a wink, after Newton Minow. Minow was the chairman of the Federal Communications Commission under President Kennedy, and in 1961 he gave a famous speech in which he called television "a vast wasteland." Sherwood Schwartz, along with many in the television industry, did not appreciate that remark. So, according to Schwartz, he named the little shipwrecked boat after the chairman. It was Hollywood's way of saying thank you very much.

The pilot also looked quite different from the series we know. In the original version, the castaways included two secretaries and a high school teacher instead of Ginger, Mary Ann, and the Professor, and the theme music was a calypso song rather than the ballad everyone remembers. CBS was not satisfied. Schwartz recast those roles, bringing in Tina Louise, Dawn Wells, and Russell Johnson, and he wrote a new theme song himself, with George Wyle, telling the story of the castaways. That song, The Ballad of Gilligan's Isle, became one of the most famous theme songs ever written. Schwartz later explained that he wrote it so that every new viewer would understand the premise in about a minute. And it worked.

Gilligan's Island premiered on CBS on September 26, 1964. Critics were not kind. Many reviewers dismissed it as silly. But the audience did not care. The show was a hit from the start.

Once the series was underway, the castaways did not return to Hawaii. The island you see on the show was built on a soundstage and backlot at CBS Studio Center in Studio City, California, where the production crew constructed a lagoon, palm trees, and the huts the castaways called home. The lagoon was a man-made pond, and according to cast recollections, it could get chilly and murky, especially when Bob had to fall into it, which was often. Gilligan probably took more tumbles into water than any other character in sitcom history, and Bob did a great deal of that physical comedy himself.

That physical comedy was one of Bob's real gifts. He had a natural, almost silent-film quality to his clowning. Watch closely and you will see how much he does without words, the way Gilligan's eyes widen, the way his whole body goes limp when he realizes he has ruined everything again, the way he snaps to attention when the Skipper shouts. Sherwood Schwartz compared him to the great clowns of the silent era, and Bob reportedly took great care in working out his gags so they looked effortless.

Then there is the famous question of Gilligan's first name. In all three seasons of the show, it was never actually spoken on screen. According to Sherwood Schwartz, in his own notes for the character, Gilligan's first name was Willy. As for the name Gilligan itself, Schwartz said he found it by flipping through a telephone book looking for a name that sounded right. A simple, friendly, slightly silly Irish name for a simple, friendly, slightly silly young man.

Gilligan's wardrobe became iconic too. The red rugby-style shirt, the white canvas hat, and the rolled-up white pants made him instantly recognizable, even in silhouette. The Skipper wore his blue polo and captain's hat, and together they looked like a matching set. Interestingly, the first season of Gilligan's Island was filmed in black and white. The show switched to color for its second and third seasons, in 1965 and 1966, and that is when America got its first good look at just how red Gilligan's shirt really was.

As the show went on, the stories became more and more fanciful. The castaways met visiting rock bands, mad scientists, jungle boys, cosmonauts, and headhunters, and Gilligan dreamed his way into fantasy versions of Dracula, the Wild West, and even a musical version of Hamlet. Through it all, Bob kept Gilligan grounded and sincere. No matter how wild the plot became, he played every moment as if Gilligan truly believed it, and that honesty is a big reason the show still makes children laugh today, sixty years later.

Secret number four. Bob Denver stood up for his co-stars when they were left out of the theme song.

Here is something many fans never noticed. In the first season of Gilligan's Island, the opening theme song did not name all seven castaways. It sang about Gilligan, the Skipper too, the millionaire and his wife, the movie star, and then, simply, "and the rest." The rest were the Professor and Mary Ann. Russell Johnson and Dawn Wells were left out of both the lyrics and, in the opening credits, the star billing.

According to accounts from the cast, including Dawn Wells and Russell Johnson themselves, Bob Denver went to bat for them. His contract reportedly gave him a say in the billing of the show, and he reportedly told the producers and the network that if Russell and Dawn were not given proper credit, he was prepared to give up his own top billing. Whether it went exactly that way or not, the change came. Starting in the second season, the theme song was updated to say "the Professor and Mary Ann, here on Gilligan's Isle." Dawn Wells and Russell Johnson spoke for the rest of their lives about how grateful they were. Dawn Wells called Bob a sweet and generous man. It was a small gesture that said a great deal about who he was.

Speaking of the cast, they remained a family off screen. Bob and Alan Hale Jr. were especially close, and when Alan passed away in 1990, his ashes were scattered at sea, a fitting farewell for the Skipper. Bob reportedly kept in touch with his castaway friends for the rest of his life, and he joined them again and again at reunions and fan events.

Secret number three. Gilligan's Island was canceled to save a western, and the castaways never got off the island on the original show.

At the end of its third season in 1967, Gilligan's Island was still doing well in the ratings. In fact, the cast and producers fully expected a fourth season. Sherwood Schwartz had even planned ahead. But then something happened at CBS. The network's long-running western Gunsmoke was in danger of being canceled. According to the most commonly told version of the story, CBS chairman William Paley, and reportedly his wife, Babe, were fond of Gunsmoke, and Paley insisted that it be saved. To make room for it in the schedule, the network moved Gunsmoke to a new time slot. Gilligan's Island was the show that lost its place.

The cast reportedly learned about the cancellation after they had already gone on summer break, assuming they would return. Because no one had planned a final episode, the castaways were never rescued on the original series. They were left stranded forever, as far as the 1960s were concerned. Ninety-eight episodes had been made, and that was that.

But television had other plans. In syndicated reruns, Gilligan's Island became bigger than it ever was in its original run. Generations of children came home from school and watched it every afternoon. Fans were so attached to the castaways that, more than a decade later, NBC aired a TV movie called Rescue from Gilligan's Island in October of 1978. It was a ratings blockbuster, one of the highest-rated television movies of its time. Two more followed, The Castaways on Gilligan's Island in 1979 and The Harlem Globetrotters on Gilligan's Island in 1981. There were also animated series, The New Adventures of Gilligan in the 1970s and Gilligan's Planet in the early 1980s, with Bob voicing his famous character. Gilligan simply would not go away.

Secret number two. Bob Denver reportedly earned very little from all those reruns, and Gilligan followed him for the rest of his career.

You might think that a show replayed for decades around the world would have made its stars rich. That was not the case. Under the standard contracts of that era, actors typically received residual payments for only a limited number of reruns. After those were used up, they received little or nothing more. According to interviews given by members of the cast, including Dawn Wells and Russell Johnson, the actors saw only modest money from the show's long life in syndication. Sherwood Schwartz, who owned a share of the series, did much better. Bob Denver was reportedly philosophical about it. He was not bitter in public. He simply accepted that it was the way business was done at the time.

The other challenge was typecasting. After Gilligan's Island, Bob was so firmly identified with Gilligan that it was hard for casting directors to see him as anyone else. He tried. He starred in a sitcom called The Good Guys on CBS from 1968 to 1970, alongside Herb Edelman, about two friends who run a diner. Then, in 1973, he starred in Dusty's Trail, a western comedy also produced by Sherwood Schwartz. It was, as many critics joked, Gilligan's Island on a wagon train, with Forrest Tucker in the Skipper role. It lasted one season.

Bob also worked in theater, touring in plays across the country, and he appeared in guest roles on many shows, often playing Gilligan or a wink to him. He appeared as Gilligan on episodes of other series, including ALF, and he good-naturedly played along. He wrote a memoir in 1993 called Gilligan, Maynard and Me, sharing his memories of both of his famous characters. He seemed, in the end, to make his peace with being Gilligan. As he reportedly said in interviews, if you are going to be remembered for one thing, you could do a lot worse than making people happy.

Secret number one. Bob Denver's most important role was not Gilligan. It was being a father, and he found peace far from Hollywood in the hills of West Virginia.

Bob Denver was married four times. His fourth wife, Dreama Perry Denver, was a woman from West Virginia whom he married in 1979. Together they had a son named Colin. When Colin was young, he was diagnosed with autism. At the time, many families faced a lack of understanding and support for children with autism, and Bob and Dreama threw themselves into caring for their son.

The family made a choice that surprised many in show business. They settled in the small town of Princeton, West Virginia, in the Appalachian Mountains, far away from the bright lights of Los Angeles. There, Bob lived a quiet life. He was a familiar and well-liked neighbor. He and Dreama ran a small radio station in the area, and Bob hosted a show playing oldies music, chatting with listeners the way he might talk to friends over the backyard fence. People in Princeton have said over the years that he never acted like a celebrity. He was just Bob.

Dreama has spoken many times about how devoted Bob was to Colin. He arranged his life around his son. He turned down work that would have kept him away too long. Dreama has carried that devotion forward through the Denver Foundation, which has worked to help families and children in the region, including those with special needs. Bob's love for his son became a lasting gift to their whole community.

Bob Denver's health began to fail in the early 2000s. He was diagnosed with cancer and underwent treatment, including heart bypass surgery. He passed away on September 2, 2005, at Wake Forest University Baptist Medical Center in Winston-Salem, North Carolina. He was seventy years old. Dreama and his family were with him. When the news spread, fans all over the world paid tribute. Dawn Wells said she had lost one of her dearest friends. Sherwood Schwartz praised him as the perfect Gilligan. And in thousands of homes across America, people who had grown up watching him felt as if they had lost a member of their own family.

So the next time you catch Gilligan's Island on television, and you see that skinny fellow in the red shirt tripping over a coconut while the Skipper chases him with his hat, remember who he really was. A mailman and a schoolteacher who got lucky. A beatnik who taught America to fear the word "work." A young man who nearly got drafted. An actor who stepped aside so his friends could have their names in the song. A man who made millions laugh and never got rich from it. And most of all, a father in the mountains of West Virginia who cared more about his son than about any spotlight.
