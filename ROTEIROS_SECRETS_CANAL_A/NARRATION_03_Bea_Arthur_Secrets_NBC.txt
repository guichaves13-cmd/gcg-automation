For most of her life, Bea Arthur had a simple answer whenever someone asked whether she had served in the military during World War Two. She said no. She brushed the question aside. And yet, after she passed away, records from the United States Marine Corps told a very different story, one that included a truck, a staff sergeant's stripes, and a young woman who was among the very first to answer her country's call. It is one of the most surprising discoveries in the history of classic television. Today we are counting down eight secrets about Bea Arthur that NBC never told the fans who tuned in to The Golden Girls every Saturday night. And our number one secret, the one hidden in those military files, along with the extraordinary gift she left behind, may give you a whole new respect for Dorothy Zbornak. So grab a slice of cheesecake, and let's head down to Miami.

Before she was Dorothy, before she was Maude, she was Bernice Frankel. She was born on May 13, 1922, in New York City, to Philip and Rebecca Frankel. When she was still a little girl, the family moved to Cambridge, Maryland, a small town on the Eastern Shore of the Chesapeake Bay, where her parents ran a women's clothing store. Bernice grew tall early. By her teenage years she was already around five foot nine, with a deep voice that made her stand out in any room. She later said in interviews that she felt awkward and self-conscious as a girl, and that she discovered she could make people laugh as a way to protect herself. That instinct would one day make her one of the most beloved comic actresses in America.

Secret number eight. The name Bea Arthur came from a husband, and she changed the spelling.

Bernice Frankel did not become Bea Arthur by accident or by a studio publicity department. In the 1940s, she married a man named Robert Alan Aurthur, a writer who later became a well-known television and film producer and screenwriter. Aurthur was spelled with a U in the middle. When Bernice decided to use the name professionally, she kept it but simplified the spelling to A-R-T-H-U-R. Their marriage did not last, but the name stayed with her for the rest of her life.

Her longest marriage came next. In 1950, she married Gene Saks, a young actor who would go on to become one of the most successful directors on Broadway, winning Tony Awards for directing plays such as I Love My Wife, Brighton Beach Memoirs, and Biloxi Blues. Bea and Gene adopted two sons, Matthew and Daniel, in the 1960s. They were married for twenty-eight years before divorcing in 1978. Gene Saks directed Bea in some of her most important work, including the Broadway musical Mame and the 1974 film version. So when you hear the name Bea Arthur, you are actually hearing a little piece of her first marriage, and her career was shaped in huge ways by her second.

Secret number seven. Bea Arthur studied acting alongside some of the most famous names of the twentieth century.

After the war, Bea moved back to New York and enrolled in the Dramatic Workshop at the New School for Social Research. The workshop was led by Erwin Piscator, a celebrated German director who had fled Nazi Germany. In the years right after the war, the Dramatic Workshop became one of the most important training grounds in American theater. Among the students who passed through it in that era were Marlon Brando, Tony Curtis, Rod Steiger, Harry Belafonte, Walter Matthau, and Elaine Stritch. Bea was right there in that remarkable generation.

She began her career in off-Broadway productions and serious theater, playing classical roles and appearing in works by playwrights like Bertolt Brecht. In 1954 she appeared in a famous off-Broadway revival of The Threepenny Opera, playing Lucy Brown, alongside Lotte Lenya, the widow of the composer Kurt Weill. Bea also worked as a nightclub singer and appeared in early television variety shows, including Sid Caesar's programs. People who only know her as the sharp-tongued Dorothy are often astonished to learn that she had a rich, powerful singing voice. She was not just a comedienne. She was a classically trained stage actress who could sing Brecht and Weill.

Secret number six. Bea Arthur was the very first Yente the Matchmaker, and she won a Tony playing Lucille Ball's role before Lucille Ball played it.

In 1964, a new musical opened on Broadway called Fiddler on the Roof. Zero Mostel starred as Tevye, the milkman. And the role of Yente, the busybody village matchmaker, was originated by Bea Arthur. Fiddler went on to become one of the longest-running musicals in Broadway history, and Bea's Yente became the template for every production that followed. Interestingly, according to accounts of the production, some of Yente's material was trimmed as the show developed, and Bea did not stay with it for its long run.

Two years later, in 1966, she opened in Mame, the musical version of Auntie Mame, with Angela Lansbury in the title role. Bea played Vera Charles, Mame's boozy, glamorous best friend, a stage actress with a cutting wit. Bea and Angela sang a duet called Bosom Buddies, a hilarious song in which the two friends insult each other while professing their eternal friendship. Bea won the Tony Award for Best Featured Actress in a Musical for that role, and Angela Lansbury won Best Actress. The show was directed by none other than Bea's husband, Gene Saks.

When Hollywood made the film version of Mame in 1974, Angela Lansbury was passed over for the lead, and the role went to Lucille Ball. But Bea Arthur was brought back to play Vera. So for one strange and wonderful moment in 1974, you had Lucy and Maude on the big screen together, trading barbs in Bosom Buddies. The film was not a critical success, but Bea's performance is still a highlight for fans of both women.

Secret number five. Bea Arthur appeared in Star Wars, and most fans have never seen it.

This might be the most surprising credit on her entire résumé. In November of 1978, CBS aired a two-hour television special called The Star Wars Holiday Special. It brought back Mark Hamill, Harrison Ford, Carrie Fisher, and the rest of the movie cast, and it centered on Chewbacca's family trying to celebrate a holiday called Life Day. The special also featured variety acts and guest stars. Among them was Bea Arthur.

Bea played a character named Ackmena, the bartender at the famous Mos Eisley cantina from the original film. In her segment, the Empire imposes a curfew, and Ackmena has to convince the cantina's strange alien patrons to go home. She does it by singing a song, set to the tune of the cantina music, called Goodnight, But Not Goodbye. And one of the patrons, a lovesick alien, has a crush on her.

The special aired only once and was never officially rerun or released, and George Lucas reportedly has expressed his regret over it many times. It became a strange piece of television legend, passed around on bootleg tapes for decades. But many fans who have seen it say that Bea Arthur's segment is the best part of the whole thing. She played it completely straight, with total commitment, and she sang beautifully.

Secret number four. Before The Golden Girls, Bea Arthur starred in one of the most controversial sitcom episodes ever broadcast.

In December of 1971, Bea guest-starred on All in the Family as Edith Bunker's cousin, Maude Findlay. Maude was a loud, proud, outspoken liberal who could argue Archie Bunker into the ground. Viewers loved her, and CBS executives reportedly asked Norman Lear whether she could have her own show. In September of 1972, Maude premiered on CBS, with Bea Arthur as a suburban woman in Tuckahoe, New York, married to her fourth husband, Walter, played by Bill Macy. Its theme song, And Then There's Maude, compared her to Lady Godiva, Joan of Arc, and Betsy Ross.

Just two months after the premiere, in November of 1972, Maude aired a two-part story called Maude's Dilemma. In it, Maude, who is forty-seven years old, discovers she is pregnant and, after much discussion with her husband, decides to have an abortion. This was two months before the Supreme Court's decision in Roe versus Wade. Abortion had just been made legal in the state of New York, where the show was set, and the episode treated the decision seriously. The reaction was enormous. Some CBS affiliates refused to air the episodes, and when the network reran them the following summer, protests and sponsor withdrawals followed.

Maude also dealt with Maude's struggles with depression and her husband Walter's alcoholism, which were remarkably serious topics for a sitcom in the 1970s. Bea handled all of it with a combination of fierce comic timing and real emotion. In 1977, she won the Emmy for Outstanding Lead Actress in a Comedy Series. Maude ran for six seasons, until 1978, and it made Bea Arthur a household name and a symbol of the strong, outspoken women of the decade. That would make her next role even harder to say yes to.

Secret number three. Bea Arthur almost turned down The Golden Girls, and two of her co-stars nearly played each other's parts.

In the early 1980s, NBC executive Brandon Tartikoff reportedly had an idea for a show about older women after watching a comedy sketch featuring two older actresses, Selma Diamond and Doris Roberts, at a network promotional event. The concept went to writer Susan Harris, who had created Soap, and she developed it with producers Paul Junger Witt and Tony Thomas. The show would be about four women sharing a house in Miami.

Susan Harris reportedly wrote the role of Dorothy with a Bea Arthur type in mind. When it came time to cast, Bea was hesitant. According to accounts from the production, her first reaction was concern that Dorothy was too similar to Maude, and that audiences would see her as repeating herself. There was another issue. Rue McClanahan, who had played Maude's best friend Vivian on Maude, had been considered for the role of Rose, the sweet, naive widow from Minnesota. And Betty White, famous as the man-hungry Sue Ann Nivens on The Mary Tyler Moore Show, had been considered for Blanche, the flirtatious Southern belle.

According to the often-told story, the director of the pilot, Jay Sandrich, suggested switching them. He felt that if Betty played another man-hungry woman, audiences would think she was simply doing Sue Ann again, and that Rue would be a better fit for Blanche. The switch was made. With the roles flipped and Estelle Getty cast as Dorothy's mother, Sophia, she agreed. Here is a fun fact about that casting. Estelle Getty was actually younger than Bea Arthur in real life, by more than a year. It took hours in the makeup chair to make her look like Bea's mother.

The Golden Girls premiered on NBC on September 14, 1985. It was an instant hit. It finished in the Nielsen top ten for six of its seven seasons, and all four of its stars won Emmy Awards for their roles, an extraordinary accomplishment. Bea won hers in 1988, for Outstanding Lead Actress in a Comedy Series. The show also won the Emmy for Outstanding Comedy Series twice. Not bad for a role Bea Arthur almost walked away from.

Secret number two. Bea Arthur chose to leave The Golden Girls while it was still popular, and behind the scenes, she and Betty White were reportedly very different people.

By 1992, The Golden Girls had run for seven seasons. Bea was approaching seventy, and she reportedly felt that the show had told its story and that she wanted to leave on a high note. The series finale, One Flew Out of the Cuckoo's Nest, aired on May 9, 1992. In it, Dorothy marries Blanche's uncle Lucas, played by Leslie Nielsen, and moves away to Atlanta. The final goodbyes between Dorothy and the other women, and especially between Dorothy and Sophia, left millions of viewers in tears.

The other three women continued in a spinoff called The Golden Palace, which moved to CBS in the fall of 1992. In it, Rose, Blanche, and Sophia buy a hotel in Miami. Bea appeared in a two-part guest appearance, but the show lasted only one season.

Now, about Betty. Over the years, fans have heard many stories about the relationship between Bea Arthur and Betty White. On screen they were perfect together. Off screen, according to Betty White's own later interviews, she felt that Bea did not always care for her. Betty said she was never sure why, though she guessed that her own cheerful, upbeat personality between takes may have rubbed Bea the wrong way. People who worked on the show have described Bea as a serious professional who liked to concentrate on the work, while Betty liked to chat with the studio audience and keep things light. Neither style was wrong. They were just different. And in later years, Betty spoke of Bea with real affection and respect for her talent, and the two women did reunite on stage and at events. Whatever their personal differences, they gave the world one of the finest ensembles ever assembled.

One more lovely detail. Bea famously preferred to go barefoot whenever she could, and she disliked wearing shoes. She did it on stage, in rehearsals, and even at public appearances. In 2002, she brought her one-woman show, Bea Arthur on Broadway: Just Between Friends, to New York, telling stories and singing songs from her career, and she earned a Tony Award nomination for it. She was eighty years old, and she was still a star.

It is worth taking a moment to appreciate just how special the Dorothy Zbornak character really was, because so much of what made her work came from Bea herself. Dorothy was a substitute teacher, divorced from her husband Stan, a cheating novelty salesman played to perfection by Herb Edelman. She was the practical one in the house, the one who paid attention to the bills, the one who saw through Blanche's romantic stories and Rose's St. Olaf tales with a single raised eyebrow. And she was the daughter who could never quite win an argument with her own mother.

The relationship between Dorothy and Sophia became the emotional backbone of the series. Estelle Getty, a stage actress who had spent years in New York theater before her breakthrough in Harvey Fierstein's play Torch Song Trilogy, made Sophia a tiny, sharp-tongued Sicilian powerhouse with a wicker purse and no filter at all. Bea and Estelle had a wonderful rhythm together. Sophia would deliver an insult, Dorothy would freeze, and Bea would turn slowly toward the audience with that famous look. Those silent reactions were sometimes funnier than any line in the script. Directors and writers on the show often said that Bea could get a laugh simply by holding a pause longer than anyone else would dare.

Behind the scenes, the writers' room on The Golden Girls was a remarkable place in its own right. A number of young writers who worked on the series went on to great success elsewhere, and several of them have said that writing for Bea was both a privilege and a challenge, because she had such precise instincts about what was funny and what was not. If a joke did not ring true for Dorothy, according to those writers, Bea would let them know. She cared about the character, and she protected her.

The show also tackled serious subjects, just as Maude had a decade earlier. The Golden Girls dealt with aging, loneliness, chronic fatigue syndrome, which Dorothy suffered from in a two-part story, as well as homelessness, prejudice, and the fear of AIDS. In one celebrated episode, Dorothy's friend Jean, a lesbian, comes to visit and develops feelings for Rose, and the episode handled the situation with warmth and humor at a time when few network shows would touch the subject. Bea fought for that kind of storytelling throughout her career.

And then there was the cheesecake. Late-night kitchen scenes, with the four women gathered around the table sharing a dessert and talking through their problems, became the show's signature. Fans have reported that, according to cast interviews, Bea did not actually enjoy cheesecake. Whether that is true or a bit of happy legend, it is a perfect example of what a professional she was. Week after week, she sat at that table, picked up her fork, and made millions of people believe these four women truly loved one another.

Secret number one. Bea Arthur served in the United States Marine Corps during World War Two, and she left behind a gift that is still saving young lives today.

As we mentioned at the start, Bea Arthur often denied that she had served in the military. But in 2010, the year after her death, her Marine Corps service records were made public. They revealed that in 1943, at the age of twenty-one, Bernice Frankel enlisted in the Marine Corps Women's Reserve, which had just been created that year. She was one of the first women to join. During her service, she worked as a typist and later as a truck driver and dispatcher, and she was stationed at bases including the Marine Corps Air Station at Cherry Point, North Carolina. By the time she was honorably discharged in 1945, she had risen to the rank of staff sergeant.

Her file reportedly even included candid remarks from her superiors about her strong personality, and fans have joked ever since that the Marine Corps met Maude Findlay years before Norman Lear ever did. Why did Bea keep it quiet? No one knows for certain. Some have suggested she simply considered it private, or that she preferred not to discuss that period of her life. Whatever her reasons, she was part of a generation of American women who stepped forward when their country needed them, and she deserves to be remembered for it.

And that was not the only quiet act of service in her life. Bea Arthur was a passionate advocate for causes she believed in. She was a committed supporter of animal rights and worked with organizations to protect animals. She also became a devoted champion for LGBT people at a time when many celebrities stayed silent. She had a large and loyal following in the gay community, and she returned that love. She spoke out during the AIDS crisis, at a time when many people were afraid to.

Bea Arthur passed away from cancer on April 25, 2009, at her home in Los Angeles. She was eighty-six. In her will, she left three hundred thousand dollars to the Ali Forney Center in New York City, an organization that provides housing and support for homeless LGBT young people. Many of those young people had been rejected by their own families. In 2017, the center opened a residence for homeless youth and named it the Bea Arthur Residence. To this day, young people who had nowhere else to go have found shelter under a roof bearing her name.

So when you remember Bea Arthur, remember Dorothy's withering stare, and her deadpan delivery, and those moments when she would simply look at the camera and let the audience laugh. Remember Maude's thunderous "God'll get you for that, Walter." But also remember the tall, shy girl from Maryland who learned to make people laugh, the young Marine driving trucks during the war, the Tony-winning Broadway star, and the woman who, even after she was gone, made sure that kids who had lost everything would have a place to call home. Thank you for being a friend, Bea.
