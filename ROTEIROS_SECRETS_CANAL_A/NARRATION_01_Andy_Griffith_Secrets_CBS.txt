In the spring of 1968, The Andy Griffith Show finished its eighth season as the number one program in America. Not top ten. Number one. And yet the man whose name was on the title never won a single Emmy for playing Sheriff Andy Taylor. In fact, he was never even nominated. Five Emmys went to his best friend. One went to Aunt Bee. But Andy Griffith himself went home empty-handed every single year. That is just one of the surprising stories behind the quiet little town of Mayberry. Today we are counting down eight secrets about Andy Griffith that CBS never really told the fans who tuned in every week, and the number one secret, the decision that shocked television executives in 1968, may change the way you remember the show forever. So pour yourself a glass of sweet tea, settle into your favorite chair, and let's take a stroll down to the fishing hole.

Before Andy Griffith ever pinned a star to his shirt, he was a boy from Mount Airy, North Carolina. He was born on June 1, 1926, the only child of Carl and Geneva Griffith. The family did not have much. For a while, as an infant, Andy reportedly slept in a dresser drawer because his parents could not afford a crib. Mount Airy was a small textile and granite town tucked up against the Blue Ridge Mountains, and decades later people would point to it as the real-life inspiration for Mayberry. Andy himself was always careful about that. He said many times that Mayberry was not a copy of Mount Airy, but he admitted the names, the voices, and the feeling of the place came straight out of his childhood. And that brings us to our first secret.

Secret number eight. Andy Griffith was not supposed to be a comedian at all. He was supposed to be a preacher.

As a teenager, Andy fell in love with music. He learned to play the trombone through a local Moravian minister named Ed Mickey, who taught brass to the young people of the town. Andy admired that man so deeply that when he enrolled at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, he intended to study for the ministry. He planned to become a Moravian preacher. But somewhere along the way the stage pulled harder than the pulpit. Andy switched his major to music, sang in campus productions, and joined the university's theater and opera groups. He graduated in 1949 with a degree in music, and for a short time he actually taught music and drama at a high school in Goldsboro, North Carolina.

Meanwhile, every summer from 1947 to 1953, Andy performed in an outdoor drama called The Lost Colony, staged on Roanoke Island along the North Carolina coast. The play tells the story of the English settlers who vanished from Roanoke in the 1580s, and Andy worked his way up through the cast until he landed the role of Sir Walter Raleigh. That island would stay in his heart for the rest of his life. He eventually made his home in Manteo, right there on Roanoke Island, and that is where he would spend his final years. So the next time you hear Andy Taylor strumming his guitar on the front porch and singing an old hymn, remember that the man behind the character really did train for a life of music and faith. Those songs were not an act. They were who he was.

Secret number seven. Before he ever played a sheriff, Andy Griffith became famous for a comedy record about football.

In the early 1950s, Andy and his first wife, Barbara Edwards, were touring the South with a little stage act. They sang, he told stories, and they played for civic clubs and conventions. One of Andy's monologues was a routine about a simple country preacher who wanders into a college football game without having the faintest idea what he is watching. He describes the players as a bunch of fellas fighting over a "punkin," and he cannot understand why everybody is so excited about it. The routine was called What It Was, Was Football.

In 1953, a small North Carolina label recorded it, and it became so popular regionally that Capitol Records picked it up. The record went on to sell somewhere around eight hundred thousand copies, a remarkable number for a spoken-word comedy album at the time. Suddenly this young music teacher was being booked on The Ed Sullivan Show. He was a national novelty act, and New York started paying attention.

That attention led him to Broadway. In 1955, Andy starred in No Time for Sergeants, playing a lovable Georgia farm boy named Will Stockdale who gets drafted into the Air Force. The show was a hit, and Andy earned a Tony Award nomination for his performance. In 1958 he starred in the movie version for Warner Brothers, and in that film, in a small role as a nervous corporal, was a skinny young actor from West Virginia named Don Knotts. Keep that name in mind. It is going to matter a great deal.

Secret number six. Before he was everybody's favorite small-town sheriff, Andy Griffith played one of the darkest villains of 1950s cinema.

If you have only ever known Andy as the gentle, wise father figure of Mayberry, you owe it to yourself to see a 1957 film called A Face in the Crowd. It was directed by Elia Kazan, the man who had just made On the Waterfront, and it was written by Budd Schulberg. Andy played a character named Larry "Lonesome" Rhodes, a drifter discovered in an Arkansas jail who becomes a television and radio sensation. As his fame grows, Lonesome Rhodes turns into an arrogant, manipulative, power-hungry monster who uses his folksy charm to fool the public and influence politicians.

It is a ferocious performance. Critics at the time were stunned, and many film historians today consider it one of the great screen debuts of the decade. According to accounts of the production, Kazan pushed Andy extremely hard to reach the emotional extremes the role demanded, and Andy later said the experience shook him. He reportedly told people that playing someone that ugly inside was difficult to shake off at the end of the day.

Here is the fascinating part. Just three years later, Andy would become the face of American decency on CBS. The same man who played a fraud deceiving television audiences became perhaps the most trusted figure television audiences ever had. Some viewers who grew up on Mayberry have watched A Face in the Crowd later in life and simply could not believe it was the same actor. That range tells you something important about Andy Griffith. He was never just a nice fellow reading lines. He was a serious, gifted actor who chose warmth.

Secret number five. The Andy Griffith Show began as an episode of a completely different program, and in the beginning, Andy was the one telling the jokes.

The first time America ever saw Sheriff Andy Taylor was not in his own show. It was on February 15, 1960, in an episode of The Danny Thomas Show, also known as Make Room for Daddy. The episode was called Danny Meets Andy Griffith. In the story, Danny Thomas is passing through a little town called Mayberry, gets pulled over for running a stop sign, and lands in front of the local sheriff, who also happens to be the justice of the peace and the editor of the newspaper. Ronny Howard appeared as little Opie, and Frances Bavier was in the episode too, though in a different role than the one she would later make famous.

This was what television people call a backdoor pilot. Sheldon Leonard, who produced The Danny Thomas Show, used it as a test to see how audiences would respond to this country sheriff. The response was terrific. General Foods signed on as a sponsor, and The Andy Griffith Show premiered on CBS on Monday night, October 3, 1960.

But in that first season, the Andy Taylor you see is a little different from the one you remember. He is broader and more of a cut-up. He plays the hayseed, telling tall tales and getting most of the big laughs. Then something happened. Don Knotts, that skinny actor from No Time for Sergeants, had seen the pilot and reportedly called Andy to ask whether the sheriff might need a deputy. He was cast as Barney Fife, and very quickly Andy realized something that many stars would never have the humility to admit. Don was funnier. Andy later explained in interviews that he understood he should play straight man to Don, that his job was to stand there and react while Barney exploded with nervous energy.

That choice is the foundation of everything we love about the show. Andy Taylor became the calm center of Mayberry. He became the patient father to Opie, the steady hand on Barney's shoulder, the man who solved problems with a quiet word instead of a gun. In fact, Andy Taylor famously did not carry a gun most of the time, and Barney was only allowed one bullet, which he kept in his shirt pocket. A lesser star might have fought to keep the laughs for himself. Andy gave them away, and the show became a classic because of it.

Secret number four. Don Knotts left Mayberry at the height of its success because of a promise Andy made out loud.

The partnership of Andy and Barney is one of the greatest in television history. Don Knotts won five Emmy Awards for playing Barney Fife, in 1961, 1962, 1963, 1966, and 1967. The last two came for guest appearances after he had already left the show. So why did he leave?

According to Don Knotts himself, the answer goes back to something Andy said early on. Andy had told people, including Don, that he planned to do The Andy Griffith Show for five years and then stop. Don took him at his word. As the fifth season approached its end, Don began looking ahead and signed a film contract with Universal Pictures. Then Andy changed his mind and decided to keep the show going. By then, Don's movie deal was done. In his memoir, Don wrote that he was surprised, and that if he had known Andy was going to continue, he might have stayed.

Don Knotts went on to make The Ghost and Mr. Chicken in 1966, The Reluctant Astronaut, The Shakiest Gun in the West, and a string of family comedies that did very well. But fans of Mayberry felt the loss deeply. The show moved to color in its sixth season, in the fall of 1965, and Barney was gone. Writers brought in new faces, including Jack Burns as Deputy Warren Ferguson, who did not last. Later seasons leaned more on Opie, Aunt Bee, Goober, Howard Sprague, and Emmett the fix-it man.

What matters most is that the friendship survived. Andy and Don remained close for the rest of Don's life. Don returned for guest appearances, he later joined Andy on Matlock, and the two reunited for the 1986 television movie Return to Mayberry, which became one of the highest-rated TV movies of that season. When Don Knotts passed away in February of 2006, Andy was reportedly at his bedside in the hospital, and he spoke of him afterward with deep emotion, calling him his best friend. Barney may have left Mayberry, but Don never left Andy.

Secret number three. Behind the scenes, Andy and Aunt Bee did not always get along.

On screen, Frances Bavier was the very picture of a loving aunt. Aunt Bee cooked the fried chicken, entered the pickle contest, worried about Opie, and fussed over Andy. Off screen, the story was more complicated. Frances Bavier was a trained stage actress from New York, a graduate of the American Academy of Dramatic Arts, who had worked on Broadway and in films for decades before she ever came to Mayberry. She took her craft very seriously, and by many accounts, she was a private and sensitive person who did not care for the joking, loose atmosphere that Andy and the cast often enjoyed on set.

Andy spoke about this candidly in later years. He said in interviews that the two of them sometimes clashed and that he felt she did not always enjoy working with him. He did not speak ill of her talent. He always praised her work. And her work was honored. In 1967, Frances Bavier won the Emmy for Outstanding Performance by an Actress in a Supporting Role in a Comedy. Remember that detail, because it brings us closer to our next secret.

When the show ended, Frances Bavier continued on in the spinoff Mayberry R.F.D. for a couple of seasons before retiring. Then she did something surprising. She moved from Hollywood to the small town of Siler City, North Carolina, and lived there quietly for the rest of her life. She became something of a recluse, living with her many cats, and rarely gave interviews.

But there is a touching postscript. According to Andy's own account, near the end of her life, Frances Bavier telephoned him. He said she told him she was sorry that she had been difficult during their years together. Andy said he was grateful for that call. Frances Bavier passed away in December of 1989 and was laid to rest in Siler City, in the very state that gave the world Mayberry. Her headstone, fans are often moved to learn, carries a reference to the character of Aunt Bee. Whatever happened between them on the set, the two of them created a family that millions of Americans still feel a part of.

Secret number two. Andy Griffith was never nominated for an Emmy for The Andy Griffith Show. Not once.

Let that sink in. For eight seasons, Andy Griffith was the anchor of one of the most successful sitcoms in American history. The show never finished a season lower than seventh in the Nielsen ratings, and it closed its run at number one. Don Knotts won five Emmys. Frances Bavier won one. And Andy, the man holding the entire thing together, was never nominated for his performance as Sheriff Taylor.

How could that happen? Part of the explanation is the role itself. When Andy decided to become the straight man, he took on the hardest and least glamorous job in comedy. The straight man rarely gets the big laugh. He makes everyone around him look good. Award voters, then and now, tend to reward the loud, showy performance, and Andy's work was so natural and so relaxed that many people simply did not notice how skillful it was. He made it look like he wasn't acting at all.

Andy, for his part, rarely complained publicly. But the snub is a well-documented piece of television trivia, and many fans feel it is one of the great oversights in Emmy history. Recognition did come later in his life from other places. In 1996, he released a gospel album called I Love to Tell the Story, a collection of the old hymns he had sung since his boyhood. It sold very well and won a Grammy Award for Best Southern, Country, or Bluegrass Gospel Album. And in 2005, President George W. Bush awarded Andy Griffith the Presidential Medal of Freedom, the highest civilian honor in the United States. The citation honored him for a lifetime of performances that reflected the best of American values. So perhaps the man from Mount Airy did not need a television statue after all. He received something far greater.

Secret number one. Andy Griffith walked away from the number one show in America by his own choice, and the years that followed tested him in ways fans never knew.

When The Andy Griffith Show aired its final episode on April 1, 1968, it was the top-rated program on television. Only a handful of shows in history have ended their runs at number one. I Love Lucy did it. Seinfeld would do it decades later. And The Andy Griffith Show did it. CBS did not cancel it. The network would have happily kept it going for years. It was Andy who decided it was time to stop.

He reportedly felt he had said everything he could say with the character, and that he wanted to try new things, especially in film. CBS kept the town alive by turning the show into Mayberry R.F.D., starring Ken Berry as Sam Jones, a widowed farmer and town councilman. The cast of Goober, Howard, Emmett, and Aunt Bee carried on, and Andy appeared in the early episodes to help pass the torch. Mayberry R.F.D. was itself a ratings success and ran until 1971, when CBS canceled it along with many of its rural shows in what became known as the rural purge.

But for Andy, life after Mayberry was hard. His movie career did not take off the way he hoped. He returned to television with a 1970 series called Headmaster, and then The New Andy Griffith Show in 1971. Both were short-lived. Through the 1970s, he worked steadily in TV movies and miniseries, often playing tough or even villainous roles, but he never again found the kind of success he had walked away from. His first marriage ended in divorce in 1972, and a second marriage, to Solica Cassuto, also ended. He later married Cindi Knight in 1983, and she would remain by his side for the rest of his life.

Then, in 1983, came the frightening part of the story. Andy was struck by Guillain-Barré syndrome, a rare disorder in which the body's immune system attacks the nerves. He was left paralyzed from the waist down and in tremendous pain. He spent months in rehabilitation, learning to walk again. According to his own account, it took a long time before he could move normally, and there were moments when he wondered whether he would work again.

He did. In 1986, the same year as Return to Mayberry, NBC launched a legal drama called Matlock. Andy played Ben Matlock, a shrewd, folksy Atlanta defense attorney in a light gray suit who loved hot dogs and almost always uncovered the real killer in the courtroom. A whole new generation fell in love with him. Matlock ran for six seasons on NBC and then moved to ABC for three more, wrapping up in 1995. More than twenty-five years after leaving Mayberry, Andy Griffith was a television star all over again, and many of the viewers who watched Matlock had once been the children watching Opie.

Andy Griffith passed away on July 3, 2012, at his home on Roanoke Island in North Carolina. He was eighty-six years old. He was buried just hours later, in a private family ceremony, not far from the stage where he once played Sir Walter Raleigh in The Lost Colony. There was no grand Hollywood farewell. It was quiet, simple, and close to home. In other words, it was a very Mayberry way to say goodbye.

So the next time you catch a rerun and hear that whistled theme song, and you see Andy and Opie walking down to the fishing hole with their poles over their shoulders, remember what was behind that peaceful picture. A boy who once wanted to be a preacher. A comic who made a hit record about football. An actor who could play a monster but chose to play a good man. A star who gave away the laughs to his best friend, who walked away from number one, who learned to walk all over again, and who came back.

That music, by the way, was composed by Earle Hagen, and it was Hagen himself who whistled it. Lyrics were written for it too, under the title The Fishin' Hole, and Andy recorded them, but on the show you only ever heard the whistle. Somehow that was all it needed.
