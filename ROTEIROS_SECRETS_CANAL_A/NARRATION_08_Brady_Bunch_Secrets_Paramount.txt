Secret number eight. The Brady Bunch was inspired by a statistic in a newspaper, and the networks were nervous about one word: divorce.

The creator of The Brady Bunch was Sherwood Schwartz, the same man who had already given America Gilligan's Island. According to Schwartz, the idea came to him in the mid sixties when he read a newspaper item, reportedly in the Los Angeles Times, noting that a large share of marriages in the United States, around twenty-nine percent, included a child or children from a previous marriage. Blended families were becoming common, but almost no one on television was talking about them.

Schwartz wrote a script about a widower with three boys marrying a woman with three girls. He took it around to the networks in 1966, and they liked it, but each one wanted changes. Then, in 1968, a movie called Yours, Mine and Ours, starring Lucille Ball and Henry Fonda, came out with a very similar premise about a widow and widower blending their large families. It was a big hit. Schwartz reportedly worried people would think he'd copied it, but the movie's success actually helped convince ABC that audiences wanted this kind of story. The Brady Bunch premiered on ABC on September 26, 1969, produced by Paramount Television.

Now here's the part the network didn't want to discuss. Mike Brady was clearly a widower. But what happened to Carol's first husband? Schwartz reportedly wanted Carol to be a divorcee. In 1969, though, the network was uncomfortable with the idea of a divorced mother as the heroine of a family sitcom. So the show simply never said. In the pilot, there's no explanation of what happened to Carol's first husband. Was she widowed? Was she divorced? The Brady Bunch never told us, and that silence was a compromise with the network.

Secret number seven. The show was never a top ratings hit during its original run, and it didn't become a phenomenon until the reruns.

This one surprises almost everybody. The Brady Bunch feels like one of the most popular shows ever made. But during its original five seasons on ABC, from 1969 to 1974, it was never a top twenty hit in the Nielsen ratings. It did respectably, especially with children, but it was never a ratings powerhouse. When it was canceled in 1974, it wasn't a shocking loss for the network.

What made The Brady Bunch immortal was syndication. As soon as it went into reruns, it began airing in the afternoons on local stations across America, right when kids were getting home from school. A whole generation, and then another, grew up watching the Bradys every single day. That's how we all learned the theme song by heart. That's how lines like "Oh, my nose!" and "Marcia, Marcia, Marcia!" became part of American pop culture.

The show's afterlife became enormous. There were reunion movies, like The Brady Girls Get Married in 1981 and A Very Brady Christmas in 1988, which was the second highest rated TV movie of that season. There was a drama series, The Bradys, in 1990. There were two hit theatrical comedies in the 1990s, The Brady Bunch Movie in 1995 and A Very Brady Sequel in 1996, which lovingly poked fun at the show's wholesome innocence. A show that was never a smash in its original run became one of the most enduring franchises in television history.

Secret number six. That famous house didn't have a second story, and the bathroom on the show had no toilet.

The Brady house is one of the most recognizable homes in American television. The exterior shots were filmed at a real house in Studio City, California, on Dilling Street. But here's the catch. The real house is a split-level ranch, and it only has one main floor. In the show, of course, the Bradys had a big upstairs with bedrooms for the boys and girls. To make the exterior look more like it had a second story, the production reportedly added a fake window to the front of the house for filming.

The interior, meanwhile, was a set built on a soundstage at Paramount Studios in Hollywood. The famous floating staircase led up to bedrooms that existed only on the soundstage. If you ever wondered how the house in the outside shots could possibly fit everything inside, now you know. It couldn't.

And then there's the bathroom. The Brady kids shared a famous Jack and Jill bathroom between the boys' and girls' rooms, and it was the setting for many classic fights over who got to use it first. But if you look closely, you'll notice something missing. There's no toilet. In the late 1960s, network standards still considered it improper to show a toilet on a family sitcom. So America's most famous fictional bathroom had a sink, a tub, and a mirror, but nothing else.

The real house got a happy ending, by the way. In 2018, HGTV bought the house for about three and a half million dollars, well over the asking price. The network then renovated it to match the TV interiors as closely as possible, adding a second story, the floating staircase, and that orange kitchen. The six actors who played the Brady kids all came back to help, and the series A Very Brady Renovation aired in 2019. For the first time, the house on the outside finally matched the house on the inside.

Secret number five. Florence Henderson wasn't the first choice for Carol, and the shag hairdo she wore in the first season was actually a wig.

Florence Henderson became America's sweetheart as Carol Brady. But she wasn't the producer's first pick. According to accounts of the casting, Sherwood Schwartz reportedly favored actress Joyce Bulifant, who later appeared on The Mary Tyler Moore Show, for the role of Carol. And for Mike Brady, Schwartz is said to have wanted a then little-known actor named Gene Hackman, who would go on to win two Academy Awards. The studio reportedly preferred Robert Reed, who was already under contract with Paramount, and that decision would shape the show in ways no one could have predicted. Henderson, a Broadway star who had appeared in shows like Oklahoma on tour and The Sound of Music, was known more for musical theater than sitcoms. But she had warmth and a beautiful singing voice, and she won the part.

There's a funny detail about her first season look. Just before filming began, Henderson had cut her hair very short. The producers wanted Carol to have a longer, softer style, so for the first season she wore a wig in that famous flipped look. Once her own hair grew out, she wore the shag style that became one of the most copied haircuts of the early seventies.

Henderson stayed with the Brady family for decades, appearing in nearly every reunion and spin-off. She also became a beloved talk show host and appeared on Dancing with the Stars in 2010. She passed away in 2016, and the actors who played her TV children spoke about her as a true second mother. Maureen McCormick, who played Marcia, called her a mentor and a friend.

And the other mother figure in that house deserves her moment, too. Ann B. Davis, who played Alice Nelson, the housekeeper, was already an Emmy winner when she joined the show, having won two Emmy Awards for her role as Schultzy on The Bob Cummings Show in the 1950s. She brought a seasoned comedian's timing to every scene, and the kids adored her. Off camera, Davis was a deeply devoted woman of faith who later lived in a religious community in Colorado and then Texas. Alice was the heart of the Brady kitchen, and Ann B. Davis was the heart of the set.

Secret number four. While the cameras were off, the Brady kids were having real romances with each other, and even with their TV mom.

Here's a secret that came out years later, mostly thanks to a best-selling book. In 1992, Barry Williams, who played Greg Brady, published a memoir called Growing Up Brady: I Was a Teenage Greg. In it, he revealed that the Brady kids were going through all the normal ups and downs of adolescence, and some of their crushes were on each other.

According to his memoir, Williams and Maureen McCormick, who played his TV stepsister Marcia, shared a real romance at one point and kissed during a trip to Hawaii while filming the famous Hawaii episodes. Williams also wrote that Christopher Knight, who played Peter, and Eve Plumb, who played Jan, had a teenage romance of their own. And the youngest, Mike Lookinland, who played Bobby, and Susan Olsen, who played Cindy, reportedly had a sweet childhood crush too.

But the most surprising revelation in the book was about Florence Henderson. Williams wrote that as a teenager he had a crush on his TV mom, and that he once took her out on a date. According to his memoir, they went out to dinner and it was entirely innocent, and Henderson later confirmed it with good humor in interviews. She reportedly said she was flattered. It was one date, nothing more, but it became one of the most talked about Brady stories ever.

Williams also admitted in the book to a few teenage mistakes. According to his memoir, he once showed up to work on the set after smoking marijuana, and he said he was so nervous about how he'd do that he vowed never to do it again. Paramount certainly never mentioned any of this at the time. On screen, the Bradys were wholesome. Off screen, they were real kids growing up in Hollywood in the early seventies.

Secret number three. Behind Marcia Brady's perfect smile, Maureen McCormick was fighting a painful battle, which she later revealed in her own memoir.

Marcia Brady was the golden girl of the Brady family, the pretty, popular oldest daughter whose life seemed perfect. Maureen McCormick, who played her, became one of the biggest teen idols of the seventies. But in her 2008 memoir, Here's the Story: Surviving Marcia Brady and Finding My True Voice, McCormick opened up about what was happening behind the smile.

She wrote that after the show ended, she struggled with drug addiction, particularly cocaine, in the late seventies and early eighties. She also wrote about her battles with depression, bulimia, and the pressure of living up to Marcia's perfect image. She has said that people expected her to be Marcia forever, and that it was very hard to grow up in public as the ideal American teenager.

The happy part of her story is her recovery. McCormick sought help, found strength in her faith and her family, and has been married to her husband Michael Cummings since 1985. She has spoken openly about mental health to help others who might be struggling, and she's appeared on shows like Dancing with the Stars and the Brady renovation series. Her honesty has helped a lot of people see that even the brightest smiles can hide pain, and that it's never too late to find your way back.

And by the way, that famous football to the nose? In the episode "The Subject Was Noses," Marcia gets hit in the face by a football thrown by Peter, and her nose swells up right before a big date. "Oh, my nose!" remains one of the most quoted lines in sitcom history.

The Brady kids weren't just actors, either. They were recording artists. Under the name The Brady Kids, the six young stars recorded several albums in the early seventies, and songs like "Sunshine Day" and "Keep On" were featured right in the episodes. The singing wasn't always polished, and the cast members themselves have laughed about it over the years, but the records sold, and the Brady Kids even got their own Saturday morning cartoon series on ABC in 1972. Florence Henderson, with her Broadway training, was the real singer of the family, and she often helped coach the kids.

And then there were the famous trips. In the fourth season premiere, the Bradys traveled to Hawaii for a three-part adventure. Bobby finds a small tiki idol that's said to bring bad luck, and suddenly everything goes wrong. Greg wipes out surfing, a heavy wall decoration nearly falls on Alice, and a tarantula crawls onto Peter. The episodes even featured horror legend Vincent Price as an eccentric archaeologist. The show also took the family to the Grand Canyon, and Hawaii and the Grand Canyon trips are still among the most beloved episodes ever made. For kids watching at home in the early seventies, a family vacation like that seemed like the most glamorous thing in the world.

Secret number two. The final season brought in Cousin Oliver, and the Brady Bunch ended without anyone knowing it was over.

By the fifth season, which aired from 1973 to 1974, the Brady kids were growing up. Barry Williams was nearly twenty. Even little Cindy and Bobby weren't so little anymore. The producers worried that the show was losing the cute young kid appeal that drew in younger viewers. So in the final season, they introduced a new character, Cousin Oliver, played by Robbie Rist, a young boy who came to live with the Bradys.

It didn't work. Oliver was widely seen as unnecessary, and the term "Cousin Oliver" became TV slang for a cute new kid added to a show to try to recapture its youthful appeal, usually near the end of a show's run. The Brady Bunch was canceled after that season. And here's the strangest part. Nobody involved knew that the last episode they filmed would be the series finale. There was no goodbye episode. The last episode to air was called "The Hair-Brained Scheme," in which Greg dyes his hair orange right before graduation. It was a silly ending for a show that would go on to mean so much to so many people.

Many fans don't know that the story of that final episode connects directly to our number one secret, because one member of the family refused to be in it.

Secret number one. Robert Reed, America's favorite TV dad, reportedly despised the show's scripts, and he kept a deeply personal secret his entire life.

Robert Reed played Mike Brady, the wise, kind architect who always had the perfect lesson at the end of every episode. But off camera, Reed's relationship with The Brady Bunch was famously complicated. Reed was a classically trained actor. He had studied at Northwestern University and at the Royal Academy of Dramatic Art in London, and he had been a star of the courtroom drama The Defenders, playing Kenneth Preston alongside E. G. Marshall. He considered himself a serious actor, and by many accounts he felt the Brady scripts were silly and unrealistic.

According to Sherwood Schwartz and others who worked on the show, Reed regularly wrote long memos to the producers, criticizing scripts for things like scientific inaccuracies or illogical plots. Schwartz's son Lloyd Schwartz, who also worked on the show, later shared some of those memos publicly. Reed argued about everything from how a character would really behave to whether a plot made any sense at all.

The tension came to a head with that final episode, "The Hair-Brained Scheme." Reed reportedly objected so strongly to the script that he refused to appear in it, and the episode was filmed without Mike Brady. Sherwood Schwartz later said that if the show had been renewed for a sixth season, he planned to write Mike out and replace him with another actor. Reed was fortunate, in a way, that the show was canceled.

But here's the beautiful and surprising thing. Despite all his complaints, Robert Reed loved the cast. He came back for nearly every Brady reunion, including The Brady Bunch Hour variety show in 1976 and 1977, The Brady Girls Get Married, A Very Brady Christmas, and The Bradys. The young actors who played his TV children have described him as a true father figure, generous and protective, who took them to plays, taught them about acting, and genuinely cared about them. Barry Williams and Florence Henderson both spoke about him with great affection.

And then there was the secret. Robert Reed was gay, and in an era when coming out could end an actor's career, he kept it private. Reed had been married once, in the 1950s, and had a daughter, Karen. But as an adult he lived a private life that Hollywood, and the public, knew little about. Some of his castmates knew or suspected, but they protected his privacy. In the 1970s, being openly gay as the star of a family sitcom would have been unthinkable to the network and to the studio.

Robert Reed passed away in May 1992 at the age of fifty-nine. His death certificate listed colon cancer as the primary cause, and it was later reported that he had also been HIV positive. The news came as a shock to many fans who had grown up watching Mike Brady. Florence Henderson said that she'd had no idea about his illness, and the Brady family, on screen and off, mourned him deeply. Barry Williams later said that Reed was one of the most important people in his life.

Looking back, there's something very moving about Robert Reed's story. Here was a man who spent years playing the ideal American father, giving gentle lessons about honesty and acceptance, while he couldn't safely be honest about who he was. And yet he gave the young actors around him exactly what Mike Brady gave his kids, guidance, kindness, and love. In the end, he may have been more like Mike Brady than he ever wanted to admit.
