On a Tuesday night in January of 1971, CBS was so nervous about a new comedy that it put a warning on the screen before the very first episode. The announcement told viewers that the program they were about to see sought to throw a humorous spotlight on our frailties, prejudices, and concerns. Network executives reportedly stationed extra operators by the phones, bracing for a flood of angry calls. The show was All in the Family. The man in the armchair was Carroll O'Connor. And what almost nobody watching knew was that the actor playing America's most famous loudmouth was, in real life, a soft-spoken, well-read man who had studied in Dublin, taught school in New York, and held political views almost exactly opposite to Archie Bunker's. Today we are counting down eight secrets about Carroll O'Connor that CBS never really told the fans. And our number one secret is not about a script or a contract at all. It is the most personal fight of his life, and it is a story every fan deserves to hear. So pull up a chair. Just not that one. That one's Archie's.

Carroll O'Connor was born on August 2, 1924, in the Bronx, New York. His father, Edward, was a lawyer, and his mother, Elise, was a schoolteacher. The family later moved to Forest Hills in Queens. That is worth remembering, because Archie Bunker lived in Queens too, at 704 Hauser Street in the fictional Astoria neighborhood. Carroll knew those blocks, those accents, and those working men. He grew up surrounded by the very people Archie would one day represent on television. But Carroll's own path would take him far from Queens before it brought him back.

Secret number eight. Before he was Archie Bunker, Carroll O'Connor went to sea during World War Two.

When America entered the war, young Carroll wanted to serve, and he joined the United States Merchant Marine. The Merchant Marine was the fleet of civilian ships that carried troops, fuel, food, and weapons across oceans patrolled by enemy submarines. It was dangerous duty. Merchant mariners suffered a casualty rate that, by many estimates, was higher proportionally than most branches of the armed forces. Carroll sailed aboard cargo and troop ships during the war years, and those experiences, of long nights, tough shipmates, and real danger, stayed with him.

So when Archie Bunker bragged about his service in World War Two, and when he lectured his son-in-law Mike about patriotism, there was a man under that character who had actually been out there on the water during the war. Carroll did not talk about it much. But it gave him an understanding of the generation he was portraying that no amount of acting class could have taught.

Secret number seven. Carroll O'Connor fell in love in Montana and got married in Ireland.

After the war, Carroll enrolled at the University of Montana in Missoula. He wrote for the campus newspaper and became involved in theater. It was there that he met a fellow student named Nancy Fields, an art student. The two fell in love. Carroll later went to Ireland to study at University College Dublin, and Nancy followed. They were married in Dublin in 1951, and they would stay married for the rest of his life, fifty years in all. In a business known for short marriages, Carroll and Nancy were a remarkable exception.

In Ireland, Carroll began acting professionally. He performed with the Gate Theatre in Dublin and toured with companies around Ireland and Britain. He fell deeply in love with Irish literature, with Joyce and Yeats and the great playwrights, and he would carry that love for the rest of his life. When he returned to the United States, acting work was not steady at first. To support his family, he became a substitute teacher in the New York City public schools, teaching English. Picture it. The man who would become famous for mangling the English language with his famous slip-ups and mixed-up words that fans came to call Bunkerisms was, in real life, an English teacher who could quote poetry from memory.

Secret number six. Archie Bunker was nearly played by someone else, and the show was rejected twice before CBS took a chance.

All in the Family was adapted by producer Norman Lear from a British comedy called Till Death Us Do Part, which aired on the BBC and featured a bigoted working-class father named Alf Garnett. Lear saw the potential in an American version and developed it first for ABC. The first pilot, filmed in 1968, was called Justice for All, and the family's name was Justice. Carroll O'Connor played the father, Archie, and Jean Stapleton played his wife, Edith. But the actors playing the daughter and son-in-law were different from the ones we know. ABC was not convinced. Lear filmed a second pilot in 1969, now titled Those Were the Days, with yet another young couple. ABC passed again.

Before Carroll got the role, Lear had reportedly considered other actors for Archie. According to Lear's own recollections, one of them was Mickey Rooney, who is said to have turned the part down, reportedly believing that a show like this would get everyone involved shot. Whether or not those exact words were said, it shows just how dangerous the material seemed at the time.

Then CBS stepped in. The network's new president, Robert Wood, wanted to move away from the rural comedies that had dominated the schedule and attract younger, urban viewers. He bought the show. With Rob Reiner now cast as Mike Stivic and Sally Struthers as Gloria, All in the Family premiered on CBS on January 12, 1971. The ratings in those first weeks were not spectacular. But over the summer, as reruns aired and word of mouth spread, the audience exploded. By the fall of 1971, All in the Family was the number one show in America, and it stayed there for five consecutive seasons, from 1971 through 1976. No sitcom had ever done that before.

Secret number five. Carroll O'Connor reportedly made CBS promise him a ticket home to Rome.

When Norman Lear came calling, Carroll O'Connor was not a struggling unknown. By the late 1960s he had built a respectable career as a character actor in movies and television. He had appeared in the epic Cleopatra in 1963, in the war comedy What Did You Do in the War, Daddy?, in the crime thriller Point Blank with Lee Marvin, and in Kelly's Heroes alongside Clint Eastwood, which came out in 1970. He had also guest-starred on many television series, from Bonanza to The Wild Wild West.

At the time the show was being picked up, Carroll and Nancy had been spending a great deal of time in Rome, where film work had taken him. According to well-known accounts of his negotiations, Carroll was so unsure that All in the Family would succeed that he asked for a guaranteed round-trip ticket back to Italy as part of his deal. He fully expected the show to be canceled after a handful of episodes. Instead, he would never really go back to that life. The little sitcom that he thought would be a short detour turned into the role that defined his career and made him one of the most famous actors in the world.

And here is a fun connection. Archie Bunker's armchair, that beat-up, wing-backed seat nobody else in the family was allowed to sit in, was reportedly bought from a thrift store for a few dollars when the show began. In 1978, that chair, along with Edith's chair, was given to the Smithsonian Institution. Today they are part of the collection of the National Museum of American History in Washington, D.C. Not bad for a couple of secondhand chairs, and not bad for an actor who had his bags packed for Rome.

Secret number four. Carroll O'Connor's real beliefs were nearly the opposite of Archie's, and that was exactly the point.

Archie Bunker was a conservative, suspicious, stubborn man who distrusted nearly everyone who was not exactly like him. Carroll O'Connor, by contrast, was a lifelong liberal Democrat. He was thoughtful, well read, and openly supportive of civil rights. He understood perfectly that Archie was meant to be a figure people could laugh at, and that the laughter could make audiences think about their own attitudes.

But Carroll also worried about something that troubles every actor who plays a memorable character. Some viewers did not laugh at Archie. They agreed with him. They saw him as a hero speaking up for them. Carroll reportedly found this troubling, and he sometimes spoke in interviews about his concern that a part of the audience was missing the joke. That is also why he insisted on playing Archie with humanity. Archie was ignorant, but he was not evil. He loved Edith. He loved Gloria. He worked hard on the loading dock and later drove a cab. He was a man shaped by fear and by the world he grew up in. Carroll made sure that when Archie was wrong, he was wrong in a way that viewers could recognize and learn from.

One of the most famous moments of the series came in 1972, when Sammy Davis Jr. appeared as himself. Sammy leaves a briefcase in Archie's cab, comes to the Bunker house to retrieve it, and at the end of the episode, as the family poses for a photograph, he plants a kiss on Archie's cheek. Archie's stunned face got one of the longest laughs in the history of the show. Sammy Davis Jr. was reportedly a huge fan of All in the Family, and the moment remains a landmark of 1970s television. And Carroll's performance in that instant, a mix of outrage, confusion, and helplessness, is a masterclass in comic timing.

The Television Academy recognized it. Carroll O'Connor won the Emmy for Outstanding Lead Actor in a Comedy Series four times for playing Archie Bunker, in 1972, 1977, 1978, and 1979. He was nominated many more times. Jean Stapleton won three Emmys as Edith. Together they were one of the finest pairings in television history.

Secret number three. In 1974, Carroll O'Connor walked off the number one show on television, and the writers had to make Archie disappear.

By the mid-1970s, All in the Family was a juggernaut. Norman Lear's company had spun off Maude, The Jeffersons, and more. And Carroll O'Connor, the face of it all, was unhappy. According to many accounts of that period, there were tensions between Carroll and the producers over money, over creative control, and over how Archie was being written. Carroll felt strongly about the character and reportedly pushed for more say in the scripts.

In 1974, as the fifth season was set to begin production, the dispute boiled over. Carroll reportedly stayed away from the set. The production could not wait, so the writers came up with a solution. In a short storyline at the start of season five, Archie goes off to a lodge convention in Buffalo and then simply does not come home. The family worries, Edith frets, and the episodes play out with Archie as an absent presence, discussed but not seen. Eventually, the matter was resolved, Carroll returned, and Archie came back to his chair.

Carroll and Norman Lear did have a complicated relationship over the years. They were two strong-willed, brilliant men with clear ideas, and they did not always agree. But by most accounts, they came to deeply respect one another. Lear spoke warmly of Carroll after his death, and he called Archie Bunker one of the finest performances he had ever seen.

The show continued through 1979, when it ended its original run. Rob Reiner and Sally Struthers had left the series before its final season, and Carroll continued Archie's story in a follow-up series, Archie Bunker's Place, which ran on CBS from 1979 to 1983. Jean Stapleton appeared in its first season but decided she had done all she could with Edith. In the fall of 1980, the show revealed that Edith had died of a stroke. The episode in which Archie finally grieves for her, alone in their bedroom, holding one of her slippers, is considered by many fans one of the most moving scenes in television history. Carroll reportedly drew on deep personal emotion for that moment, and it shows in every second.

Secret number two. Carroll O'Connor wrote the lyrics to the song you heard at the end of every episode.

Everyone knows the opening theme, Those Were the Days, with Archie and Edith at the piano, Edith hitting those famously shrill high notes. That song was written by Charles Strouse and Lee Adams, the team behind the Broadway musical Bye Bye Birdie. But at the end of each episode, as the credits rolled over the outside of the Bunker house, there was a gentle, wistful piano instrumental. That piece was called Remembering You.

The music was composed by jazz pianist Roger Kellaway. And the lyrics, which were not sung on the show but were later recorded, were written by none other than Carroll O'Connor. A vocal version was later recorded as well. It is a nostalgic, tender piece about memory and looking back, which, when you think about it, fits All in the Family perfectly. The show was about a man clinging to a world that was changing around him. And the man who played him quietly wrote words about the beauty of remembering.

This was a side of Carroll that viewers rarely saw. He wrote. He read constantly. He wrote for other projects as well, and later in his career he worked on scripts and took a creative role in the series that would bring him back to television stardom. In 1988, NBC premiered In the Heat of the Night, based on the 1967 film. Carroll played Chief Bill Gillespie of Sparta, Mississippi, a tough but fair Southern lawman working alongside Howard Rollins as Detective Virgil Tibbs. The series dealt directly with racial issues in the modern South, and many people noticed the poetic turn in Carroll's career. The man famous for playing a bigot now played a police chief who learned to see past prejudice. In 1989, Carroll O'Connor won his fifth Emmy, this time for Outstanding Lead Actor in a Drama Series. The show moved from NBC to CBS for its final seasons and ran until 1995, with several follow-up television movies.

It is worth pausing here to talk about the partnership that made all of this possible, because Carroll O'Connor never pretended he did it alone. Jean Stapleton, who played Edith, was a stage-trained actress from New York who had worked on Broadway in shows like Damn Yankees and Bells Are Ringing. In real life she was sharp, educated, and every bit as thoughtful as Carroll. Edith was sweet and scatterbrained, and she trotted to the kitchen to fetch Archie his beer, but Jean played her with a quiet dignity that slowly turned her into the moral heart of the series. Over the years, it was Edith, not Mike, who most often showed Archie what decency really looked like.

Carroll openly admired her. In interviews over the years he praised her timing and her generosity as a performer, and by most accounts the two of them shared a warm professional friendship that lasted long after the cameras stopped rolling. Their scenes together had a rhythm that cannot be faked. Archie would bluster, Edith would answer with some innocent remark that cut right through his nonsense, and Archie would be left sputtering. That give and take, repeated week after week, is a big reason the show still plays so well today.

The younger actors felt Carroll's influence as well. Rob Reiner, who of course went on to become one of the most successful film directors in Hollywood, with movies like This Is Spinal Tap, Stand by Me, The Princess Bride, and When Harry Met Sally, has spoken over the years about how much he learned watching Carroll work. Rob won two Emmys of his own for playing Mike Stivic, and Sally Struthers won two for Gloria. Think about that for a moment. Every one of the four principal cast members of All in the Family won at least two Emmy Awards. Very few ensembles in television history can say the same.

Carroll was also known on the set for his preparation. According to people who worked with him, he would study a script closely and arrive with strong opinions about every line. Sometimes those opinions led to friction, as we just heard. But they also produced a character so fully realized that, decades later, people still talk about Archie Bunker as if he were a real man who lived on a real street in Queens. In a sense, he did. Carroll built him out of the voices he had heard growing up, the men he had sailed with during the war, and the fathers and uncles he had known all his life. Archie was a portrait of a generation, painted with both affection and honesty, and that is a very hard thing to pull off.

Secret number one. Carroll O'Connor's greatest fight was not on a soundstage. It was to save his son, and after he lost him, to protect other families.

Carroll and Nancy O'Connor had one child, a son named Hugh. They adopted him in 1962 while living in Rome, when he was just a baby, and they named him after Carroll's younger brother Hugh, who had died in an accident not long before. Carroll adored his son. When In the Heat of the Night began, Hugh joined the cast as Officer Lonnie Jamison, and father and son worked side by side for years. It should have been a dream.

But Hugh had struggled for a long time. As a teenager, he began using drugs, and according to his family, the battle with addiction followed him into adulthood. He went through treatment more than once. His father tried everything he could think of. Then, on March 28, 1995, Hugh O'Connor took his own life at his home in Pacific Palisades, California. He was thirty-two years old. He left behind a wife and a young son.

Carroll was devastated. But in his grief, he did something remarkable. Instead of retreating from the world, he spoke out. He publicly named the man he believed had supplied his son with drugs, calling him a partner in murder. That man sued Carroll for slander. In 1997, a jury sided with Carroll O'Connor and found in his favor. Carroll had stood by his words, and he said afterward that he did it for every family that had lost a child the way he had.

He went further. He became an advocate for anti-drug efforts and lobbied for legislation in California that would allow the families of drug users to sue those who supplied the drugs for damages. He spoke at public events, he recorded public service messages, and he told his own family's painful story again and again in the hope that it might save someone else. For a man who valued his privacy as much as Carroll did, that took enormous courage.

Carroll O'Connor's own health declined in the years that followed. He had battled diabetes, and he had undergone heart surgery. On June 21, 2001, he passed away of a heart attack in Culver City, California. He was seventy-six years old. Nancy, his wife of fifty years, was by his side. He was laid to rest at Westwood Village Memorial Park in Los Angeles.

So when you think of Carroll O'Connor, yes, think of Archie Bunker in that armchair, cigar in his hand, calling Mike a meathead and telling Edith to stifle herself. Think of the laughter. But also think of the sailor in wartime, the student in Dublin, the English teacher in New York, the man who wrote the lyrics to Remembering You, and the father who turned his heartbreak into a crusade to protect other people's children. There was so much more to him than the man in the chair.
