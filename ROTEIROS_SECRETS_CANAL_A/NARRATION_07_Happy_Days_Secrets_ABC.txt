Secret number eight. Happy Days started as a rejected pilot, and it only came back to life because of a hit movie by George Lucas.

Long before the show premiered, producer Garry Marshall and his partners made a pilot called "New Family in Town." It was about a Midwestern family in the 1950s, and it starred a young Ron Howard as Richie Cunningham, along with Anson Williams as his pal Potsie. Marion Ross was already in it as the mom. But ABC passed. In the late sixties and early seventies, the networks weren't convinced anyone wanted to watch a show about the fifties.

Rather than throwing the pilot away entirely, ABC aired it in February 1972 as a segment of the anthology series Love, American Style, under the title "Love and the Happy Days." And that might have been the end of it. But a young filmmaker named George Lucas was looking for actors for a movie about teenagers in the early sixties, and he reportedly watched that segment while casting. He was impressed by Ron Howard, and he cast him as Steve in American Graffiti.

American Graffiti came out in 1973 and became a huge hit, one of the most profitable films of its time. Suddenly, nostalgia was in. America in the early seventies was weary from Vietnam and Watergate, and audiences wanted to remember a gentler time. ABC took another look at that old pilot, and Happy Days premiered on January 15, 1974. In other words, a show the network had already turned down came back because a movie made the past fashionable again. Without George Lucas, there might never have been a Happy Days.

Secret number seven. The first two seasons of Happy Days looked and sounded completely different from the show most people remember.

If you only know Happy Days from its later years, the early episodes can come as a surprise. For its first two seasons, the show was filmed with a single camera, much like a movie, without a live studio audience. It used a laugh track instead. The look was softer and more realistic, with outdoor scenes and a lot of quiet family moments.

Even the theme song was different. In those first two seasons, the opening credits used "Rock Around the Clock" by Bill Haley and His Comets, a real rock and roll hit from 1954. The famous "Happy Days" theme, written by Norman Gimbel and Charles Fox, the same team behind "Killing Me Softly with His Song," was heard in the closing credits at first. Starting with the third season, it took over as the opening theme, and that's the version most of us can still sing from memory. "Sunday, Monday, happy days."

The bigger change came in season three, when the producers switched to filming in front of a live studio audience with multiple cameras, like a stage play. That change gave the show a new energy. The audience would cheer and scream when Fonzie walked through the door, and the actors fed off that excitement. The ratings shot up. By the 1976 to 1977 season, Happy Days was the number one show on American television. A gentle, single camera nostalgia piece had transformed into a loud, joyful, live-audience hit.

Secret number six. ABC didn't want Fonzie to wear a leather jacket, because the network thought it made him look like a criminal.

It's hard to imagine Arthur Fonzarelli without that leather jacket. It's one of the most iconic costumes in television history. But in the beginning, ABC was nervous. In the early seventies, a black leather jacket was associated with hoodlums and juvenile delinquents. The network reportedly worried that parents would see the Fonz as a bad influence, a gang member who might lead their kids astray.

So in the first season, Fonzie wore a tan cloth windbreaker. It made him look less threatening. Garry Marshall, a clever producer, came up with a way around the rule. He argued that a man who rides a motorcycle needs a leather jacket for safety. The network agreed that Fonzie could wear leather when he was with his motorcycle. So, according to Marshall, he simply made sure Fonzie was near his motorcycle in as many scenes as possible. Eventually, as the character exploded in popularity and audiences clearly saw him as a hero rather than a hoodlum, the leather became permanent.

The casting of the Fonz itself is a story. The producers reportedly imagined the character as a big, tall, intimidating tough guy. Among those who were said to be considered was Micky Dolenz of the Monkees. But Henry Winkler, who stood only about five foot six, walked into his audition and changed the producers' minds. He has said that he based the character's swagger partly on Sylvester Stallone, and that he decided the Fonz would never comb his hair on camera in a mirror, because he'd already be perfect. One of the show's most famous running jokes, that Fonzie physically couldn't say the words "I was wrong," always stumbling over "I was wrrrr," came out of that same idea. The Fonz had to be cool at all times, even when he was wrong.

Even Fonzie's home life had a sweetness to it. In the early seasons he lived elsewhere, but the writers soon moved him into the small apartment above the Cunninghams' garage. That turned him into part of the family. Howard and Marion Cunningham became the parents he never really had, and Mrs. C was the only person in Milwaukee allowed to call him Arthur. Under that leather jacket, the Fonz was a lonely kid who had been abandoned by his father, and the Cunninghams gave him a place to belong. That's one reason viewers loved him so much. He was tough, but he had a heart.

And here's the happy ending to that story. In 1980, one of Fonzie's leather jackets was donated to the Smithsonian Institution's National Museum of American History in Washington, D.C. That's right. The jacket a network once thought was too dangerous for television is now part of the nation's official collection, alongside other treasures of American pop culture.

Secret number five. Henry Winkler, the coolest guy on television, was struggling with a learning disability that nobody had diagnosed.

On screen, the Fonz could do anything. He could snap his fingers and girls would come running. He could fix a jukebox with a single punch. But off screen, Henry Winkler was carrying a burden he didn't fully understand until he was in his thirties.

Winkler was born in New York City in 1945, the son of German Jewish immigrants who had fled Nazi Germany. As a boy, he had terrible trouble in school, especially with reading and math. His parents, by his own account, called him lazy and unmotivated. Teachers thought he wasn't trying. But he kept going, and he eventually earned a master's degree from the Yale School of Drama. It wasn't until he was thirty-one, after he had already become famous as the Fonz, that he learned he had dyslexia. He has said it happened when his stepson was being tested for learning difficulties, and Winkler realized that everything they described was what he had struggled with all his life.

Reading scripts was hard for him. He has said he often improvised or memorized lines in creative ways. Later in life, he turned his experience into something wonderful. Together with writer Lin Oliver, he wrote a series of children's books about a boy named Hank Zipzer, a kid with dyslexia, to help young readers feel less alone. The books became bestsellers, and Winkler was honored in Britain with an honorary OBE in 2011 for his work raising awareness about dyslexia.

And there's another surprise. The tough, motorcycle-riding Fonz was played by a man who, by his own admission, was not comfortable riding the motorcycle. Winkler has said he could barely ride it, and in many scenes the bike was pushed or placed on a platform, or the camera simply cut before he had to ride very far. The coolest biker on television was, in real life, a gentle, funny, deeply kind man who was a little nervous on two wheels.

Secret number four. Fonzie jumped a shark, and that one episode gave the world a phrase that is still used today.

In September 1977, Happy Days opened its fifth season with a three-part story in which the Cunninghams and their friends travel to Hollywood. In the third part, Fonzie, wearing his leather jacket and swimming trunks, goes water skiing and jumps over a shark penned up in the water to prove his bravery. It was a ratings success at the time. Millions of people tuned in to see it.

But years later, the episode took on a whole new meaning. In the late 1990s, a radio personality named Jon Hein and his college friends coined the phrase "jumping the shark" to describe the moment when a long-running TV show starts to decline, trying something so outrageous that you know its best days are behind it. Hein launched a website in 1997 dedicated to the idea, and the phrase spread everywhere. Today, people use "jump the shark" to describe everything from TV shows to businesses to political campaigns.

Here's the twist that Happy Days fans love to point out. The show didn't actually decline right after that episode. It stayed popular for several more seasons and ran until 1984. Henry Winkler himself has always taken the phrase with good humor. And here's a detail most people don't know. Winkler was actually an experienced water skier, and he reportedly did much of the skiing himself for the scene, though a stunt double handled the actual jump. Years later, Winkler even poked fun at the moment by appearing in an episode of Arrested Development in which his character literally jumps over a shark. Now that's being a good sport.

Secret number three. Happy Days launched more spin-offs than almost any show in history, including one that introduced the world to Robin Williams.

Happy Days wasn't just a hit. It was a factory. Its most famous spin-off, Laverne and Shirley, starred Penny Marshall, Garry Marshall's sister, and Cindy Williams as two working women at a Milwaukee brewery. They first appeared as dates for Richie and Fonzie in a 1975 episode. Laverne and Shirley premiered in 1976 and quickly became one of the highest rated shows in America, even beating its parent show in the ratings at times.

But the strangest and most magical spin-off began with a little boy. Garry Marshall's young son, Scott, reportedly told his father that he liked the movie Star Wars, and that Happy Days ought to have a spaceman. Marshall took the idea seriously. In the February 1978 episode "My Favorite Orkan," an alien from the planet Ork named Mork lands in Milwaukee and tries to take Richie back to his home planet.

The role went to an unknown comedian named Robin Williams. As the story goes, when he came in to audition and was asked to sit down, he reportedly sat on his head, the way he imagined an alien would. Garry Marshall said later that Williams was the only alien who auditioned. He was hired, the episode was a sensation, and within months ABC launched Mork and Mindy, starring Robin Williams and Pam Dawber. That show made Williams a superstar and launched one of the great comedy careers of all time.

Arnold's itself went through changes, too. The owner most fans remember first was Arnold himself, played by the wonderful Pat Morita, who would later earn an Academy Award nomination as Mr. Miyagi in The Karate Kid. Morita took on the role in the mid seventies, and when he left after a single season, Al Molinaro joined as Alfred Delvecchio, and Morita later returned as Arnold in the show's final seasons. And remember Fonzie's office? It was the men's restroom at Arnold's, where he held court in front of the mirror. Only the Fonz could make a restroom feel like the Oval Office.

There were other spin-offs, too. Joanie Loves Chachi, with Erin Moran and Scott Baio. Blansky's Beauties. Out of the Blue. Not all of them lasted, but the fact that one show produced so many tells you how much America loved the world of Happy Days.

Secret number two. Richie's older brother simply vanished, and the show never explained where he went.

If you watched the very first season of Happy Days, you might remember that Richie had an older brother named Chuck. He was a tall basketball player who would bounce through the house, usually on his way somewhere else. Chuck was played by Gavan O'Herlihy in the early episodes, and then by Randolph Roberts.

But by the third season, Chuck was gone. No farewell. No explanation. One season he was part of the family, and then the Cunninghams were talking about having just two kids, Richie and Joanie. In the final season, Howard Cunningham even raises a toast mentioning his two children. The character had essentially been erased.

The disappearance became so famous that it gave TV fans a term, "Chuck Cunningham syndrome," which is used to describe any character who disappears from a show without explanation as if they never existed. The producers apparently felt the character just wasn't working and that the show was better focused on Richie, his friends, and of course, the Fonz. Garry Marshall, with good humor, reportedly joked over the years about the missing brother. To this day, fans like to wonder where Chuck went. Maybe he's still out there shooting hoops somewhere in Wisconsin.

Secret number one. Happy Days was supposed to be Ron Howard's show, until Fonzie took over and ABC reportedly considered changing the title.

When Happy Days began, Richie Cunningham was the star. The show was built around him, the all-American kid with red hair and a good heart, learning about life, girls, and growing up in 1950s Milwaukee. Arthur Fonzarelli was a supporting character, the cool older guy from the neighborhood who gave Richie advice. Henry Winkler was billed far down in the credits in the first season.

But audiences went wild for the Fonz. By the time the show moved to a live audience, every time Fonzie walked into a room, the crowd erupted. Fonzie merchandise was everywhere, lunch boxes, posters, T-shirts. The writers began giving Fonzie more and more stories, and before long he had moved from the edges of the show to its center. Henry Winkler went from fifth billing to third, and later to the top.

According to Ron Howard, there was a point when ABC reportedly wanted to retitle the show "Fonzie's Happy Days." Howard has said in interviews and in the memoir he wrote with his brother Clint Howard, The Boys, published in 2021, that this was a difficult time for him. He had been a star since he was a little boy as Opie on The Andy Griffith Show, and he felt protective of the show he was supposed to be leading. Garry Marshall, according to Howard, wouldn't let the title change happen.

But here's the beautiful part. Ron Howard and Henry Winkler never let any of it ruin their friendship. Winkler has said many times that Howard was gracious, generous, and a true leader on the set, and Howard has always spoken warmly about Winkler. They remain dear friends to this day. Winkler has said that Howard made it easy, and that he never once felt resentment from his co-star.

And Ron Howard had bigger dreams. He had always wanted to direct movies. In 1980, he left Happy Days to pursue that goal. He went on to direct Splash, Cocoon, Apollo 13, and A Beautiful Mind, which won him the Academy Award for Best Director in 2002. Richie Cunningham became one of the most successful filmmakers in Hollywood history. And Henry Winkler, after years of being typecast as the Fonz, enjoyed a remarkable second act, finally winning his first Emmy Award in 2018, at the age of seventy-two, for his role as acting teacher Gene Cousineau on the HBO series Barry. When he accepted it, he said he had written his speech forty-three years earlier. There wasn't a dry eye in the room.

There's one more chapter worth mentioning, though ABC and the studio certainly didn't advertise it. In 2011, several Happy Days cast members, including Marion Ross, Anson Williams, Don Most, Erin Moran, and the estate of Tom Bosley, filed a lawsuit against CBS, which by then controlled the show's rights through Paramount. They said they hadn't been paid what they were owed for merchandise using their images, including a Happy Days slot machine. The case was settled in 2012. It was a reminder that even the happiest shows can have complicated business behind the scenes.

After Ron Howard left in 1980, the show had to find a new way forward. Richie, and his pal Ralph Malph, played by Don Most, were written out by having them join the Army and get stationed in Greenland. The producers brought in new young characters, and Ted McGinley joined as Roger Phillips, Marion's nephew and a teacher at Jefferson High. Fonzie himself grew up, becoming a teacher and eventually the dean of boys at the high school, and later adopting a young boy. The Fonz, once the rebel the network feared, became a role model and an authority figure. For a character who began as a minor hood in a cloth jacket, it was quite a journey.

Ron Howard did come back for visits. In the 1983 to 1984 final season, Richie returned for a two-part story when Joanie married Chachi, bringing his wife Lori Beth and their son along. The finale, which aired in 1984, ended with something no sitcom had really done before. Tom Bosley, as Howard Cunningham, turned directly to the camera and thanked the audience for being part of the family for all those years. It was a simple, heartfelt goodbye, and many viewers still remember feeling as though Mr. C was speaking to them personally. In a way, he was.

And through it all, the family at the center of the show, Tom Bosley as the wise and loving Howard Cunningham, and Marion Ross as the warm and funny Marion, made the Cunningham house feel like home for millions of viewers. Ross was nominated for two Emmy Awards for playing Mrs. C, and to this day, many fans still say she was the mom they wish they'd had.
