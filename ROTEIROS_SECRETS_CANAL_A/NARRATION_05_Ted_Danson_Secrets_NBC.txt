And the man playing him, Ted Danson, was about to become one of the most beloved faces in American television. But here's what most Cheers fans never knew. That show almost died in its very first season. The man behind the bar was carrying secrets he didn't share with the audience for years. And some of the most important moments of Ted Danson's life happened far away from the cameras, in places NBC never talked about. Secret number eight. Ted Danson was not supposed to be a ballplayer, and he was nowhere near the obvious choice for Sam Malone.

When the creators of Cheers, brothers Glen and Les Charles along with director James Burrows, first imagined the man behind the bar, they had a different picture in mind. In early development, the bartender was conceived as a former football player, a big physical guy who'd made his name on the gridiron. One of the actors in the running was Fred Dryer, a real former NFL defensive end who had played for the New York Giants and the Los Angeles Rams. Dryer was a natural fit for the jock side of the character, and he reportedly came very close to landing the part. He went on to star in Hunter on NBC, so things turned out fine for him.

But Ted Danson brought something different into the room. He was lean, a little goofy, quick on his feet, and he had a kind of vulnerability under the charm. When the producers chose Danson, they rethought the character's background. A lanky actor didn't look like a linebacker, so Sam became a former relief pitcher for the Boston Red Sox instead, a guy nicknamed "Mayday" Malone whose career had flamed out. That one change gave the writers a gold mine. A washed-up pitcher in Boston, a city that lives and breathes baseball, sitting in a bar full of fans who still remembered his glory days, and some of his worst outings, too.

And here's a little twist most people don't know. Danson himself was not much of an athlete by his own description. In interviews over the years he has joked about how un-jock-like he really was. He grew up in Flagstaff, Arizona, where his father, Edward Danson, was an archaeologist and the director of the Museum of Northern Arizona. Young Ted spent his childhood around Native American culture, desert landscapes, and museum collections, not locker rooms. He went to Stanford and then transferred to Carnegie Mellon University in Pittsburgh to study drama, graduating in 1972. So the man America came to see as the ultimate ladies' man ex-jock was, in real life, a thoughtful drama student and the son of a museum director. That's what you call acting.

Secret number seven. Before Cheers, Ted Danson's biggest claim to fame was a cologne commercial, and his career had been, in his own words, a long slow climb.

Today we think of Ted Danson as a television institution. But for most of the 1970s he was a working actor who couldn't quite break through. He did stage work in New York. He appeared on the daytime soap opera Somerset in the mid seventies. And for a stretch of time he was known to advertising audiences as the Aramis man, a handsome spokesman for a men's fragrance. It paid the bills, but it wasn't exactly Shakespeare.

His first feature film role of note came in 1979 with The Onion Field, a gritty crime drama based on Joseph Wambaugh's true story. Danson played a police officer, Ian Campbell, who is kidnapped and killed. It was a small but haunting part, and people in the industry took notice. He followed it with guest roles on series like Magnum, P.I. and Taxi. On Taxi, in 1982, he played a hairstylist in an episode directed by James Burrows, the same James Burrows who would soon direct Cheers. Burrows had been watching him.

So when Danson walked onto the Cheers set, he was in his mid thirties, a man who had been knocking on Hollywood's door for about a decade. He has said in interviews that he felt like he was learning comedy on the job, surrounded by sharper comic minds. He described Shelley Long and the writers as the ones who knew exactly what they were doing, while he was trying to keep up. Many fans assume Sam Malone came naturally to him. According to Danson himself, it took years to feel completely comfortable with the rhythm of that character. If you watch the first season and then the last, you can see that growth. The swagger gets smoother, the timing gets sharper, and by the end, nobody on television could deliver a line like Sam Malone.

Secret number six. Cheers was nearly canceled, and it finished near the very bottom of the ratings in its first season.

This is one of the most remarkable facts in television history, and NBC certainly wasn't bragging about it at the time. When Cheers premiered on September 30, 1982, almost nobody watched. The very first episode landed near the bottom of the weekly ratings. And as the season went on, things didn't improve much. By most accounts, Cheers finished its first season ranked around seventy-fourth out of seventy-seven shows. In today's terms, that is a show the network would quietly pull off the air before Thanksgiving.

So why didn't NBC cancel it? Much of the credit goes to Grant Tinker, the head of NBC at the time, and to programming chief Brandon Tartikoff. NBC was struggling as a network in those years, and the executives were willing to be patient with shows they believed in. Critics adored Cheers. The writing was clever, the characters were rich, and it earned a stack of Emmy nominations in that first year, winning Outstanding Comedy Series. The network decided quality was worth waiting for.

And then something wonderful happened. Over the summer, reruns gave new viewers a chance to discover the show. Word of mouth spread. NBC was also building what would become one of the greatest Thursday night lineups of all time, and when The Cosby Show arrived in 1984, followed by Family Ties, Cheers suddenly had a huge audience flowing right into it. Within a few years, a show that had nearly been canceled was sitting in the top ten. It stayed a top ten hit for most of its run, and in the 1990 to 1991 season it was the number one show in America.

It's worth remembering what kind of show Cheers was in that first year, because it explains why audiences took a while to catch on. It didn't have flashy gimmicks. It didn't have a cute kid or a catchphrase-spouting neighbor. It was mostly people talking in one room, and the heart of it was the slow-burning, will-they-or-won't-they tension between Sam Malone and Diane Chambers, the overeducated grad student played by Shelley Long. Sam was a working-class charmer, Diane was a bookish romantic, and they drove each other crazy. That kind of story needs time to breathe. Viewers had to come back week after week to feel it building. Once they did, they were hooked. By the end of season one, when Sam and Diane finally kiss in the season finale, the people who were watching were completely invested. NBC just needed more people to find the door to that bar. And the theme song, "Where Everybody Knows Your Name," written by Gary Portnoy and Judy Hart Angelo, became one of the most recognizable television themes ever written, a little promise of warmth that welcomed everyone inside.

Think about that for a moment. Ted Danson's entire legacy as Sam Malone, the eleven seasons, the awards, the place in history, all hung on a few executives deciding not to give up. Today networks rarely show that kind of patience. Cheers is a reminder of what can happen when they do.

Secret number five. Sam Malone's most famous feature, that perfect head of hair, wasn't entirely his own.

In the world of Cheers, Sam Malone was vain about his hair. It was a running gag. He fussed over it, he protected it, and in one memorable storyline the other characters discover that the great Sam Malone might be hiding a hairpiece. That episode, from the later seasons, had Sam secretly wearing a toupee, and the gang at the bar had a field day.

What many viewers didn't realize was how close that joke cut to real life. Ted Danson has been refreshingly open in later years about the fact that he wore a hairpiece during much of his career, starting during the Cheers years. He has talked about it in interviews with good humor, and by the time he was playing Dr. John Becker, and later Michael, the afterlife architect on The Good Place, with that famous shock of white hair, he was happy to joke about how much effort had gone into his look over the years.

There's something lovely about that honesty. Hollywood has always had its secrets about appearance, and in the eighties nobody talked about these things. Studios and networks preferred that leading men seem effortlessly perfect. Danson, as he got older, simply decided it wasn't worth hiding. He has said that letting go of the vanity was freeing. And the writers of Cheers, perhaps knowingly, turned the whole idea into one of Sam's most human moments. A man who seemed to have it all was just as insecure as the rest of us.

Secret number four. While America saw a carefree ladies' man on Thursday nights, Ted Danson was quietly going through one of the hardest chapters of his life at home.

This is a story Danson didn't make public for many years, and it reveals a side of him that Cheers fans never saw on screen. In 1977, he married Casey Coates, a designer. In 1979, Casey gave birth to their daughter Kate, and during the delivery she suffered a serious stroke. It left her partially paralyzed, and recovery was long and difficult.

Danson has said in interviews that he put much of his career on hold to help care for his wife during her recovery. He has spoken about how that experience changed him, how it forced him to grow up and think about what really mattered. Casey worked her way back, and the couple later adopted a second daughter, Alexis, in 1985. The marriage eventually ended in divorce in the early nineties, and Danson has been candid that he made his own mistakes, but he has always spoken about Casey with respect and gratitude.

When you think about Sam Malone tossing out one-liners and chasing every pretty customer through the door, it's striking to realize the actor playing him had just come through something so serious. Danson has also said that during those years he went to a lot of therapy and did a lot of soul searching. He has described himself as someone who spent his early adulthood trying to figure out how to be a decent man. The breezy charm America fell in love with was real, but it sat on top of a person who had been tested in ways the audience never knew.

Secret number three. Sam Malone was written as a recovering alcoholic, and Ted Danson took bartending seriously, even though the bar was built on a soundstage in Hollywood.

It's easy to forget this detail because Cheers is so funny, but one of the foundations of Sam Malone's character was that he was a recovering alcoholic. His drinking had helped end his baseball career. He owned a bar, he served drinks all day, and he never touched a drop himself. That gave the show a layer of quiet seriousness under all the jokes. The writers occasionally brought it to the surface, and in a few episodes Sam is tempted to fall off the wagon. Those moments gave Danson some of his best dramatic acting on the series.

To look natural behind the counter, Danson reportedly took bartending lessons before production started, learning how to pour, mix, and handle bottles like a pro. Watch him on screen and you'll see how comfortable he is, sliding a mug down the bar, wiping the counter, doing a little flip with a bottle. That ease was practiced.

And here's the part that surprises a lot of people. That cozy Boston bar never existed in Boston at all, at least not the inside. The show was filmed on a soundstage at Paramount Studios in Los Angeles in front of a live studio audience. The famous exterior shot, though, the one with the stairs leading down and the sign out front, is real. It's the Bull and Finch Pub on Beacon Street in Boston, near the Public Garden. After the show became a hit, the pub became one of the biggest tourist attractions in the city, and it eventually changed its name to Cheers Beacon Hill. Fans walked down those steps expecting to find Sam Malone and Norm's barstool, and while the interior looked nothing like the set, a replica bar was later built at a second location in Faneuil Hall to give visitors the experience they were hoping for.

Secret number two. Cheers didn't end because NBC wanted it to. It ended because Ted Danson decided it was time to walk away.

By the early nineties, Cheers was a money machine for NBC. It was a top ten hit, it had survived the departure of Shelley Long in 1987 by bringing in Kirstie Alley as Rebecca Howe, and its ensemble had grown into one of the finest in TV history, with Rhea Perlman, George Wendt, John Ratzenberger, Kelsey Grammer, and Woody Harrelson. Ted Danson was reportedly one of the highest paid actors on television, with widely published reports putting his salary around four hundred and fifty thousand dollars an episode in the final seasons.

NBC would have happily kept the show going. But Danson felt the show had told its story, and he wanted to go out while it was still great. He announced that the eleventh season would be his last, and without Sam Malone, the creators agreed there was no Cheers. The network had to accept it.

The finale, titled "One for the Road," aired on May 20, 1993. It was a television event. Tens of millions of Americans tuned in, with estimates around eighty million viewers, making it one of the most watched series finales ever. Shelley Long returned as Diane Chambers, and for one last time, Sam and Diane faced the question of whether they belonged together. In the end, Sam chooses the bar. The last scene shows him alone, straightening a picture on the wall, as a customer knocks at the door and Sam says, "Sorry, we're closed." It was perfect.

There's a sweet footnote here. The night the finale aired, the cast famously gathered in Boston at the Bull and Finch to watch, and they appeared on The Tonight Show with Jay Leno broadcasting from the city. Reports from the time say the cast had celebrated quite a bit, and it made for one of the loosest, funniest late night appearances in memory. And Danson's Emmy record proves how much the industry respected his work. He was nominated for Outstanding Lead Actor in a Comedy Series for Sam Malone every single season, eleven nominations in all, and he won twice, in 1990 and in 1993, the year he said goodbye.

Secret number one. The most important thing Ted Danson ever did may have had nothing to do with television. It started on a polluted beach.

Here's the secret that NBC never put in a promo. In the middle of his Cheers fame, around 1987, Ted Danson was walking with his young daughters at a beach in Santa Monica when he came across a sign warning that the water was polluted and unsafe for swimming. He has told this story many times. He was stunned. Here was one of the most famous beaches in the world, and his kids couldn't safely play in the water.

Instead of shrugging it off, he did something about it. That year he helped found the American Oceans Campaign, an organization dedicated to protecting the oceans and coastal waters. Remember, this was the era when Sam Malone was the king of Thursday night, and Danson was using that fame to knock on doors in Washington and speak about water quality, offshore drilling, and overfishing. Plenty of celebrities lend their names to causes for a while and move on. Danson didn't. In 2001, the American Oceans Campaign merged into Oceana, which grew into one of the largest international organizations focused solely on ocean conservation. Danson has served on its board for decades.

In 2011, he published a book, Oceana: Our Endangered Oceans and What We Can Do to Save Them, written with Michael D'Orso. He has testified before Congress, met with world leaders, and spoken out repeatedly against offshore oil drilling. The son of an archaeologist who grew up respecting the land of northern Arizona became one of Hollywood's most dedicated environmental voices.

And there's one more chapter to his personal story that ties everything together. In 1993, Danson made a movie called Pontiac Moon, and his co-star was the Oscar-winning actress Mary Steenburgen. The movie didn't make much of a splash, but the two of them did. They married in 1995 and have now been together for three decades. Danson has called her the love of his life, and he has said she helped him become the man he always hoped he could be. They share a love of family, a sense of humor, and a commitment to causes they care about.

What's remarkable is how Danson's career kept going long after Cheers. He starred in Becker on CBS for six seasons. He played a ruthless billionaire on the drama Damages and earned Emmy nominations. He was wonderfully silly on Bored to Death, then led CSI: Crime Scene Investigation and CSI: Cyber. He brought quiet strength to the second season of Fargo. And then, from 2016 to 2020, he became a whole new generation's favorite as Michael on The Good Place, an angel-ish demon with a heart, which earned him even more Emmy nominations. In 2024, he starred in the Netflix comedy A Man on the Inside, playing a retired widower who goes undercover at a retirement community. That same year he and his old friend Woody Harrelson launched a podcast fittingly called Where Everybody Knows Your Name.

So that's the real secret. The bartender who seemed to care about nothing except the next pretty face and the next ballgame was, behind the scenes, a man who cared deeply about his family, about doing better, and about the oceans that cover most of our planet. Sam Malone closed the bar in 1993. Ted Danson just kept opening new doors.
