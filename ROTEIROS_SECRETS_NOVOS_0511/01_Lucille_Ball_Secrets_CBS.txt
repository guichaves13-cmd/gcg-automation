On the night of January 19, 1953, something happened in America that has never really happened since. Roughly forty-four million people turned on their television sets to watch the same half hour of comedy. That was more people than would watch President Eisenhower's inauguration the very next day. Lucy Ricardo was having a baby. And on that very same day, in a hospital in Los Angeles, the woman who played her, Lucille Ball, really did give birth to a son. CBS had spent months tiptoeing around a word it would not allow on the air. The network executives were nervous, the sponsor was nervous, and a panel of clergymen had been asked to read the scripts. That is just one of the stories behind the most famous redhead in television history. Today we are counting down eight secrets about Lucille Ball that CBS never really told the fans who loved I Love Lucy, and the number one secret, the one about who truly ran the studio, may change the way you see her forever. So get comfortable, pour yourself a cup of coffee, and let's step back into that little apartment on East 68th Street.

Before there was Lucy Ricardo, there was a little girl from western New York. Lucille Désirée Ball was born on August 6, 1911, in Jamestown, New York. When she was only three years old, her father, Henry, died of typhoid fever. Her mother, Desirée, who everybody called DeDe, was left with a toddler and another baby on the way. For a time, Lucille was raised partly by her grandparents in the little lakeside village of Celoron, right next to Jamestown. There was an amusement park on the lake, with a boardwalk and a dance hall and a roller coaster, and people who knew her later said that noisy, bright, ridiculous place stayed inside her for the rest of her life. Keep that grandfather in mind, too. His name was Fred Hunt, and decades later he would very nearly cost her everything.

Secret number eight. Lucille Ball was told, as a teenager, that she had no talent at all.

At fifteen, Lucille talked her family into letting her go to New York City to study at the John Murray Anderson–Robert Milton School of Drama. One of the star pupils in her class was a young woman named Bette Davis. Lucille, on the other hand, was so shy and so frightened that she could barely speak on stage. According to her own memoir, Love, Lucy, the teachers sent a note home to her mother saying, more or less, that she was wasting her money, because Lucille simply did not have it. She went home to Jamestown humiliated.

But she did not stay there. She came back to New York and worked wherever she could. She was fired from chorus lines. She worked behind a soda fountain. She became a fashion model for the designer Hattie Carnegie, and she was chosen as one of the Chesterfield cigarette girls on advertising posters. It was during this time that she dyed her hair blond for the first time. The famous red would come later. That poster work got her a trip to Hollywood in 1933 as one of the Goldwyn Girls, a group of showgirls hired for a musical called Roman Scandals. She was supposed to stay six weeks. She never went back.

Secret number seven. For most of her movie career, Hollywood treated Lucille Ball as a second-string actress, and they even gave her a nickname for it.

From the mid-1930s through the 1940s, Lucille made dozens of films, first at Columbia, then at RKO, and later at MGM. She was beautiful, hard-working and reliable, and she landed some good parts, including a sharp role in Stage Door in 1937 alongside Katharine Hepburn and Ginger Rogers. But she never became a top-tier movie star. Instead, the press started calling her the Queen of the B's, because she kept starring in low-budget pictures that played on the bottom half of double features.

At MGM, the studio's hair and makeup people decided she needed a signature look. The hairstylist Sydney Guilaroff is often credited with the idea of turning her hair that bright, fiery color. Lucille was naturally a brunette. The red was a henna rinse, and she kept it for the rest of her life. In a strange way, MGM handed her the trademark that would make her immortal, and then never figured out what to do with her. She later said the studios had her doing glamour when what she really wanted was to fall down, get wet and make people laugh. Some of the people who did see it were old silent-film comedians. Buster Keaton, who was working at MGM at the time, reportedly took an interest in her and gave her advice about handling props and physical comedy. Think about that the next time you watch her stomp grapes.

Secret number six. CBS did not want Desi Arnaz on the show. Lucille had to fight for her own husband.

Lucille met the Cuban bandleader Desi Arnaz in 1940 on the set of an RKO musical called Too Many Girls. They married that same year, on November 30, 1940, after eloping to Connecticut. It was a passionate, stormy marriage almost from the start. Desi was on the road with his orchestra. Lucille was in Hollywood making movies. They barely saw each other, and in 1944 she actually filed for divorce before the two of them reconciled.

In 1948, Lucille began starring in a CBS radio comedy called My Favorite Husband, playing a scatterbrained wife married to a bank executive. The show was a hit, and CBS wanted to move it to the new medium of television. But they wanted to keep her radio husband, played by the actor Richard Denning. Lucille said no. She wanted Desi. She believed working together was the only way to save their marriage.

According to many accounts, the network and the advertising people were not convinced. They doubted American audiences would accept a red-headed all-American girl married to a Cuban man with a heavy accent. So Lucille and Desi set out to prove them wrong. In 1950, they put together their own vaudeville act and took it on the road. Lucille played a slapstick character who kept interrupting Desi's band. One routine had her climbing into a cello case and coming out as a trained seal. Audiences loved it. The tour helped convince CBS and the sponsor, Philip Morris, that the couple could carry a show. To make it happen, Lucille and Desi formed their own company. They named it by combining their names. Desilu.

Secret number five. The way I Love Lucy was filmed changed television forever, and Lucy and Desi paid for it out of their own pockets.

In 1951, most live television came out of New York. Shows on the West Coast were seen by the rest of the country through something called a kinescope, which was basically a film camera pointed at a TV monitor. The pictures were fuzzy and dim. Lucille was pregnant with her first child, Lucie, and she did not want to move to New York. And she wanted a live audience, because she said she needed to hear people laugh.

So Desi and his team came up with an idea. They would shoot the show on high-quality 35 millimeter film, with three cameras rolling at the same time, in front of a real studio audience. To light it properly, they hired Karl Freund, a legendary cinematographer who had shot the 1927 silent classic Metropolis. Freund designed a flat, even lighting system so the cameras could move freely without the stars looking washed out.

It was expensive. More expensive than CBS wanted to pay. According to the widely told story, Lucy and Desi agreed to take a pay cut, and in exchange, Desilu kept ownership of the film. At the time, nobody thought old episodes were worth much. Who would want to watch a rerun? It turned out to be one of the smartest business decisions in entertainment history. Those films are why I Love Lucy still looks crisp today, why it could be rerun forever, and why the whole idea of syndicated reruns exists. In 1957, CBS bought the rights to the I Love Lucy films back from Desilu for a reported four and a half million dollars, money that helped the couple buy an entire studio. More on that in a moment.

Secret number four. CBS would not let Lucy say the word pregnant.

In 1952, Lucille discovered she was expecting her second child. In those days, that was the kind of news that could end a television show. Pregnancy was considered too private, too personal, maybe even too improper to show on screen. Desi went to the network and the sponsor with an unusual plan. Instead of hiding it, write it into the show. Let Lucy Ricardo be expecting, too.

The executives were uneasy. According to accounts from the production, the network insisted that the word pregnant would not be spoken. Instead, the characters used words like expecting. The scripts for the pregnancy episodes were reportedly reviewed by a group of religious leaders, including a Catholic priest, a Protestant minister and a rabbi, to make sure nothing would offend viewers. None of them objected.

The episode where Lucy tells Ricky the news, Lucy Is Enceinte, aired in December 1952. Enceinte is a French word for pregnant, which was one more polite way around it. Then came the big night. Lucy Goes to the Hospital aired on Monday, January 19, 1953. Earlier that same day, Lucille gave birth to Desi Arnaz Jr. by a scheduled cesarean section. The doctors had planned the date, so the show's writers knew the real baby and the TV baby would arrive together. The episode drew a rating that historians still describe with amazement, with more than seventy percent of American households with a television tuned in. Little Ricky's birth made the cover of the very first issue of TV Guide in April 1953, with a photograph of baby Desi Jr. over the words Lucy's $50,000,000 Baby.

Secret number three. In 1953, Lucille Ball was accused of being a Communist, and it could have ended I Love Lucy overnight.

Remember her grandfather, Fred Hunt? He was a passionate believer in socialist causes, and he cared deeply about working people. In 1936, to make the old man happy, Lucille registered to vote as a member of the Communist Party. She also signed papers related to a party candidate. She later said she never attended a meeting and did not really understand what she was signing. It was a gesture for her grandfather.

Seventeen years later, at the height of the Red Scare, that registration surfaced. Lucille had quietly testified to an investigator from the House Un-American Activities Committee earlier in 1953, and she was cleared. But in September 1953, the gossip broadcaster Walter Winchell hinted on the air that a top comedienne had been linked to the Communist Party. Within days, newspapers ran her name in giant headlines. Back then, an accusation like that could destroy a career. People had been blacklisted for far less.

What happened next became legend. Before a filming of I Love Lucy, Desi stepped out in front of the studio audience and spoke about the situation directly. He told them Lucy had never been a Communist, not now and not ever. And then he delivered the line that people still quote. The only thing red about Lucy is her hair, and even that is not legitimate. Then he brought her out, and the audience gave her a standing ovation. The sponsor stood by her. The show stayed number one. And Lucille Ball, the woman some thought would be ruined, walked straight into the most successful years of her career.

Secret number two. Behind the laughter, the marriage at the heart of I Love Lucy was falling apart.

On screen, Lucy and Ricky argued, made up, and always ended the half hour in each other's arms. Off screen, it was far more painful. Desi Arnaz was a brilliant producer, and people inside Desilu considered him a genius at the business side of television. But he also drank heavily, and stories of his gambling and infidelity followed him for years. Lucille, by most accounts, was demanding and exacting on the set, and she carried enormous pressure as the face of the whole operation.

After I Love Lucy ended its regular half-hour run in 1957, the couple continued with a series of hour-long specials, later known as The Lucy–Desi Comedy Hour. The final one, Lucy Meets the Mustache, was filmed in early 1960. People who were there remembered it as heartbreaking. In the last scene, the script called for the two of them to kiss. They did, and witnesses later said both of them were in tears. On March 3, 1960, one day after Desi's forty-third birthday, Lucille filed for divorce.

What many fans never knew was how much the two of them still cared for each other. They remained friends. They spoke on the phone. When Desi was dying of lung cancer in 1986, Lucille spoke with him, and their daughter Lucie later shared that, in one of their last conversations, Lucille told him she loved him. Desi died on December 2, 1986. Their love story did not have a TV ending, but in its own way it never really ended.

Secret number one. The ditzy redhead America laughed at for six seasons was secretly one of the most powerful executives in Hollywood.

Here is the part that CBS never really put up on the screen. Remember that money from selling the I Love Lucy films back to the network? In 1957, Desilu used it, along with other funds, to buy the RKO studio lots. That is the very same studio where Lucille had once been called the Queen of the B's. The girl who had been told she had no talent now owned the place.

Then, in November 1962, after the divorce, Lucille bought out Desi's share of the company. That made her the president of Desilu Productions, and she is widely recognized as the first woman to run a major Hollywood production studio on her own. Plenty of men in the business did not take her seriously. She was a comedian. She was the one who fell into vats of grapes. But Lucille Ball signed the checks.

And some of those checks changed television history. In the mid-1960s, Desilu developed a strange science-fiction pilot from a writer named Gene Roddenberry. NBC rejected the first pilot. Lucille's studio backed a second one, which was almost unheard of at the time. That show was Star Trek. Desilu also developed Mission: Impossible. Without Lucille Ball's support, the Starship Enterprise might never have left the dock. In 1967, she sold Desilu to Gulf and Western, the parent company of Paramount, for a reported seventeen million dollars. The old Desilu lots became part of Paramount Television, and you could argue that a sizable chunk of what television became in the decades that followed grew out of what the woman in the polka dot dress built.

She never stopped working. She went on to star in The Lucy Show and Here's Lucy on CBS, which kept her on the air nearly every season into the 1970s. In 1989, she made a brief appearance at the Academy Awards, standing next to Bob Hope, and the audience rose to its feet. Less than a month later, on April 26, 1989, Lucille Ball died in Los Angeles after heart surgery. She was seventy-seven years old.

So, the next time you see I Love Lucy flicker on late at night, think about the girl from Jamestown who was told to go home. Think about the radio star who refused to take a different husband. Think about the woman who fought the network over a single word, survived an accusation that destroyed other careers, and quietly became the boss of the very studio that had once ignored her. Lucy Ricardo never got to be in the show. But Lucille Ball ran the whole place.
