In 1961, a twenty-four-year-old actress walked into an audition for a new CBS comedy. She had been in the business for years, and yet almost nobody in America knew her face. Her most famous television job so far was one where the audience only ever saw her legs. A few months later, she was being hailed as the freshest young star on television, and a pair of capri pants she wore on screen started a battle with the show's sponsors. A decade after that, she tossed her hat into the air on a cold Minneapolis street and became a symbol for a whole generation of women. But behind that bright, famous smile, Mary Tyler Moore was carrying secrets, heartbreaks and private battles that viewers at home never saw. Today we are counting down eight secrets about Mary Tyler Moore that CBS kept from The Dick Van Dyke Show fans, and the number one secret, the one she hid for most of her life, is the reason she spent her final decades fighting for other people. So get comfortable, and try not to trip over the ottoman on your way in.

Mary Tyler Moore was born on December 29, 1936, in Brooklyn, New York. She was the oldest of three children of George and Marjorie Moore. When she was about eight, the family moved to Los Angeles. Her childhood was not as cheerful as the characters she later played. In her memoirs, she wrote openly that both of her parents struggled with alcohol, and that home could be a cold and difficult place. She found her escape in dance. She decided very early that she was going to be a dancer and a performer, and she held onto that dream with remarkable determination.

Secret number eight. Mary Tyler Moore's first big television job was as a tiny dancing elf in a home appliance commercial.

At seventeen, fresh out of high school, Mary was hired to play Happy Hotpoint, a little pixie who danced around Hotpoint appliances in television commercials. The commercials aired during The Adventures of Ozzie and Harriet in the mid-1950s. She wore a tiny elf costume, and she was so small on screen that she seemed to pop out of the stoves and refrigerators. She reportedly made dozens of these spots. There was one problem. Not long into the job, Mary became pregnant, and as her pregnancy began to show, it became impossible to hide it under the elf costume. That was the end of Happy Hotpoint.

She had married young. In 1955, at just eighteen, she married Richard Meeker, and in 1956 she gave birth to her only child, a son named Richard Jr. She was barely more than a teenager herself, raising a baby and trying to build a career at the same time.

Secret number seven. For one TV role, America only saw Mary Tyler Moore's legs.

In 1959, Mary landed a part on a CBS detective series called Richard Diamond, Private Detective, starring David Janssen. She played Sam, the detective's sultry telephone answering service operator. The gimmick was that the audience never saw Sam's face. The camera showed only her legs, her hands, and sometimes her silhouette, while her smoky voice purred over the phone. Viewers became curious about who the mystery woman was. It was a clever bit of marketing, but for Mary it was frustrating. She was getting attention, but not for her face or her acting. She later said she left the role after a short time, and someone else stepped in to play Sam.

She kept auditioning. She tried out for a role as one of the daughters on The Danny Thomas Show, and Danny Thomas himself turned her down. The way the story is often told, he said her nose was too small, and that no daughter of his could have a nose like that. But Danny Thomas did not forget her. And that turned out to matter enormously.

Secret number six. Mary Tyler Moore almost didn't get the role of Laura Petrie, and Carl Reiner had to fight to keep her.

In 1961, Carl Reiner was casting a new comedy he had created about a television comedy writer. He had originally written it for himself, but CBS and the producers wanted a different star, and they cast Dick Van Dyke. Now they needed a wife for him. They auditioned dozens of actresses. Danny Thomas, who was one of the show's producers, remembered that girl with three names who had come in to read for him. He suggested they call her in.

Carl Reiner later said that the moment Mary read her lines, he knew. He reportedly told people that he grabbed her by the head and walked her down the hall to the producer's office, saying, in effect, that he had found her. There was a problem, though. Dick Van Dyke was in his mid-thirties and Mary was only twenty-four, and some people worried she was too young to play his wife and the mother of a school-age boy. Reiner did not care. He believed she had a natural gift, and he was right. Once the show got going, he and the writers discovered she was not only a lovely straight woman but a great comedian, and they started writing more and more comedy for her. Her trembling, tearful cry of "Oh, Rob!" became one of the most famous lines in television.

Secret number five. Laura Petrie's capri pants caused a real fight with the sponsors, and CBS almost lost the show in its first year.

In the early 1960s, television wives wore dresses. Pearls. Heels. Even while they were vacuuming. Mary did not think that made any sense. Real young housewives she knew wore slacks around the house. So she began wearing snug capri pants on the show. According to Carl Reiner and others who worked on the series, the sponsors were uneasy. They were concerned the pants were too tight and too revealing for family viewing. A compromise was reportedly worked out, where Laura would wear the pants in only a scene or so per episode. Over time, the restrictions faded away, and women across the country started copying her look. Mary Tyler Moore had quietly changed what an American housewife looked like on television.

The bigger secret is how close the show came to never surviving at all. The Dick Van Dyke Show premiered on CBS on October 3, 1961, and its first season ratings were not good. CBS cancelled it. The show was saved when the producer Sheldon Leonard and others lobbied hard, and the sponsor Procter and Gamble agreed to stay with it. CBS reversed itself. In the second season, the show took off, and it went on to win multiple Emmys, including two for Mary herself. Imagine television history without Rob and Laura Petrie. It nearly happened.

Secret number four. When CBS bought The Mary Tyler Moore Show, the network would not let Mary Richards be divorced.

The Dick Van Dyke Show ended in 1966, by Carl Reiner's choice, while it was still popular. Mary tried movies and Broadway, but things did not go well, and for a few years her career seemed stuck. Then in 1969, she reunited with Dick Van Dyke for a CBS special, and it was such a hit that CBS offered her a series of her own. Mary and her husband at the time, the television executive Grant Tinker, formed their own production company. They called it MTM Enterprises, and its logo would become famous, a kitten named Mimsie meowing in a parody of the MGM lion.

The writers James L. Brooks and Allan Burns created the show. Their original idea was that Mary's character would be a divorced woman starting over in a new city. That idea ran straight into resistance from CBS. Executives worried that audiences would assume Mary had divorced Dick Van Dyke, and that the idea of a divorced heroine was simply too much for viewers in 1970. Over the years, the creators told a now-famous story about one executive's response, a remark about how American audiences supposedly did not want to see certain kinds of people on TV, divorced women included. So Mary Richards became a thirty-year-old single woman who had just ended a long relationship with a man she had helped through medical school. She moved to Minneapolis, got a job in a television newsroom, and started over on her own.

It was still groundbreaking. Here was a woman on network television who was single, who worked for a living, who cared about her career, and whose story was not about finding a husband. The show premiered on September 19, 1970, and it changed television. It won dozens of Emmys. It launched spinoffs like Rhoda, Phyllis and Lou Grant. And that hat toss in the opening credits, filmed on Nicollet Mall in downtown Minneapolis, became one of the most famous images in American pop culture. In 2002, a bronze statue of Mary throwing her hat was unveiled right on that spot.

Secret number three. Behind the scenes of the most famous newsroom on TV, Mary was battling a disease she had only just learned she had.

Around the time her new show was being developed, Mary suffered a miscarriage. During her stay in the hospital, a routine blood test revealed something unexpected. Her blood sugar was dangerously high. At thirty-three years old, she was diagnosed with type 1 diabetes, a disease that is usually found in children. It meant daily insulin injections, careful monitoring and constant management, all while she was working long hours as the star and the face of a brand-new show.

She did not make a big public deal of it at the time. Television stars in those days did not talk much about chronic illness. But she later said the disease affected nearly every day of her life. Over time, she would face serious complications, including problems with her eyesight. And she would eventually decide to turn her private struggle into something much bigger, which we will come to in a moment.

Secret number two. The woman who made the world smile was carrying enormous private grief.

Alcohol had shadowed Mary's family for years. In 1978, her much younger sister Elizabeth died at just twenty-one after an overdose of painkillers and alcohol. It was a terrible blow. And then, in October 1980, came the worst tragedy of her life. Her only son, Richie, was killed at the age of twenty-four when a shotgun he was handling accidentally went off. It was ruled an accident. Mary was devastated.

Strangely, and painfully, that same year Mary had starred in the film Ordinary People, directed by Robert Redford. She played Beth Jarrett, a cold, controlled mother who cannot express love for her surviving son after the death of his older brother. It was the opposite of everything audiences thought of when they heard her name. She was nominated for an Academy Award for Best Actress. Critics were stunned that America's sweetheart could be so chilling. Many people later noticed how heartbreaking it was that she played a grieving mother on screen in the same year she lost her own son in real life.

Mary also came to terms with her own drinking. In her memoir, she wrote honestly that she had come to rely on alcohol, much as her parents had, and in 1984 she checked into the Betty Ford Center for treatment. It was a brave thing to admit at a time when many stars hid those struggles. She later said that getting sober saved her life. That same decade, her marriage to Grant Tinker ended, and in 1983 she married Dr. Robert Levine, a cardiologist she met when he treated her mother. They stayed married for the rest of her life.

Secret number one. The thing Mary Tyler Moore hid in the 1960s and 1970s became the cause she fought for until the very end.

For years, she kept her diabetes mostly private. But eventually Mary decided that the most famous smile in television could be used for something more important than selling shows. She became the international chairman of the Juvenile Diabetes Research Foundation, now known as Breakthrough T1D, a role she held for many years. She testified before Congress about the need for funding for diabetes research. She brought children with type 1 diabetes to Capitol Hill, so lawmakers could see who they were really helping. She appeared in public service campaigns, wrote about living with the disease, and raised enormous sums of money for research.

She did not hide the costs, either. In her later years, diabetes took a serious toll on her eyesight, and she spoke about losing much of her vision. In 2011, she underwent brain surgery to remove a benign tumor. Through it all, she kept speaking up for others. She also became a passionate advocate for animals, working with her husband on causes like ending cruelty in puppy mills and supporting animal welfare groups.

Mary Tyler Moore died on January 25, 2017, at the age of eighty, with her husband by her side. Her family said she died of complications from pneumonia, and many reports noted her long battle with diabetes. Dick Van Dyke, her old television husband, said he could not believe she was gone, and he called her one of the most remarkable people he had ever worked with.

So the next time you see that hat fly up into the cold Minnesota sky, or hear Laura Petrie cry "Oh, Rob," think about the woman behind it. A little girl from Brooklyn who grew up in a troubled home. A dancing elf, a pair of legs on a detective show, a young mother who was told her nose was wrong. A star who fought the sponsors over a pair of pants and fought the network over a divorce. A woman who lost her son, faced her own demons, and turned her private illness into a crusade that helped millions. As the theme song said, she was going to make it after all. And she did.
