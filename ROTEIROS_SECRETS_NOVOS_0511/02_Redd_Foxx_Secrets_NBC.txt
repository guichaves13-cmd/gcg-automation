In the spring of 1974, the second most popular show on American television suddenly had a problem. Its star had disappeared. Sanford and Son was pulling in tens of millions of viewers every Friday night on NBC, and Redd Foxx, the man who clutched his chest and cried out to his late wife Elizabeth, had simply stopped showing up for work. The network kept making episodes without him. The writers sent Fred Sanford off to St. Louis for a funeral and hoped nobody would ask too many questions. But the real story of why Redd Foxx walked away, and what he wanted from NBC, was something the network never put on the air. Today we are counting down eight secrets about Redd Foxx that NBC didn't want Sanford and Son fans to know, and the number one secret, the way his story finally ended, is so unbelievable that the people standing right next to him did not believe it either. So pull up a chair, ignore the junk piled up in the yard, and let's head down to 9114 South Central Avenue.

Before there was a Fred Sanford, there was a boy named John Elroy Sanford. He was born on December 9, 1922, in St. Louis, Missouri. His father, an electrician, left the family when John was still a little boy, and his mother moved the children to Chicago, where he spent much of his childhood. Money was always tight. The boy who would become one of the richest comedians in America grew up knowing exactly what it felt like to have nothing. And yes, you heard the name right. Sanford. Hold onto that, because it is the first of our secrets.

Secret number eight. Fred Sanford was not a made-up name. It belonged to Redd Foxx's own family.

When Norman Lear and Bud Yorkin's team created the show, they needed a name for the cantankerous old junk dealer at its center. Redd suggested Sanford, his own real last name. And the first name, Fred, was taken from his older brother, Fred Sanford. So when millions of viewers yelled the name Fred Sanford across the living room every week, they were actually calling out the name of a real man from St. Louis and Chicago, the big brother of the star.

The stage name had its own story. People called young John Sanford Red because of his reddish hair and light complexion. Later he added a second d. And the last name Foxx was reportedly borrowed from a baseball slugger, Jimmie Foxx, one of the great home run hitters of the 1930s. Put them together and you get Redd Foxx, a name that sounded like a nightclub marquee long before it ever was one.

Secret number seven. As a young man in Harlem, Redd Foxx ran with the future Malcolm X.

As a teenager in the late 1930s, Redd left home and played music on the streets with a washtub band called the Bon Bon Buddies. Eventually he made his way to New York City. In Harlem during the early 1940s, he worked as a dishwasher and hustled to survive. One of the young men he got to know there was a red-haired hustler from Michigan named Malcolm Little. Because both of them had reddish hair, people needed a way to tell them apart. In The Autobiography of Malcolm X, Malcolm describes a dishwasher friend called Chicago Red, and he calls him the funniest dishwasher on this earth. That dishwasher was Redd Foxx. Malcolm, of course, was known in those days as Detroit Red.

Think about that. Two young men scraping dishes in a Harlem restaurant. One would become one of the most important and controversial voices of the civil rights era. The other would become one of the most beloved comedians in television history. And they started out side by side, laughing in the back of a kitchen.

Secret number six. Long before NBC hired him, Redd Foxx was the king of the so-called party records, albums so raunchy that people hid them from their kids.

In the 1950s, the clubs that would book a Black comedian were limited, and the networks were not calling. So Redd built his career another way. Starting in the mid-1950s, he recorded comedy albums for a Los Angeles label called Dooto Records. They had titles like Laff of the Party, and they were filled with what people then called blue material, adult jokes far too dirty for radio or television. The records were not advertised in the usual places. They were sold under the counter, passed around at parties, and tucked away where children could not find them. And they sold. Over his career, Redd recorded dozens of these albums, and they are often credited with selling in the millions.

Those records made him a legend among other comedians, and they built him a following that NBC executives probably knew very little about. When the network later turned Redd Foxx into America's grumpy, lovable father figure, a lot of grown-ups in the audience were already very familiar with a completely different side of him. And many of them, no doubt, were very careful not to mention it to their children.

Secret number five. Sanford and Son was not an original American idea. It came from Britain.

In 1971, Norman Lear and Bud Yorkin were coming off the giant success of All in the Family on CBS, which itself was adapted from a British comedy. They looked overseas again, and they found a BBC sitcom called Steptoe and Son, about a cranky old London rag-and-bone man and his frustrated adult son who dreams of escaping the family business. Lear and Yorkin bought the rights, and NBC agreed to air an American version.

The question was who could play the father. According to accounts of the show's development, Bud Yorkin had seen Redd Foxx play a junk dealer in the 1970 film Cotton Comes to Harlem and thought he would be perfect. Redd was a comedian, not really a trained sitcom actor, and some at the network were uncertain. But Redd had a gift for timing that you cannot teach. For his son Lamont, the producers cast Demond Wilson, a young actor and Vietnam veteran. Sanford and Son premiered on NBC on January 14, 1972, and within weeks it was a hit. Before long it was one of the top-rated shows in the country, sitting just behind All in the Family. Lear and Yorkin had done it again, and NBC finally had an answer to CBS.

Secret number four. Some of the most famous parts of Sanford and Son were taken straight from Redd Foxx's real life and friendships.

The heart attack routine is the perfect example. Whenever Fred got bad news, he would clutch his chest, stagger around the room and call out to the ceiling to his late wife, Elizabeth, telling her he was coming to join her. It became one of the most imitated bits in television. It was not originally a big plan from the writers. Redd shaped it into his own signature move, and it fit him so perfectly that the writers kept giving him chances to do it.

Then there was Aunt Esther, the Bible-toting sister-in-law who traded insults with Fred in some of the funniest scenes the show ever produced. She was played by LaWanda Page. What many viewers did not know is that LaWanda Page and Redd Foxx had known each other since they were kids in St. Louis. She had been working as a nightclub performer, including a fire-eating act, and she was far from a household name. Redd pushed hard for her to get the role. According to the stories told by people on the show, she struggled with her lines when she first arrived, and the producers considered replacing her. Redd stood by his old friend and helped her through it. She became one of the most beloved characters on the series. Redd's loyalty to the people he came up with was one of the most consistent things about him. He also helped bring other older Black performers onto the show, entertainers who had worked the clubs and the old chitlin' circuit for decades without ever getting a break on network television.

Secret number three. Redd Foxx's famous walkout was not really about a window.

Here is the story NBC fans heard in 1974. Redd Foxx had disappeared from the set. Reports said he was complaining about a lot of things. One detail that got repeated over and over was that he wanted a window in his dressing room. It made him sound like a spoiled star being difficult about a trivial thing. And it made a good headline.

But the real fight was about money and ownership. Sanford and Son was one of the biggest hits on NBC, and Redd believed he was not getting a fair share of what the show was earning. He pushed for a much bigger salary and, according to many accounts, a piece of the show's profits. He also had concerns about working conditions and about being treated with respect. The production company sued him for breach of contract. While the lawyers fought, the show went on without him. Fred Sanford was written out for several episodes, said to be visiting St. Louis, and the stories centered on Lamont and the other characters.

Eventually, a new deal was reached, and Redd came back to the show. Reports said the new contract gave him a substantial raise and a share of the profits. The window story, it turns out, was the part of the fight that made it into the papers, because it was the easiest way to make the star look unreasonable. The part about who owned what was the part the network was not eager to discuss.

Before we get to the last two secrets, let's take a little detour through the junkyard, because there are a few things about the show itself that most viewers never noticed.

Start with that music. The moment you hear that funky, rolling harmonica and bass line, you know exactly what is about to come on. The theme song was called The Streetbeater, and it was composed by none other than Quincy Jones, the same Quincy Jones who would later produce Michael Jackson's Thriller. A sitcom about a junk dealer in Watts had one of the most famous musicians in America writing its opening tune. It is one reason the show still feels so alive when you put on a rerun today.

Then there is the house. Fred and Lamont's address on the show was 9114 South Central Avenue in the Watts neighborhood of Los Angeles. Watts in the early 1970s was still carrying the scars of the 1965 uprising, and many viewers around the country knew it only from news footage. Sanford and Son put a working family from that neighborhood on network television every single week, arguing, scheming, and loving each other like any other family. The show was taped in front of a studio audience in Burbank, of course, but the opening credits, with that old truck rolling past real Los Angeles streets, gave it a sense of place that few sitcoms of the era had.

And then there was Grady. When Redd walked out in 1974, the producers needed someone to keep the junkyard running. They turned to Whitman Mayo, who played Fred's easygoing, slightly scatterbrained friend Grady Wilson. Whitman Mayo was a stage actor, and in real life he was considerably younger than the old man he played. He had to be made up and directed to seem older. During Redd's absence, Grady moved in to look after the business and keep an eye on Lamont. Audiences liked him so much that NBC later gave him his own short-lived spinoff, called Grady, in 1975. It did not last, but it showed just how deep the bench was on that show.

The relationship between Redd Foxx and Demond Wilson is another story worth telling. On screen, Fred and Lamont fought constantly. Lamont was always threatening to leave, and Fred was always faking a heart attack to keep him from going. Off screen, the two men had a complicated bond. Demond Wilson later spoke and wrote about Redd with real affection, but also with honesty about the pressures of the set, especially during the contract fight, when the younger actor was left carrying the show without his partner. When Redd finally left for ABC in 1977, Demond Wilson was not part of the plan to continue the show, and he moved on too. Years later, he became an ordained minister and devoted much of his life to faith and ministry, a turn that would probably have given Fred Sanford plenty of material for jokes, and maybe secretly would have made him proud.

There is one more thing about Redd Foxx that is easy to forget. Long before NBC, he had been a pioneer on the nightclub stage. He was one of the Black comedians who broke into the big rooms of Las Vegas and played to mixed audiences at a time when many hotels on the Strip still had ugly rules about who could stay there and who could walk through the front door. He loved Las Vegas. He made his home there, and he kept performing his stand-up act there for years, even when his television career was on top. In a way, Sanford and Son was the clean version of a man who had spent decades as one of the sharpest and most daring comics in the country. The network got the family-friendly Redd Foxx. The nightclubs always had the real one.

Secret number two. Redd Foxx walked away from the biggest show of his life to switch networks, and it was the beginning of a long fall.

In 1977, Redd left Sanford and Son for good. ABC had offered him his own variety program, and he took it. The Redd Foxx Comedy Hour premiered that fall. It was supposed to be a triumph, the moment the king of the party records got a show built entirely around himself. Instead, it struggled almost immediately. The ratings were weak, and Redd reportedly clashed with the network over material and control. ABC cancelled it after only one season. Meanwhile, back at NBC, the network tried to continue without its star. A spinoff called The Sanford Arms, without Redd or Demond Wilson, lasted only a few weeks in 1977.

Redd did come back to NBC in 1980 with a revival simply called Sanford, but without Lamont at his side the magic was not the same, and it did not last. And then came the money troubles. Redd Foxx had earned a fortune, but he spent freely, gave money away to friends, went through several marriages, and by many accounts did not pay close attention to his taxes. In November 1989, agents from the Internal Revenue Service arrived at his home in Las Vegas. They seized his house, cars, jewelry and many of his personal belongings to cover a debt that was reported at more than three million dollars. Photographs of the agents hauling his possessions away were printed in newspapers across the country. It was a humiliating scene for a man who had once been the highest-paid star on network television. Redd later talked about the experience in interviews with a mixture of anger and his usual sharp humor.

Secret number one. Redd Foxx died doing exactly what he became famous for, and the people next to him thought it was a joke.

By 1991, Redd Foxx was nearly seventy years old, and he was working again. He had landed a new sitcom on CBS called The Royal Family, starring alongside Della Reese, who had been his friend for many years. It was a chance at a comeback, and it was a chance to get out from under the debts. The show premiered that fall.

On October 11, 1991, Redd was rehearsing on the set when he suddenly collapsed. He had suffered a heart attack. And here is the part that is almost too painful to believe. For a moment, according to the people who were there, some of his castmates thought he was doing the old routine. For nearly twenty years, the entire country had laughed every time Redd Foxx grabbed his chest and staggered around. They waited for the punchline. It did not come. Della Reese reportedly knelt beside him and called for help, and he was rushed to the hospital. Redd Foxx died later that night. He was sixty-eight years old.

The joke that made him famous had become the last thing many people saw. It is one of the saddest coincidences in television history, and it is one reason that people who loved him still find it hard to talk about. The Royal Family tried to continue without him, but, like Sanford and Son before it, it struggled without its center, and it ended the following year.

What Redd Foxx left behind, though, is enormous. He opened doors on network television that had been locked for generations. He brought Black family life and Black humor into living rooms across America at a time when that was rare. He fought for the right to share in the money his talent created, long before stars routinely did that. Comedians like Richard Pryor, Eddie Murphy and many others have spoken about the debt they owed him. He came from a kitchen in Harlem and a poor childhood in St. Louis and Chicago, and he made the whole country laugh. And he did it by giving his own family's name to a junk dealer.
