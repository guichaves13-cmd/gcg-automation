On a Sunday in early March of 1963, a small plane went down in the woods near Camden, Tennessee, in bad weather. On board was a thirty-year-old woman from Winchester, Virginia, who had been a household name for barely six years. Her career at the top lasted only a short time. Yet today, more than six decades later, her greatest hits album has sold over ten million copies in the United States alone, and her voice still drifts out of jukeboxes, car radios, and kitchen windows all across America. Her name was Patsy Cline. But the story her record company told the public was only part of the truth. There was a contract that nearly trapped her. There was a hit she couldn't stand. There was a car crash that almost ended everything, and a song by an unknown songwriter named Willie Nelson that she reportedly did not want to sing. Tonight, we are going to tell the rest of the story. These are eight Patsy Cline secrets Decca never told country fans. So turn down the lights, pour a glass of sweet tea, and let's go walking after midnight.

She was born Virginia Patterson Hensley on September eighth, 1932, in Winchester, Virginia, though she grew up in a number of small towns in the Shenandoah Valley before her family settled back in Winchester. Her mother, Hilda, was only sixteen when Virginia was born. Her father, Sam Hensley, was a blacksmith and a much older man, and he left the family when Virginia was a teenager. To help her mother and her younger brother and sister, she dropped out of high school and went to work, at a poultry plant, as a soda jerk at a drugstore, and wherever else she could find a paycheck. As a girl, she had suffered a serious throat infection and rheumatic fever, and she later said that the illness had left her with a voice that boomed like Kate Smith's. She sang in church, at local clubs, and on Winchester radio. She even, according to the family story, walked up to country star Wally Fowler backstage at a show in Winchester and asked for an audition, which got her a chance to sing that very night. In 1953, she married a man named Gerald Cline, and she took the name that would make her famous. Her manager and bandleader, Bill Peer, suggested a new first name, Patsy, after her middle name, Patterson. And with that, Patsy Cline was born.

Secret number eight. Patsy Cline's first record contract was a trap. In 1954, Patsy signed with a small Pasadena label called Four Star Records, run by a man named Bill McCall. The deal looked like her ticket to stardom. In reality, it was one of the worst contracts in country music history. Under its terms, she was reportedly only allowed to record songs that were controlled by Four Star's own publishing operations. That meant she could not simply pick the best songs available in Nashville. She had to choose from whatever McCall's company owned. Her royalty rate was also extremely low, and by many accounts she earned very little money from the records she made during those years. Four Star did not have the distribution muscle of a major label, so McCall made an arrangement to lease her recordings to Decca Records, which released them on Decca and its subsidiary Coral label. So although Patsy's early records came out on Decca, she was not truly a Decca artist in those years. She was a Four Star artist on loan. Her first single, "A Church, a Courtroom, and Then Goodbye," came out in 1955 and failed to chart. Single after single followed, songs like "Hidin' Out" and "I Love You, Honey," and none of them became hits. Decca's producer, Owen Bradley, could see her talent, but he was limited by the songs she was allowed to sing. Patsy reportedly grew bitter about the arrangement, and it was not until her Four Star contract finally expired that she could sign directly with Decca. That happened in 1960. In the meantime, she would have to find another way to break through, and she found it on national television.

Secret number seven. The song that made Patsy Cline a star was one she reportedly did not even like. In January of 1957, Patsy appeared on "Arthur Godfrey's Talent Scouts," one of the most popular programs on television, broadcast on CBS. Contestants were brought to the show by a talent scout, and Patsy's scout was her own mother, Hilda. That was a little trick of the trade, by the way, since the show required somebody to present the performer. On the night of January twenty-first, 1957, Patsy sang "Walkin' After Midnight," a song written by Alan Block and Don Hecht. The program's producers reportedly asked her to wear a cocktail dress rather than the cowgirl outfit her mother had sewn for her, because they wanted a more sophisticated look. Patsy won the night, judged by the studio audience's applause meter. And here is the secret. According to many who knew her, Patsy did not care for the song at all. She reportedly called it "nothing but a little old pop song," and she had wanted to sing something more country. But Four Star had the publishing on it, and she was stuck with it. The public did not share her opinion. "Walkin' After Midnight" was rushed out as a single on Decca, and it climbed to number two on the Billboard country chart and number twelve on the pop chart. It was her first hit, and for several years, it would be her only big one. That same year, in 1957, she divorced Gerald Cline and married Charlie Dick, a linotype operator from Winchester. Their daughter, Julie, was born in 1958, and their son, Randy, in 1961. For the next few years, Patsy struggled to follow up her hit, partly because of that same restrictive contract, and partly because she was busy raising a baby. By the end of the decade, some in the business wondered whether she would turn out to be a one-hit wonder.

Secret number six. Patsy Cline wanted to be a cowgirl, and Owen Bradley wanted to make her a pop singer. When Patsy signed directly with Decca in 1960, her producer was Owen Bradley, the architect of what would come to be known as the Nashville Sound. Bradley believed country music could reach a far bigger audience if it softened its rough edges. He replaced fiddles and steel guitars with smooth piano, background vocal groups, and, eventually, string sections. He recorded at his own studio on Sixteenth Avenue South, known as the Quonset Hut, which became one of the cornerstones of Music Row. Patsy, however, reportedly considered herself a honky-tonk singer at heart. She loved to yodel. She loved the music of Hank Williams and the old Western swing bands. And according to people who worked with her, she and Bradley clashed over the direction of her records. She reportedly complained that the smooth, lush arrangements were not country enough, and she did not always want the polished backing vocals of the Jordanaires on her songs. Bradley, for his part, saw something in her voice that he believed could sell to everyone, from farmers in Oklahoma to secretaries in New York. In the end, Bradley's vision won out, and history has largely proven him right. Their partnership produced some of the most beautiful records ever made in Nashville. But Decca's publicity rarely mentioned the tension in the studio, or the fact that their most famous country girl singer reportedly felt that the records she made were not quite country. It is worth remembering what a polished Patsy Cline session sounded like. Bradley surrounded her with the finest session players in town, the group later nicknamed the A-Team, including guitarist Hank Garland, pianist Floyd Cramer, bassist Bob Moore, and drummer Buddy Harman. These were musicians who could play anything, and they gave her records a warmth and elegance that made them sound at home in a honky-tonk or in a supper club. Patsy, for all her complaints, reportedly came to respect them deeply. And it was the very first hit of their new partnership that would change her life. Earlier that same year, in January of 1960, Patsy had also realized one of her greatest dreams when she became a member of the Grand Ole Opry.

Secret number five. Patsy's first number one hit was climbing the charts while she lay in a hospital bed, fighting for her life. In early 1961, Decca released "I Fall to Pieces," written by Hank Cochran and Harlan Howard. Patsy, according to many accounts, did not like that song either at first, but Bradley talked her into it, and she cut it with the Jordanaires providing harmony. The song began climbing the country and pop charts slowly through the spring. Then, on June fourteenth, 1961, Patsy and her brother Sam were in a terrible head-on collision on Old Hickory Boulevard in Nashville. Patsy was thrown into the windshield. She suffered a jagged cut across her forehead, a broken wrist, and a dislocated hip, and she was in critical condition. One person in the other car was killed. According to later accounts, the first person to reach her after the crash was a young singer named Dottie West, who reportedly picked glass out of Patsy's hair while they waited for the ambulance. Patsy later said that she came close to dying on the operating table and that she had a deep religious experience in the hospital. While she was recovering, "I Fall to Pieces" reached number one on the Billboard country chart in August of 1961, and it climbed to number twelve on the pop chart. Patsy spent about a month in the hospital. When she finally returned to the stage, she walked with crutches and wore headbands and wigs to cover the scars on her forehead. She returned to the Opry in a wheelchair, and, as the story is told, the crowd gave her a long standing ovation. It was the beginning of the most successful period of her career. But she was still in pain when she walked into the studio for the record that would make her immortal.

Secret number four. Patsy reportedly did not want to sing "Crazy." In 1961, a young Texas songwriter named Willie Nelson was struggling to make a living in Nashville. He had written a song called "Crazy," and through Patsy's husband, Charlie Dick, the song found its way to her. According to the story that has been told many times, Charlie Dick heard Willie's demo, loved it, and insisted that Patsy hear it. Patsy reportedly did not care for the demo at all. Willie sang it in his unusual, half-spoken style, with phrasing that lagged behind the beat, and she reportedly said it sounded nothing like a song she could sing. Owen Bradley, however, believed in it. He reworked the arrangement into a slow, aching ballad and told Patsy to sing it her own way. The first session in August of 1961 reportedly did not go well. Patsy was still recovering from the car wreck, her ribs were reportedly still painful, and she had trouble hitting the long notes. The band recorded the instrumental track, and Patsy came back a few days later to put down her vocal. According to the legend, she recorded it in a single take. Whether it truly took only one try or a few, the result is now considered one of the greatest vocal performances in the history of American popular music. Released in the fall of 1961, "Crazy" reached number two on the Billboard country chart and number nine on the pop chart. Willie Nelson later said that her recording of his song was his favorite of all time, and it helped launch his career as a songwriter. Today, "Crazy" is reportedly among the most-played jukebox songs in American history. And just a few months after its release, Patsy would find herself on one of the most famous stages in the world. In November of 1961, she performed at Carnegie Hall in New York City, as part of a Grand Ole Opry show with Jim Reeves, Grandpa Jones, Minnie Pearl, and others.

Secret number three. Behind the tough talk, Patsy Cline was quietly holding up a whole generation of women in country music. To the public, Patsy was a confident, glamorous star. To the people who knew her, she was famously outspoken and could cuss like a sailor. She reportedly called men and women alike "Hoss." She demanded to be paid in cash before she took the stage, after being cheated in her early years. But there was another side to her that Decca never really publicized. In 1961, a young singer from Butcher Holler, Kentucky, named Loretta Lynn sang "I Fall to Pieces" on Ernest Tubb's radio show as a tribute to Patsy, who was still in the hospital. Patsy heard it on the radio, and she asked her husband to bring Loretta to see her. The two became close friends. According to Loretta's autobiography, "Coal Miner's Daughter," Patsy took her under her wing, gave her clothes, gave her advice on how to deal with promoters and the men who ran the business, and even gave her furniture and curtains. Loretta often said that Patsy was the best friend she ever had. Patsy was also a mentor to Dottie West, Brenda Lee, Jan Howard, and others. She paid bills for struggling friends without telling anyone. She insisted that women could headline shows, at a time when "girl singers" were usually just an added attraction on a male star's tour. In 1962, she reportedly became one of the first women in country music to headline her own show in Las Vegas, at the Mint Casino. Also that year, her song "She's Got You," written by Hank Cochran, became her second number one country hit, and it reached the pop top twenty as well. She had become, by the end of 1962, the most successful woman in country music. And then, almost as if she knew, she began to prepare for the end.

Secret number two. Patsy Cline reportedly had a feeling she would not live long. In the months before her death, several friends later said that Patsy spoke openly about dying young. According to accounts from people close to her, after her car crash, she told friends that she believed she would not survive a third serious accident. She had come close twice. And according to several books about her life, she reportedly wrote out her will on a sheet of Delta Air Lines stationery. Some of her friends later recalled that she had given away some of her personal belongings and talked to them about her children. June Carter reportedly said Patsy told her she would not be around much longer. Whether these stories are exactly as they were remembered, we cannot say for sure. Memory has a way of reshaping things after a tragedy. But they have been repeated by enough people who knew her that they have become part of her legend. On March third, 1963, Patsy performed at a benefit concert at the Memorial Building in Kansas City, Kansas. The show was held for the family of a disc jockey named Jack Call, nicknamed "Cactus Jack," who had died in a car accident. She performed three shows that day, reportedly wearing a white chiffon dress. Bad weather kept her plane grounded on March fourth. On March fifth, 1963, she boarded a small Piper Comanche airplane flown by her manager, Randy Hughes, along with fellow Opry stars Cowboy Copas and Hawkshaw Hawkins. After stopping to refuel, they took off into worsening weather. That evening, the plane went down in a forest near Camden, Tennessee, about ninety miles from Nashville. All four people on board were killed. Patsy Cline was thirty years old.

Secret number one. Patsy Cline's greatest success came after she was gone. The weeks after the crash were among the saddest in the history of Nashville. Just days later, country singer Jack Anglin was killed in a car accident on his way to a memorial service for Patsy. Thousands of fans came to her funeral in Winchester. And Decca Records was left with a remarkable collection of unreleased and recently released recordings. One of her final sessions, in early February of 1963, had produced songs like "Sweet Dreams," written by Don Gibson, and "Faded Love," a Bob Wills song. Decca released "Sweet Dreams" after her death, and it reached the country top five. "Faded Love" followed, reaching the country top ten. In 1967, Decca released "Patsy Cline's Greatest Hits," gathering twelve of her most beloved songs. It was a modest seller at first, but it never stopped selling. It stayed on the Billboard charts for years, and in time, it became one of the best-selling country albums by any female artist. In 2005, it was certified by the Recording Industry Association of America as having sold ten million copies, a remarkable achievement for a record made by a woman who had died before the album was even released. In 1973, Patsy Cline became the first female solo artist inducted into the Country Music Hall of Fame. Her plaque reads, in part, that her heart was as big as her voice. In 1985, the film "Sweet Dreams," starring Jessica Lange as Patsy, introduced her story to a new generation. And Loretta Lynn's own film, "Coal Miner's Daughter," from 1980, had already shown Patsy's friendship with Loretta on the big screen, with Beverly D'Angelo playing Patsy. A postage stamp was issued in her honor by the United States Postal Service in 1993. Decca, which became part of MCA, kept her records in print all through the decades, and she was given a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award in 1995. Here is the deepest secret of all. When Patsy was alive, the label had worried that she was too much of a country singer for pop radio, and she worried that she was too much of a pop singer for country fans. In the end, it didn't matter. Her voice was simply too good to belong to just one kind of music. It belonged to all of us. Her husband, Charlie Dick, spent the rest of his life helping to keep her memory alive, working with record companies on reissues and documentaries until his own death in 2015. Her children, Julie and Randy, have done the same. In Winchester, Virginia, the modest house where she lived as a teenager, on South Kent Street, has been restored and opened to visitors, and fans from all over the world come to stand in the little rooms where a young girl once dreamed of the Grand Ole Opry. In Nashville, the Patsy Cline Museum opened in 2017, just upstairs from the Johnny Cash Museum, filled with her stage dresses, her letters, and keepsakes from the home she bought for her family in Nashville's Goodlettsville area shortly before she died. She had been so proud of that house. She reportedly told friends it was her dream home, and she decorated it herself, room by room. And out near Camden, Tennessee, a simple memorial marks the spot in the woods where the plane came down. People still leave flowers there, and little notes, and sometimes old records.

So there you have it, eight secrets from Patsy Cline's years with Decca. A trap of a contract, a song she didn't like, a car crash, a demo by Willie Nelson, a friendship with Loretta, and a voice that just will not fade away.
