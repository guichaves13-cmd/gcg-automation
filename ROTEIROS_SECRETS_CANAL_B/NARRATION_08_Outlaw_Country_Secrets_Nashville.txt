In the early 1970s, Nashville had a system. The record labels picked the songs. The producers picked the musicians. The artists showed up, sang what they were told, and went home. And then a handful of long-haired rebels decided they'd had enough. They walked out of the system, recorded their own way, and ended up selling more records than the people who had tried to control them. One of them was nearly killed in the plane crash that took Buddy Holly. One was a janitor who landed a helicopter in Johnny Cash's yard. One recorded a masterpiece his label reportedly thought was an unfinished demo. Tonight, we're telling the story of outlaw country. These are eight outlaw country secrets the Nashville labels didn't want you to know. And the number one secret, the one that nearly put a legend in prison, is a story you won't forget.

If you were around in those days, you remember how it felt. The music on the radio had gotten smooth, polished, and polite. Then suddenly here came Waylon, Willie, and the boys, sounding like the honky-tonks and the highways again. It felt like somebody had opened a window in a stuffy room.

Secret number eight. Before he was an outlaw, Waylon Jennings gave up his seat on the plane that killed Buddy Holly.

Long before Waylon Jennings was a country legend, he was a young disc jockey from Littlefield, Texas, who had been taken under the wing of another West Texas boy, Buddy Holly. Buddy produced Waylon's first record, and in the winter of 1959, he hired Waylon to play bass in his band for a tour across the frozen Midwest called the Winter Dance Party.

The tour bus was a nightmare. The heater kept breaking down in the bitter cold. So after a show in Clear Lake, Iowa, on February second, 1959, Buddy Holly chartered a small plane to fly ahead to the next stop. Waylon was supposed to be on it. But J.P. Richardson, known as the Big Bopper, was sick with the flu, and Waylon gave up his seat so the Bopper could fly instead.

According to Waylon's own account, when Buddy found out Waylon wasn't flying, Buddy joked with him, saying he hoped Waylon's old bus froze up. And Waylon joked back that he hoped Buddy's plane crashed. Early the next morning, February third, 1959, the plane went down in a snowy field. Buddy Holly, Ritchie Valens, the Big Bopper, and the pilot, Roger Peterson, were all killed. That day became known as "The Day the Music Died."

Waylon later said he carried guilt over those careless words for many years. He was only twenty-one. That experience, many people believe, shaped the hard, haunted edge that would later define his music. Waylon Jennings had already looked death in the face before Nashville ever knew his name.

Secret number seven. In the Nashville system, many stars weren't allowed to use their own bands in the studio.

To understand why the outlaws rebelled, you need to understand how Nashville worked in the 1960s and early 1970s.

At the big labels, the producers were in control. Men like Chet Atkins at RCA and Owen Bradley at Decca had created what came to be called the Nashville Sound, a smooth, polished style with background vocals, string sections, and a small group of elite studio musicians known around town as the A-Team. That sound was wildly successful. It helped country music cross over into the pop market and reach millions of new listeners.

But it came with a price. Under that system, artists often didn't choose their own songs. They often didn't choose the musicians on their records. And in many cases, they weren't allowed to bring their own touring band into the studio. You might spend three hundred nights a year on the road with your own band, playing your sound, and then come to Nashville and record with a group of studio players you barely knew, in a style that didn't sound like you at all.

Waylon Jennings, who signed with RCA in the mid-1960s, chafed under those rules for years. He had his own band, the Waylors, and he wanted them on his records. He wanted to pick his songs. He wanted the records to sound like his live shows. For a long time, the answer was no.

Waylon later said that Chet Atkins was a good man and a brilliant musician, and he had real respect for him. But he also felt the system was strangling him. The rebellion was coming. It just needed the right moment.

Of course, the outlaws didn't come out of nowhere. They had heroes who had walked a rebel road before them. Johnny Cash had been wearing black and singing for prisoners long before the word outlaw was attached to anyone in Nashville. His live albums recorded at Folsom Prison in 1968 and San Quentin in 1969 were enormous hits, and they proved that country fans would embrace an artist with rough edges. Cash reportedly told the story of being pressured to tone himself down, and he simply refused. Out in California, Merle Haggard had actually served time in San Quentin as a young man, and he reportedly was in the audience when Johnny Cash performed there on New Year's Day of 1958. Merle went on to become one of the most honest songwriters in country music, writing "Mama Tried" and "Sing Me Back Home" about the life he had lived. And Merle, like Buck Owens, recorded with his own band, the Strangers, out in Bakersfield, far from the Nashville studio system.

Then there was Tompall Glaser, the oldest of the Glaser Brothers, a family harmony group that had sung with Marty Robbins and on many Nashville sessions. Tompall had a sharp business mind and a stubborn streak, and he built his own publishing company and studio, giving rebellious songwriters a place to work. Without Tompall, many of the outlaw records we love might never have been made. And there were others too, like the colorful David Allan Coe, who rode a motorcycle, wore rhinestone suits, wrote "Would You Lay with Me in a Field of Stone" for Tanya Tucker and "Take This Job and Shove It" for Johnny Paycheck, and told wild stories about his past that even his fans weren't always sure were true.

All of them shared one belief. That a country song should sound like the person singing it.

Secret number six. Willie Nelson was a hit songwriter Nashville loved, and a singer Nashville reportedly didn't know what to do with.

Here's a secret that surprises a lot of people. Willie Nelson was not a struggling unknown when he became an outlaw. He was already one of the most successful songwriters in Nashville.

In the early 1960s, Willie wrote "Crazy," which became one of Patsy Cline's most famous songs. He wrote "Hello Walls" for Faron Young, which went to number one. He wrote "Night Life," and "Funny How Time Slips Away," and "Pretty Paper" for Roy Orbison. Other singers were making a fortune singing Willie's songs.

There's a famous story, often told, that Willie sold the rights to "Night Life" early on for about a hundred and fifty dollars because he needed the money. Whether that exact figure is right or not, it's a reminder of how hard those early years were.

But when Willie stepped up to sing his own songs, Nashville didn't quite know what to make of him. His phrasing was unusual. He sang behind the beat, and sometimes ahead of it, the way a jazz singer would. His voice was nasal and conversational. Producers reportedly tried to smooth him out, adding strings and choirs, and his records for labels like Liberty, Monument, and RCA never became the giant hits that his songs were for other singers.

Then, in 1970, Willie's house outside Nashville, in Ridgetop, Tennessee, burned down. According to the story Willie himself has told many times, he rushed into the burning house to rescue his guitar, Trigger, and reportedly a stash of something else he didn't want the firefighters to find. He took the fire as a sign. Willie packed up and moved back home to Texas, settling in Austin. And there, among hippies and cowboys and college kids, he found an audience that loved him exactly as he was.

Secret number five. Kris Kristofferson was a Rhodes Scholar and Army helicopter pilot who swept floors at a Nashville studio.

If there's one story that captures how far the outlaws would go to be heard, it's the story of Kris Kristofferson.

Kris was not your typical country songwriter. He was the son of an Air Force general. He had been a Golden Gloves boxer and a college athlete. He won a Rhodes Scholarship to study at Oxford University in England. He became a captain in the United States Army and flew helicopters. He had been offered a job teaching literature at West Point.

And he gave all that up to move to Nashville and write songs. His family was reportedly stunned. To make ends meet, Kris took a job as a janitor at Columbia Recording Studios. He emptied ashtrays and swept floors while legends recorded. He was reportedly working there around the time Bob Dylan was recording the album "Blonde on Blonde" in Nashville. He also flew helicopters for oil companies in the Gulf of Mexico to pay his bills.

The story that became legend is this. Kris wanted Johnny Cash to hear his songs. So, according to the tale, he flew a National Guard helicopter and landed it on Johnny Cash's lawn in Hendersonville, Tennessee, with a tape in his hand. Cash himself told versions of the story over the years, sometimes with colorful details, while Kris later said that some of the details had grown in the retelling. Johnny reportedly wasn't even home at the time. But the bold move worked in its own way. Johnny Cash recorded Kris's song "Sunday Mornin' Comin' Down," and in 1970 it won Song of the Year at the Country Music Association Awards.

Kris went on to write "Me and Bobby McGee," "Help Me Make It Through the Night," and "For the Good Times." His songs were honest, poetic, and sometimes raw in ways that Nashville wasn't used to. He opened the door for a whole generation of songwriters who wanted to write about real life, not just polished heartbreak.

Secret number four. Waylon's manager reportedly scared RCA into giving him creative control.

By the early 1970s, Waylon Jennings was exhausted. He was sick, he was broke despite years of hits, and he was frustrated. His contract with RCA was coming up, and he hired a tough New York manager named Neil Reshen, who also worked with Willie Nelson.

Reshen had a reputation as a hard-nosed negotiator, and according to many accounts, he went into RCA and demanded things that simply weren't done in Nashville at the time. A better royalty rate. And the right for Waylon to produce his own records, or choose his own producer, and use his own band. Reportedly, he also discovered money in the accounts that he believed Waylon was owed, which strengthened his hand.

RCA agreed. And in 1973, Waylon Jennings released "Lonesome, On'ry and Mean" and then "Honky Tonk Heroes." The second one was nearly all songs written by a hard-living Texas songwriter named Billy Joe Shaver. And there's a great story behind that.

According to the often told tale, Waylon had promised Shaver that he'd record an album of his songs. Then Waylon kept putting him off. Finally, Shaver reportedly cornered Waylon in a studio and told him, in no uncertain terms, that he'd better listen to these songs or there would be trouble. Waylon listened. And he recorded them. "Honky Tonk Heroes" became one of the most influential country albums ever made.

Much of that music was recorded at a place called Hillbilly Central. It was a studio run by Tompall Glaser on Nineteenth Avenue South in Nashville, a hangout where the rebels gathered, played, argued, and stayed up all night making records the big labels would never have allowed. Hillbilly Central became the clubhouse of the outlaw movement.

Secret number three. Willie Nelson's label reportedly thought "Red Headed Stranger" was an unfinished demo.

After moving to Austin, Willie Nelson signed with Atlantic Records, where the legendary producer Jerry Wexler, known for his work with Aretha Franklin and Ray Charles, gave him freedom. Willie made "Shotgun Willie" and "Phases and Stages." They were critical successes, but Atlantic soon closed its country division.

Willie moved on to Columbia Records, and his new contract reportedly gave him creative control. In early 1975, he went into a small studio called Autumn Sound in Garland, Texas, near Dallas, with his own band and his sister Bobbie on piano. In just a few days, he recorded an entire concept album, a story about a preacher on the run after killing his wife and her lover. He called it "Red Headed Stranger."

The recording was strikingly sparse. Mostly just Willie's voice, his guitar Trigger, Bobbie's piano, and a few other instruments. The whole album reportedly cost around twenty thousand dollars to make, a tiny amount for a major label release.

When Willie delivered it to Columbia, executives were reportedly baffled. According to the story told by Willie and those around him, some at the label thought it sounded like a demo, unfinished, and suggested it needed to be taken to Nashville and properly produced. But Willie's contract gave him control. Columbia released it as it was.

And "Red Headed Stranger" became a smash. The single, "Blue Eyes Crying in the Rain," an old song written by Fred Rose, went to number one on the country chart and earned Willie a Grammy Award. The album went on to sell millions of copies. The record Nashville reportedly thought was unfinished became one of the greatest country albums of all time. Willie Nelson had proved that less could be more.

Secret number two. The album "Wanted! The Outlaws" was reportedly a quick compilation, and it became country's first platinum album.

By the mid-1970s, the word "outlaw" was everywhere. And here's the irony that the rebels themselves would point out. The label that had once tried to control Waylon now realized there was money in rebellion.

In 1976, RCA, under the leadership of Jerry Bradley, the son of the legendary producer Owen Bradley, put together an album called "Wanted! The Outlaws." It featured Waylon Jennings, Willie Nelson, Jessi Colter, who was Waylon's wife and a fine singer and songwriter in her own right, and Tompall Glaser. Most of the songs were tracks that had already been recorded. The cover looked like an Old West wanted poster.

Jessi Colter deserves more credit than she usually gets in this story. Born Miriam Johnson in Phoenix, Arizona, she took her stage name from a relative who reportedly rode with outlaws in the Old West. In 1975, she had a huge hit of her own with "I'm Not Lisa," a song she wrote herself, which went to number one on the country chart and crossed over to the pop chart as well. She was the only woman on the "Wanted" album, and she held her own beside three of the toughest men in the business. Through the hard years that were coming, she would also be the steady heart of Waylon's family.

It was, in many ways, a clever marketing move. And it worked better than anyone expected. "Wanted! The Outlaws" became the first country album certified platinum, meaning it sold a million copies. It included the hit duet "Good Hearted Woman" by Waylon and Willie, recorded with crowd noise added to make it sound live.

The outlaws had reportedly mixed feelings about the label. Waylon in particular grew tired of the word outlaw, saying it had turned into a marketing gimmick. In 1978, he even recorded a song called "Don't You Think This Outlaw Bit's Done Got Out of Hand." But there was no denying the numbers. That same year, 1978, Waylon and Willie released "Mammas Don't Let Your Babies Grow Up to Be Cowboys," and it went to number one and won a Grammy.

Before we get to number one, there's one more piece of the story. Willie Nelson's Fourth of July Picnic. The first one was held in 1973 in Dripping Springs, Texas. Long-haired kids and old cowboys sat side by side in the Texas heat to hear Willie, Waylon, Kris, and many others. In Austin, a club called the Armadillo World Headquarters became a home for this new blend of country and rock, and a young public television show called "Austin City Limits" would soon begin taping, with Willie Nelson appearing in its first pilot episode in 1974. Nashville had tried to keep these musicians in line. Instead, Texas built them a stage of their own.

And now, secret number one. The day federal agents raided a Waylon Jennings recording session, and how the evidence reportedly disappeared.

In August of 1977, Waylon Jennings was at a Nashville recording studio working on music. According to the widely reported account of what happened that night, a package addressed to him had been shipped from New York. Authorities had become suspicious of it and found that it reportedly contained cocaine. Federal drug agents, along with local authorities, came to the studio to arrest him.

And here's where the story becomes the stuff of legend. According to the version told by Waylon himself in his autobiography, the agents waited for a search warrant, and in the time that passed, the evidence was reportedly flushed away. By the time the agents searched the studio, they reportedly couldn't find what they were looking for. Waylon was arrested and charged, but the charges were eventually dropped. One of the people connected to the shipment reportedly took responsibility.

The case made headlines across the country, and it only added to the outlaw legend. Waylon turned it into music, as he did with so much of his life. The song "Don't You Think This Outlaw Bit's Done Got Out of Hand" includes lines about the night the law came knocking at the studio.

But for Waylon, it wasn't a joke. In the years that followed, he struggled with a serious cocaine addiction. He later said he spent enormous sums of money on it. And in the early 1980s, with the support of Jessi Colter and his family, and with the birth of his son Shooter, he quit. Waylon spoke openly about it later, hoping others would learn from his mistakes. He quit, he said, because he wanted to be there for his family.

Waylon Jennings passed away in 2002, after years of health problems related to diabetes. He was inducted into the Country Music Hall of Fame in 2001, though he reportedly chose not to attend the ceremony, saying he didn't like that kind of attention. Willie Nelson, of course, has kept on going, recording and touring into his nineties, still on the road again. Kris Kristofferson passed away in 2024, remembered as one of the greatest songwriters America ever produced.

So there you have it. A young bass player who gave up a seat on a doomed plane. A Nashville system that kept artists from their own bands. A hit songwriter nobody knew how to record. A Rhodes Scholar sweeping studio floors. A manager who rewrote the rules. A masterpiece that was mistaken for a demo. A rebel movement turned into a platinum record. And a night in a Nashville studio that nearly ended a legend's career. The outlaws didn't destroy Nashville. They changed it. They proved that country artists could be trusted to make their own music, and every artist who has picked their own songs since then owes them a debt.
