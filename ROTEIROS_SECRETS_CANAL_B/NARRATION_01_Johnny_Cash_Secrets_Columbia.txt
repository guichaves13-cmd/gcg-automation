In March of 1998, a full-page advertisement ran in Billboard magazine. It showed a gray-haired man in black, staring straight into the camera, raising his middle finger. Underneath were a few polite words thanking the Nashville music establishment and country radio for their support. That man was Johnny Cash. Twelve years earlier, the record company he had served for nearly three decades had let him go. Country radio had stopped playing him. And yet here he was, holding a brand-new Grammy, and making sure everyone in Nashville knew exactly how he felt. How did the biggest star Columbia Records ever had in country music end up there? The answer is buried in stories the label never put on the back of an album jacket. Tonight, we are going to dig them up, one by one. These are eight Johnny Cash secrets Columbia Records never told country fans. So pour yourself a cup of coffee, settle into your favorite chair, and let's walk the line together.

Before we begin, a little background for anyone who grew up with these records spinning on the family turntable. J.R. Cash was born on February twenty-sixth, 1932, in Kingsland, Arkansas. His family moved to the government resettlement colony at Dyess, Arkansas, when he was just a small boy, and he spent his childhood picking cotton beside his brothers and sisters. He served in the United States Air Force, stationed in West Germany, where he worked as a radio operator intercepting Soviet transmissions. When he came home, he settled in Memphis, sold appliances door to door, and pestered a man named Sam Phillips at Sun Records until he got an audition. In 1955, Sun released "Cry! Cry! Cry!" backed with "Hey Porter," and the world met the voice that would rumble through the next half century of American music. By 1956, "I Walk the Line" had gone all the way to number one on the country chart and crossed over onto the pop chart as well. And just two years later, he would do something that shocked Memphis.

Secret number eight. Johnny Cash left Sun Records for Columbia partly because he wanted to sing about God. In 1958, Cash signed with Columbia Records, one of the oldest and most powerful labels in America. Most people assumed it was simply about money, and money certainly mattered. Columbia offered a better royalty rate than the small Memphis label could afford. But Cash himself told interviewers over the years that there was something deeper going on. He had grown up singing hymns in the Baptist church and in the cotton fields with his mother, and he badly wanted to record an album of gospel songs. According to Cash's own accounts, Sam Phillips was not eager to make that kind of record. Gospel albums did not sell like rockabilly singles, and Sun was a business that lived and died on hits. Columbia, on the other hand, agreed to let him do it. And they kept their word. His first Columbia album, "The Fabulous Johnny Cash," came out in late 1958, and in 1959 the label released "Hymns by Johnny Cash." Sun, meanwhile, kept issuing the songs Cash had left behind in their vaults for years after he was gone, so fans in those days often heard new Sun singles and new Columbia singles competing on the radio at the very same time. Columbia did not advertise the fact that its new star had walked away from Memphis in part over a hymnbook. But for the rest of his life, Cash would return to gospel music again and again, recording album after album of sacred songs. It was never a side project for him. It was the reason he changed labels in the first place. And early on, the move paid off handsomely. "Don't Take Your Guns to Town" reached number one on the country chart in early 1959, proving that Columbia had bought itself a hitmaker, not just a hymn singer.

Secret number seven. "Ring of Fire" came out of a dream, and it was not Johnny's song first. Most fans know that "Ring of Fire" was written by June Carter, together with songwriter Merle Kilgore. What many do not know is that it was originally recorded by June's sister, Anita Carter, in 1962, under the title "Love's Ring of Fire." Her version did not catch fire, if you will forgive the expression. Cash, by his own account, had been listening to it, and he told people that he had a dream in which he heard the song played with Mexican mariachi horns. He said he would give Anita a few more months, and if her version did not take off, he was going to record it his way. That is exactly what happened. In March of 1963, Cash went into the studio with Columbia producer Don Law and cut the song with a trumpet section that sounded like nothing else on country radio at the time. Plenty of people in Nashville thought the horns were a mistake. Country records simply did not sound like that. But the public disagreed. "Ring of Fire" went to number one on the Billboard country chart and stayed there for seven weeks, and it crossed into the pop top twenty as well. It became one of the most beloved recordings of his entire career. Now, here is the part Columbia's publicity department never dwelled on. In 1963, Johnny Cash was still married to his first wife, Vivian Liberto, the mother of his four daughters. June Carter was married too. Over the years, June's family and friends suggested that the song described the overwhelming feelings she was struggling with at the time, feelings for a man she knew she should not fall for. June herself spoke about it in later years as a song about the frightening power of love. So the biggest hit Johnny Cash had in the early nineteen-sixties was, in all likelihood, a love letter written to him by the woman who would one day become his wife. It just took five more years, and a great deal of heartache, before the whole world understood.

Secret number six. Johnny Cash went to war with country radio over an album about Native Americans. In 1964, at the height of his popularity, Cash recorded an album called "Bitter Ballads of the American Indian," better known simply as "Bitter Tears." Many of the songs were written by folk singer Peter La Farge, and they told hard truths about broken treaties, stolen land, and the suffering of Native peoples. The centerpiece was "The Ballad of Ira Hayes," the story of a Pima Indian Marine who helped raise the flag on Iwo Jima during the Second World War, came home a hero, and died in poverty and despair in 1955. It was a brave record for a country star to make in 1964. And country radio did not want to touch it. Many disc jockeys refused to play "The Ballad of Ira Hayes," and some programmers reportedly felt the subject matter was too political. Johnny Cash did not sit back and accept it. In August of 1964, he paid for a full-page advertisement in Billboard magazine, an open letter addressed to the disc jockeys, station managers, and the industry itself. In it, he challenged them directly, asking where their guts were, and saying that "Ira Hayes" was strong medicine, but so were the deaths that inspired it. It was an astonishing thing for a contracted Columbia artist to do. Record labels in those days liked their stars polite, grateful, and quiet. But the gamble worked, at least in part. "The Ballad of Ira Hayes" climbed into the country top five, and the album itself reached the top of the country album chart. Cash later said he was proud of that record for the rest of his life. It was the first time, but certainly not the last, that Johnny Cash would publicly tell the music business it was wrong. And that brings us to a much darker chapter, one that the label would much rather have kept off the front pages.

Secret number five. In 1965, Johnny Cash's life was spinning out of control, and the record company could not hide it. By the mid-nineteen-sixties, Cash was touring constantly, and like a great many performers of that era, he had come to depend on amphetamines to keep going and on other pills to come down. He wrote openly about this in his autobiographies, so this is not rumor. It is his own testimony. In October of 1965, Cash was arrested at the airport in El Paso, Texas, by federal narcotics agents. He had crossed into Juárez, Mexico, and agents reportedly found hundreds of amphetamine and sedative tablets hidden in his guitar case. Because the pills were prescription drugs rather than heroin, which the agents had originally been looking for, he received a suspended sentence and a fine. But the news photographs of a handcuffed Johnny Cash went out across the country. That same year, there was another disaster. In June of 1965, a camper truck Cash was driving caught fire in the Los Padres National Forest in California, reportedly because of an overheated, cracked wheel bearing that sparked in the dry grass. The fire burned hundreds of acres of federal land and drove away endangered California condors from the area. When the government questioned him, Cash reportedly gave an answer that has become legend, saying something to the effect that he didn't care about their yellow buzzards. The United States government sued him, and he eventually paid a settlement reported at more than eighty thousand dollars. He was also, in those years, reportedly banned from the Grand Ole Opry for a time after smashing the footlights with a microphone stand. Through all of this, Columbia kept releasing records. The label had invested heavily in its star, and there was little it could do but hope he survived. Many in Nashville privately doubted that he would. And it was June Carter, along with her parents, Mother Maybelle and Ezra Carter, who reportedly stood by him through his attempts to get clean in the late sixties. Which leads us directly to the most famous concert in country music history.

Secret number four. Columbia Records was reportedly nervous about the Folsom Prison album. Today, "At Folsom Prison" is considered one of the greatest live albums ever made, in any genre. But in 1967, the idea of recording a country star in front of a crowd of convicts was not an easy sell. Cash had been performing for prisoners since the late nineteen-fifties, and he had written "Folsom Prison Blues" back in his Air Force days, after watching a film about the prison. He had wanted for years to record an album inside a prison. According to many accounts, executives at Columbia were not enthusiastic. Cash's image was already a concern after the troubles of the mid-sixties, and singing to murderers and armed robbers hardly seemed like the way to win back respectability. What changed everything was a new producer. Bob Johnston, a Texan who had just been producing Bob Dylan's albums for Columbia, took over Cash's recordings, and he reportedly made a few phone calls and got it done. On January thirteenth, 1968, Johnny Cash, June Carter, the Tennessee Three, Carl Perkins, and the Statler Brothers performed two shows in the cafeteria of Folsom State Prison in California. You can hear the men cheering at lines about shooting a man in Reno, and you can hear the guards' announcements over the loudspeaker. Some of those details were reportedly enhanced in the editing room, as live albums often were. The album was released in May of 1968. Tragically, it came out just weeks before the assassination of Robert Kennedy, and some radio stations reportedly hesitated to play a single that included the line about shooting a man. Columbia issued an edited version. But nothing could stop it. "Folsom Prison Blues" went to number one on the country chart, and the album went to number one on the country album chart and reached the pop top twenty, eventually selling in the millions. A year later, on February twenty-fourth, 1969, Cash went to San Quentin and did it again. That show gave him "A Boy Named Sue," written by Shel Silverstein, which reached number two on the Billboard Hot 100 and number one on the country chart. "At San Quentin" topped the pop album chart. The project the label had reportedly doubted became, for a time, the biggest-selling success in the company. And in 1968, Cash also took home the Country Music Association awards that proved Nashville had come back around. A few weeks after Folsom, by the way, on February twenty-second, 1968, during a concert in London, Ontario, Cash proposed to June Carter on stage. She said yes, and they were married on March first, 1968.

Secret number three. Johnny Cash used his network television show to defy the people who signed the checks. In June of 1969, ABC premiered "The Johnny Cash Show," filmed at the Ryman Auditorium in Nashville, the home of the Grand Ole Opry. It ran until 1971. On the surface, it was a friendly variety hour, with June, the Carter Family, the Statler Brothers, and Carl Perkins. But Cash used his platform in ways that made network executives uncomfortable. On the very first episode, his guest was Bob Dylan, at a time when Dylan almost never appeared on television. Over the months that followed, Cash welcomed Joni Mitchell, Ray Charles, Louis Armstrong, Neil Young, James Taylor, and Creedence Clearwater Revival. In 1970, he invited folk singer Pete Seeger, who had been blacklisted from network television for years because of his political past. Cash reportedly faced pressure from the network about his gospel segments, too. Some executives were said to be uneasy about how often he talked about his faith on prime-time television, and Cash, by his own account, refused to tone it down. Then there was a famous moment in 1971. Cash debuted a new song on the show, "Man in Black," in which he explained exactly why he wore black. He said he wore it for the poor and the beaten down, for the prisoner who had long paid for his crime, for the sick and lonely old, and for the young men dying in Vietnam. It was released as a Columbia single and reached the country top five. For the rest of his life, he was the Man in Black. The network canceled the show in 1971, during a period when many rural-themed programs were being pulled from the air. But during those two seasons, Cash had introduced millions of living rooms to artists they would never have seen otherwise, and he had done it entirely on his own terms. Columbia, meanwhile, enjoyed the sales. But the stubborn independence they had once welcomed would eventually come back to haunt them both.

Secret number two. The song that embarrassed Johnny Cash. By the late nineteen-seventies and early eighties, country music was changing. The urban cowboy craze was in full swing, smooth pop production ruled the airwaves, and a gravel-voiced man in black singing about prisons and trains did not fit the new formula. Cash's chart positions slipped. He still had moments, like "Highwayman," recorded with Willie Nelson, Waylon Jennings, and Kris Kristofferson, which went to number one in 1985, though that record was credited to the four of them together. But his solo career at Columbia was struggling. In 1984, he released a novelty single called "The Chicken in Black." The song told the comic story of Johnny Cash's brain being transplanted into a chicken, while he received the brain of a bank robber. The music video showed Cash dressed as a superhero bandit. The single did reach the country top fifty. But by Cash's own later admission, he hated it. In interviews, he said he felt the record made a fool of him, and he believed the whole thing had been a mistake. Some who knew him reportedly said he recorded it deliberately badly, half hoping it would fail. Whatever the truth, it became a symbol for many fans of how far the label and the artist had drifted apart. Columbia was trying to find a hit any way it could, and Cash was losing faith in the people who were supposed to be guiding his career. A man who had once demanded the right to sing about the poor, the imprisoned, and the forgotten was now singing about poultry. It was, he would say, one of the low points of his professional life. And it was about to get worse.

Secret number one. Columbia Records dropped Johnny Cash, and he made them regret it. In 1986, after twenty-eight years with the label, Columbia ended its relationship with Johnny Cash. The decision was made under the label's Nashville leadership at the time, and Cash later said he was deeply hurt by the way it was handled. This was the man who had sold tens of millions of records for the company, the man whose San Quentin album had once knocked everyone else off the top of the Billboard pop album chart. In 1969, the Country Music Association had named him Entertainer of the Year, and he swept album, single, and male vocalist honors that same night. He had been inducted into the Country Music Hall of Fame in 1980, at forty-eight, one of the youngest living members ever honored at that time. He had recorded for Columbia through four American presidents, through Vietnam and Watergate, through the arrival of rock and roll's second and third generations. He had given the company gospel records, prison records, concept albums about the railroad and the American West, and duets with June that won Grammy Awards, including "Jackson" in 1968. And yet the company decided he was no longer worth the investment. Cash signed with Mercury Records, where he recorded several albums that sold modestly. Many in Nashville assumed that his days as a major artist were over. He was in his late fifties and early sixties now, battling health problems, and country radio seemed to have no room for him at all. Then, in 1993, a most unlikely man walked into his life. Rick Rubin was a bearded record producer from Long Island, famous for working with rap groups like the Beastie Boys and Run-D.M.C. and with heavy rock bands. He signed Cash to his label, American Recordings, and he did something no one in Nashville had thought to do for years. He simply sat Johnny Cash down with an acoustic guitar and let him sing. The album "American Recordings" came out in 1994 and won the Grammy for Best Contemporary Folk Album. Its follow-up, "Unchained," won the Grammy for Best Country Album in 1998. Country radio, however, largely still would not play him. That is why, in March of 1998, Rubin's label ran that famous full-page Billboard advertisement, using a photograph from the San Quentin concert of 1969 in which Cash is giving the camera the finger. It was his answer to an industry that had written him off. In 2002, Cash recorded "Hurt," a song by the rock band Nine Inch Nails, and its music video, filmed partly at his shuttered House of Cash museum, is still considered one of the most powerful pieces of music film ever made. June Carter Cash passed away on May fifteenth, 2003. Johnny followed her less than four months later, on September twelfth, 2003, at the age of seventy-one. And in the years after his death, Columbia's parent company, Sony, released and reissued his classic recordings again and again, to a whole new generation of fans. The very label that had let him go became the keeper of his legacy. The Man in Black, it turns out, got the last word after all.

So there you have it. Eight secrets from Johnny Cash's years at Columbia, from a hymnbook in Memphis to a middle finger in Billboard. He was a man of faith and a man of failings, a rebel and a gentleman, and he never once pretended to be anything other than exactly who he was. Maybe that is why we still listen.
