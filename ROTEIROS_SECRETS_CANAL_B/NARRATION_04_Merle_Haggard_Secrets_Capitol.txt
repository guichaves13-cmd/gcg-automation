On New Year's Day of 1959, a twenty-one-year-old inmate sat in the yard of San Quentin State Prison in California and watched Johnny Cash perform for the convicts. He had a sentence of up to fifteen years hanging over his head. He had tried to escape from jails more times than most men try anything. And by his own account, something changed in him that day. Thirteen years later, the governor of California, a former movie actor named Ronald Reagan, signed a full pardon for that same man, who by then had become one of the biggest stars Capitol Records had ever known. His name was Merle Haggard. The songs he made in those years, songs like "Mama Tried," "Sing Me Back Home," and "Okie from Muskogee," made him the voice of working America. But behind those records were stories the label never printed on the album covers, from a song they reportedly refused to release, to the real meaning behind his most famous hit. These are eight Merle Haggard secrets Capitol never told country fans. So pull up a chair, and let's ride that train back to Bakersfield.

Merle Ronald Haggard was born on April sixth, 1937, in Oildale, California, just outside Bakersfield. His parents, James and Flossie Haggard, had come west from Oklahoma in the nineteen-thirties, part of the great migration of families escaping the Dust Bowl and the Great Depression. The family lived in a converted railroad boxcar that his father had remodeled into a home. When Merle was nine years old, his father died suddenly of a stroke. Merle later said that losing his father was the moment his life began to go wrong. His mother worked as a bookkeeper to support the family, and young Merle began running away from home, hopping freight trains, skipping school, and getting into trouble. He was sent to juvenile detention centers again and again, and he escaped from many of them. He would later write about those years with heartbreaking honesty. But even as a boy, he was learning to play guitar, listening to Bob Wills, Lefty Frizzell, and Jimmie Rodgers on the radio. In 1953, as a teenager, he reportedly got to meet Lefty Frizzell backstage at a show in Bakersfield, and Lefty was so impressed that he let the young man sing a couple of songs. And then came the night that sent him to the most notorious prison in California.

Secret number eight. A Johnny Cash concert at San Quentin helped turn a convict into a country star. In late 1957, Merle Haggard and a friend, both reportedly drunk, tried to break into a Bakersfield roadhouse in the middle of the night, thinking it was closed. It was not. The owner was still inside. Haggard was arrested, and after he escaped from the county jail, he was sentenced to San Quentin State Prison, where he arrived in early 1958. He was twenty years old. Inside, he ran a gambling and beer-brewing operation, by his own admission, and he spent time in solitary confinement. Then, on January first, 1959, Johnny Cash came to San Quentin to perform. Haggard was in the audience. He later said that Cash had a bad voice that day, because he had laryngitis, but that it did not matter at all. Cash had attitude. He spoke to the prisoners as equals, and he sang songs they understood. Haggard said that seeing Cash perform made him decide to turn his life around and give music a real try. Years later, in 1969, the two men would appear together on "The Johnny Cash Show" on ABC. According to Haggard, it was Cash who encouraged him to speak openly about his prison past, which until then Haggard had reportedly been reluctant to discuss in public. Cash reportedly told him that people would respect him more for the truth. Haggard was released on parole in 1960, after serving nearly three years. He went back to Bakersfield, dug ditches and wired houses for his brother's electrical business, and played in the bars at night. And within a few short years, he would be standing in front of a microphone at Capitol Records.

Secret number seven. Merle Haggard got his first big break on a tiny label, and he named his band after a song he didn't write. By the early nineteen-sixties, Bakersfield had become a hotbed of country music. While Nashville was smoothing out its sound with strings and backing choirs, the honky-tonks of Bakersfield were blasting out loud electric guitars and hard-driving rhythms, a style that came to be called the Bakersfield Sound. The biggest star of that scene was Buck Owens, who recorded for Capitol. Haggard joined the scene too, playing bass for a while in a band led by singer Wynn Stewart in Las Vegas. In 1962, he heard Wynn Stewart sing a song called "Sing a Sad Song," and he asked if he could have it. Stewart agreed. Haggard recorded it in 1963 for a small Bakersfield label called Tally Records, owned by Lewis Talley and run with Fuzzy Owen, who would become Haggard's longtime manager. The song reached the country top twenty. Then in 1964 came "(My Friends Are Gonna Be) Strangers," written by Liz Anderson, which climbed into the country top ten. Haggard liked that song so much that he named his backing band the Strangers, the name they carried for the rest of his career. Capitol Records took notice. In 1965, the label signed Haggard and bought up his Tally masters. His producer at Capitol would be Ken Nelson, the same man who had produced Buck Owens, Wanda Jackson, Gene Vincent, and Sonny James. Nelson was famous for letting artists use their own bands in the studio, rather than forcing them to use session musicians the way many Nashville producers did. That decision, which was unusual at the time, gave Haggard's records a raw, authentic sound. Also in 1965, Merle married singer Bonnie Owens, who had previously been married to Buck Owens. Bonnie sang harmony with the Strangers for years, and the couple won Academy of Country Music awards together as a duo. Even after their marriage ended in 1978, she remained in his band as a backup singer for many years.

Secret number six. Merle Haggard's first number one hit was a song written by a woman who had never been to prison. In 1966, Capitol released "I'm a Lonesome Fugitive," a song about a man on the run from the law, always looking over his shoulder. It went to number one on the Billboard country chart in early 1967, Haggard's first chart-topper. Most listeners assumed he had written it himself about his own life. In fact, the song was written by Liz Anderson, the same woman who had written "Strangers," along with her husband, Casey Anderson. Liz was the mother of country singer Lynn Anderson, who would later have a huge hit with "Rose Garden." According to the story often told, when the Andersons played the song for Haggard, he was quiet for a moment, then asked them whether they knew he had been in prison. They had no idea. The song simply fit him perfectly. After that, Capitol and Haggard began, carefully, to let the public know about his past. Haggard himself began writing songs that drew directly on what he had lived. In 1967, he released "Branded Man," a song about an ex-convict who cannot escape his record, and it went to number one. Then in 1968 came "Mama Tried," a song he wrote that includes the famous line about turning twenty-one in prison doing life without parole. Now, here is a little secret within the secret. Haggard did not actually get a life sentence. He turned twenty-one in prison, that part was true, but his sentence was not life without parole. He took a bit of artistic license to make the song stronger, and in interviews he was honest about that. The song was also used in the movie "Killers Three," in which Haggard had a small role. "Mama Tried" went to number one, and today it is considered one of the greatest country songs ever written. He wrote it, he said, for his mother, Flossie, who never stopped trying to set him straight.

Secret number five. "Sing Me Back Home" was inspired by a man Haggard knew in prison who was executed. In 1967, Capitol released "Sing Me Back Home," which became another number one hit. The song tells the story of a prisoner being led to his execution who asks a guitar-playing friend to sing him one more song from home. According to Haggard, the story was drawn from real life. He said it was inspired by a fellow inmate at San Quentin, a man he called Rabbit, who reportedly escaped from the prison, shot a police officer while on the run, and was brought back and eventually executed. Haggard said that he himself had been tempted to go along on that escape, and that friends talked him out of it. He said he watched as the man was later led past on his way to the gas chamber. There was another inmate on death row at San Quentin at that time, Caryl Chessman, a convicted kidnapper and robber whose case became internationally famous as he wrote books from his cell. Haggard said that during a stretch in solitary confinement, he was close enough to Chessman to speak with him through the walls. Chessman was executed in 1960. Haggard later said that those experiences, seeing up close what the end of the road looked like, helped persuade him to change. "Sing Me Back Home" is one of the most haunting records Capitol ever released. Haggard sang it for the rest of his life, and many fans consider it his masterpiece. Not long after, he recorded "Hungry Eyes," a song about his mother's hardships in a labor camp for migrant workers, which went to number one in 1969, along with "Workin' Man Blues," which also reached number one that year. Capitol was learning that Haggard's real life made for great records. But some of those truths would soon prove harder for the label to handle.

Secret number four. The real story behind "Okie from Muskogee" is not what most people think. In 1969, Haggard and the Strangers were riding their tour bus through Oklahoma when they passed a sign for the town of Muskogee. According to the most commonly told version of the story, drummer Roy Edward Burris made a joke along the lines of, "I bet they don't smoke marijuana in Muskogee." Haggard laughed and began writing a song. "Okie from Muskogee" was released by Capitol in September of 1969. It was a sly, catchy song in which the narrator proudly declares that the people of Muskogee don't smoke marijuana, don't take LSD, don't burn their draft cards, and still wave Old Glory down at the courthouse. It struck a chord with millions of Americans who felt left behind by the protests and the counterculture of the late sixties. It went to number one on the country chart and crossed into the pop top fifty. The live album "Okie from Muskogee," recorded in Muskogee itself, won the Country Music Association's Album of the Year award in 1970, and Haggard was named the CMA's Entertainer of the Year. But here is the secret. Over the decades, Haggard gave different explanations of what he meant by the song. In some interviews, he said it was written as a kind of humorous character study, a portrait of his father's generation of hardworking Oklahomans. In other interviews, he said he meant every word of it, especially at a time when he was angry about protesters he felt were disrespecting the soldiers in Vietnam. He also said in later years that he had become much more tolerant in his views, and he even joked about how his opinion of marijuana had changed. The truth may be that it meant different things to him at different points in his life. Capitol was more than happy to let the public decide for itself. And it followed the song up in 1970 with "The Fightin' Side of Me," which also went to number one. But the song Haggard actually wanted to release next was very different.

Secret number three. Capitol reportedly talked Merle Haggard out of releasing a song about an interracial romance. After the success of "Okie from Muskogee," Haggard reportedly worried that he was being boxed in as a symbol of one political point of view. He had written a song called "Irma Jackson," about a love affair between a white man and a Black woman, and the social pressures that kept them apart. At a time when interracial marriage had only been legal across the whole country since the Supreme Court's 1967 decision in Loving versus Virginia, it was a bold subject for a country song. Haggard reportedly wanted "Irma Jackson" to be his next single. But according to Haggard's own later accounts, Capitol, and his producer Ken Nelson in particular, advised strongly against it. The label reportedly feared that the song would confuse or alienate the very audience that had just embraced "Okie from Muskogee." Haggard went along with them, and instead "The Fightin' Side of Me" became the next single. "Irma Jackson" was eventually released in 1972, on the album "Let Me Tell You About a Song," but it was never issued as a single by Haggard. In later years, Haggard spoke about the episode with some regret, saying that he felt the song could have shown people a fuller picture of who he really was. Ken Nelson, for his part, was remembered by many artists as a producer who generally let them follow their own instincts. That makes this one decision all the more interesting. It shows just how much pressure there was, at the height of his fame, to keep Merle Haggard in a particular box. And Haggard, it turned out, had other surprises in store for his record company.

Secret number two. At the peak of his stardom, Merle Haggard insisted on recording tribute albums to his heroes. Most stars who reach number one want to keep releasing hit singles. Haggard wanted to pay his debts. In 1969, he released "Same Train, a Different Time," a double album of songs by Jimmie Rodgers, the Singing Brakeman, who had died in 1933 and who many call the father of country music. Then, in 1970, he released "A Tribute to the Best Damn Fiddle Player in the World (or, My Salute to Bob Wills)." The title alone reportedly made some people nervous, because of the word "damn" on a record cover in 1970. To get ready for the Bob Wills album, Haggard reportedly taught himself to play the fiddle in a matter of months, practicing obsessively on the road. He brought several former members of Wills's band, the Texas Playboys, into the studio with him. It was not a typical commercial move, and there was reportedly some uncertainty at the label about whether old-time Western swing could sell to country fans of the nineteen-seventies. But the album did well, and it is widely credited with helping to spark a revival of interest in Western swing music. Later, in 1973, Haggard took part in what turned out to be Bob Wills's final recording sessions, "For the Last Time," though Wills suffered a stroke during the sessions and passed away in 1975. In the same spirit, Haggard recorded a gospel album, "The Land of Many Churches," in 1971, partly recorded live in churches and even in a prison, with Bonnie Owens and the Carter Family. And in 1973 he released "If We Make It Through December," which went to number one on the country chart and crossed into the pop top thirty. For an artist the label wanted to keep on top of the charts, Haggard had a habit of spending his time honoring the men who came before him. And somehow, it always seemed to work. There is one more little Capitol story worth telling here. In 1968, Haggard released a song called "The Legend of Bonnie and Clyde," which went to number one, but its B-side, "Today I Started Loving You Again," turned out to be the bigger surprise. Written by Haggard and Bonnie Owens, it was never released as an A-side by Haggard and never climbed the charts on its own. Yet in the years that followed, it was recorded by dozens of artists, from Bobby Bland to Kenny Rogers to Emmylou Harris, and it is now considered one of the great country love songs. Haggard reportedly found it amusing that one of his most enduring songs had been tucked away on the back of a record, where the label had not expected anyone to notice it. It was a reminder that Haggard's instincts as a songwriter often ran far ahead of the business that sold his work.

Secret number one. The governor who pardoned Merle Haggard, and the label that let him go. In March of 1972, California Governor Ronald Reagan granted Merle Haggard a full and unconditional pardon for his past crimes. It is one of the most remarkable chapters in country music history. A man who had once been inmate number A-45200 at San Quentin was now one of the most celebrated performers in America, and the state that imprisoned him officially wiped his slate clean. That same year, he was invited to perform at the White House for President Richard Nixon, and he appeared there in 1973. Through the mid-nineteen-seventies, Haggard kept recording number one hits for Capitol, songs like "Carolyn," "Grandma Harp," "It's Not Love (But It's Not Bad)," "Things Aren't Funny Anymore," and "Kentucky Gambler." But by the middle of the decade, the music business was changing. Ken Nelson retired from Capitol in 1976, and Haggard soon left the label, signing with MCA Records in 1976 and later with Epic Records in 1981. At Epic he recorded more classics, including "Big City," "Pancho and Lefty" with Willie Nelson, which went to number one in 1983, and "That's the Way Love Goes," which won him a Grammy in 1985. In the years after he left, Capitol kept his old records in print, and later reissues of his catalog have introduced those Capitol classics to new generations. He was inducted into the Country Music Hall of Fame in 1994. In 2006, he received a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award. And in December of 2010, he received the Kennedy Center Honors, one of the highest awards an American artist can receive, in a ceremony attended by President Barack Obama. Merle Haggard passed away on April sixth, 2016, on his seventy-ninth birthday, at his ranch near Redding, California. Before he died, according to his family, he told them that he would go on his birthday. And he did. The boy from the boxcar, the convict who saw Johnny Cash at San Quentin, the Okie who became a poet of the working man, had gone home at last. In the end, his greatest secret was no secret at all. He told us the truth in his songs, every single time.

So there you have it, eight secrets from Merle Haggard's years with Capitol Records. A concert in a prison yard, a song by a woman who had never been behind bars, a death row story, a joke on a tour bus, a song the label reportedly did not want, and a pardon signed by a future president. He once said that he was just an ordinary guy who had made some mistakes and found a way to sing about them. But to millions of us, there was nothing ordinary about him at all.
