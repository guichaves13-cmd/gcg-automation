In 1974, one of the most famous men in the world wanted to record a song written by a young woman from the Smoky Mountains. His name was Elvis Presley. The song was "I Will Always Love You." It should have been the greatest break of her life. Instead, she said no, and she cried all night long after doing it. Why would a country girl from a two-room cabin turn down the King of Rock and Roll? And what did that one decision mean for her fortune, decades later, when another superstar took that same song around the world? The answers are tucked away in the story of her years at RCA Victor, a story that the record company's glossy publicity photos never quite told. Tonight we are going back to those years. These are eight Dolly Parton secrets RCA never told country fans. So get comfortable, maybe put on one of those old records, and let's go home to Tennessee.

Dolly Rebecca Parton was born on January nineteenth, 1946, in Sevier County, Tennessee, the fourth of twelve children born to Robert Lee Parton and Avie Lee Owens Parton. The family lived in a small cabin near the Little Pigeon River, and as Dolly has told the story many times, the doctor who delivered her was paid with a sack of cornmeal. Her father farmed and worked construction. Her mother sang the old mountain ballads that had come over from the British Isles generations before. Dolly was writing songs before she could write them down, and by the age of ten she was performing on Knoxville radio and television on the Cas Walker show. She made her first record as a young teenager, and at thirteen she reportedly appeared on the stage of the Grand Ole Opry, introduced by Johnny Cash. On the day after she graduated from Sevier County High School in 1964, she got on a bus to Nashville with her songs and a cardboard suitcase. She was the first in her family to graduate from high school. Those first months in Nashville were hard. Dolly has said there were days when she had so little money that she lived on condiments and crackers, and she would walk the halls of hotels looking for room service trays left outside doors. She knocked on the doors of publishing houses on Music Row and was turned away more often than not. But she never once thought about going back to the mountains for good. She had made a promise to herself that she would become a star, and she has said that she believed it, with the kind of faith that only a stubborn young woman from the hills can have. Now, let's begin the countdown.

Secret number eight. Dolly Parton met the love of her life on her very first day in Nashville, and for nearly sixty years he stayed almost completely out of sight. According to Dolly herself, on the day she arrived in town in 1964, she went to the Wishy Washy Laundromat to wash her clothes. A young man named Carl Dean drove by, waved, and stopped to talk with her. She has said that what she loved most about him was that he actually looked her in the face when they talked. Carl ran an asphalt paving business. He had no interest at all in show business. They were married on May thirtieth, 1966, in a small ceremony in Ringgold, Georgia, with only her mother and a preacher present. Here is the part that the record company found curious. Dolly's first label, Monument Records, reportedly asked her to delay the wedding, because they worried that a married girl singer would not appeal to male fans. So she went ahead and married him quietly anyway, and she kept the news low-key for some time afterward. Carl Dean rarely appeared with her in public in all the decades that followed. He reportedly attended only one of her events in the early years and found it miserable. That privacy led to endless tabloid speculation about whether he even existed. But Dolly has said for more than half a century that he is the reason she stayed grounded. They renewed their vows for their fiftieth anniversary in 2016. When Carl Dean passed away in March of 2025, at the age of eighty-two, Dolly shared a tender message with her fans, saying that words could not do justice to the love they had shared for more than sixty years. In all that time, there had never been a public scandal, never a messy headline, just a quiet man who mowed his own grass and a wife who came home to him whenever she could. Dolly once said that she wrote many of her love songs with him in mind, even though he was never one to listen to her records much. It was a marriage that outlasted every record deal, every movie, and every business partnership she ever had.

Secret number seven. Dolly's first label tried to make her a pop singer, and it took Porter Wagoner and RCA to make her a country star. When Dolly signed with Monument Records, the label's founder, Fred Foster, reportedly felt her small, high voice would work better on rockabilly and pop material than on traditional country. For a couple of years she recorded songs that did not quite fit her. It was only in 1967, when Monument finally let her cut country songs, that she had her first real hit, "Dumb Blonde," which reached the country top thirty, followed by "Something Fishy." Meanwhile, as a songwriter, she had been working with her uncle, Bill Owens, and had already landed a song, "Put It Off Until Tomorrow," that became a top ten hit for Bill Phillips. Then came a phone call that changed everything. In 1967, Porter Wagoner, a rhinestone-suited star with one of the most popular syndicated television programs in country music, needed a new girl singer after Norma Jean Beasler left his show. He hired Dolly. And Porter was an RCA Victor artist. He reportedly persuaded RCA to sign her, and according to several accounts, he went so far as to offer to cover the cost if her early records lost money. Her first RCA single with Porter, a cover of Tom Paxton's "The Last Thing on My Mind," reached the country top ten in early 1968. Porter's audience, however, was not happy at first. They loved Norma Jean, and according to Dolly, fans shouted for her predecessor in the early shows. It took time and talent, but she won them over. By the end of the decade, Porter and Dolly were one of the top duet acts in country music, winning the Country Music Association's Vocal Group of the Year award in 1968 and Vocal Duo of the Year in 1970 and 1971.

Secret number six. One of Dolly Parton's most beloved songs was written on the back of a dry-cleaning receipt. In 1969, Dolly was riding on Porter Wagoner's tour bus, and she began thinking about her childhood. She remembered a little coat her mother had sewn for her out of a box of colorful rags. While she worked, her mother told her the Bible story of Joseph and his coat of many colors. When Dolly wore it proudly to school, the other children laughed at her because it was made of rags. As Dolly has told it, she had no paper on the bus, so she grabbed the nearest thing she could find, which happened to be a dry-cleaning receipt from one of Porter's fancy suits. She wrote "Coat of Many Colors" right there. Porter reportedly kept that receipt, and it was later framed and displayed. When it was released on RCA in 1971, the song reached the country top five. It was never her biggest chart hit, but Dolly has said again and again that it is her favorite of all the songs she has written. Why does this belong among the secrets? Because, for all the wigs and rhinestones and stage-show glamour RCA put in front of the public, the label rarely let audiences see just how deep her writing went. Dolly was not simply a voice. She was one of the most prolific songwriters in the history of American music. She has said she has written thousands of songs, many never recorded. And in an era when many female country singers relied on male songwriters, Dolly was writing nearly all of her own hits. "Joshua," which went to number one in 1971, was her first solo chart-topper, and she wrote that one herself too. Then, in 1973, RCA released an album called "My Tennessee Mountain Home," a collection of songs about her childhood, her parents, and the cabin where she grew up. The cover even featured a photograph of the real Parton family home. It was a concept album at a time when few country women were allowed to make them, and it showed that Dolly could tell the story of an entire life in a set of songs. Critics in later years would rank it among the finest country albums of the decade. Songs like "In the Good Old Days (When Times Were Bad)" took a clear-eyed look at poverty, refusing to pretend that hunger and hardship were romantic. That honesty was something the label did not always know how to sell, but it is a big part of why her music has lasted.

Secret number five. "Jolene" was inspired by a real woman at a bank, and it was reportedly written on the same day as another classic. "Jolene" came out on RCA in late 1973 and went to number one on the Billboard country chart in early 1974. It reached the pop chart in America and later became a top ten hit in the United Kingdom. The song is a plea from a woman to a red-haired beauty, begging her not to take her man just because she can. According to Dolly herself, the inspiration came from a pretty red-headed bank teller who had developed a crush on Carl. Dolly has said, laughing, that her husband loved going to the bank during that period. She has also said that the name Jolene came from a sweet little girl with red hair who came up on stage to ask for her autograph. Dolly liked the name so much, she told the girl she was going to write a song about it. And here is the remarkable claim that Dolly has repeated in interviews over the years. She has said that she wrote "Jolene" and "I Will Always Love You" on the same day. Whether every detail of that story is exactly right, only Dolly knows for certain, but it is surely one of the most productive afternoons any songwriter ever had. And the song she wrote that day for Porter Wagoner would turn out to be the most valuable piece of paper she ever filled.

Secret number four. "I Will Always Love You" was a goodbye letter to Porter Wagoner. By the early nineteen-seventies, Dolly had become a bigger star than the show that made her. She wanted to go out on her own. Porter, by most accounts, did not want her to leave. He was her duet partner, her producer at RCA, and a business partner in their publishing company, Owepar. The partnership had become strained. According to Dolly, she tried to tell him she was leaving, and he would not listen. So she did what she did best. She wrote him a song. She went home, wrote "I Will Always Love You," and brought it to him the next day. She has said that when she sang it for him, he cried, and he told her it was the prettiest song he had ever heard. He agreed to let her go, on the condition that he could produce the record. Dolly left the Porter Wagoner Show in 1974. Released on RCA that year, "I Will Always Love You" went to number one on the country chart. But the break was not entirely peaceful. In 1979, Porter Wagoner filed a lawsuit against Dolly, reportedly seeking around three million dollars over their business arrangements. The case was settled out of court, and the terms were never fully made public. Years later, the two made their peace. When Porter was inducted into the Country Music Hall of Fame in 2002, Dolly sang at the ceremony. And when Porter was dying in 2007, Dolly visited him in the hospital. She has spoken of him with real affection ever since, calling him a mentor and giving him credit for her early success. And the song itself was about to become very valuable indeed.

Secret number three. Dolly turned down Elvis Presley. Not long after "I Will Always Love You" became a hit, word came that Elvis Presley wanted to record it. Dolly has told this story many times. She was thrilled. Then, according to her, Elvis's manager, Colonel Tom Parker, told her that it was standard practice for Elvis to receive half of the publishing rights on any song he recorded. Dolly had already learned the hard way how important publishing was. She owned her songs, and she was not willing to give half of one away, not even to Elvis. She said no. She has said that she cried all night afterward, because she could so clearly imagine how Elvis would have sounded singing it. Many people in the music business thought she was out of her mind. But she has described that decision as one of the best business choices of her life. In 1982, she re-recorded the song for the film version of "The Best Little Whorehouse in Texas," co-starring Burt Reynolds, and it went to number one on the country chart a second time. Then, in 1992, Whitney Houston recorded the song for the movie "The Bodyguard." Whitney's version spent fourteen weeks at number one on the Billboard Hot 100, and the soundtrack became one of the best-selling albums in history. Because Dolly still owned the song, she received songwriter and publishing royalties from all of it. Dolly has joked that she earned enough from Whitney's version to buy Graceland, if she had wanted it. That night of tears, it turned out, was worth a fortune.

Secret number two. RCA and Dolly gambled her country career on a crossover, and Nashville did not forgive her easily. After leaving Porter, Dolly made a bold decision. In 1976 she hired a new management team in Los Angeles, and she set out to become a mainstream pop star. Many in Nashville felt betrayed. Country radio programmers and some longtime fans grumbled that she had abandoned her roots. Dolly answered them with a line she has repeated for decades. She said she was not leaving country, she was taking it with her. In 1977, RCA released "Here You Come Again," a song written by the pop songwriting team of Barry Mann and Cynthia Weil. It went to number one on the country chart and number three on the pop chart, and the album became her first to be certified platinum. She won the Grammy for Best Female Country Vocal Performance for it. In 1978, the Country Music Association named her Entertainer of the Year. Then came Hollywood. In 1980 she starred with Jane Fonda and Lily Tomlin in "Nine to Five," and her title song, which she wrote herself, reached number one on both the Billboard Hot 100 and the country chart. It earned an Academy Award nomination and won two Grammy Awards. In 1983, she recorded "Islands in the Stream" with Kenny Rogers, a song written by the Bee Gees. It went to number one on both the pop and country charts. By then, the farm girl from Sevier County was one of the most famous entertainers in the world. But she was also, reportedly, frustrated that some of her pop records were not as strong as her country records, and in later years she admitted that a few of the slicker productions of that era were not her favorites. There was also a television variety show in 1976 called "Dolly," a syndicated program that she later said she did not enjoy, because she felt she had too little control over it. Then, in 1987, ABC gave her a big-budget variety series, also called "Dolly," which lasted a single season. These were bumps in the road, but they taught her an important lesson. Whenever she let other people decide who she was supposed to be, things did not go as well. Whenever she trusted her own instincts, she usually came out on top. Before long, the RCA chapter would come to a close.

Secret number one. The real Dolly Parton fortune was not in the records. It was in the paper she wrote them on. In 1986, Dolly's long relationship with RCA came to an end, and she soon signed with Columbia Records, where she recorded hits like "Why'd You Come in Here Lookin' Like That" and "Yellow Roses." In 1987, she also joined Emmylou Harris and Linda Ronstadt on the album "Trio," which won a Grammy and reached the top of the country album chart. That same decade, in 1986, she opened Dollywood, the theme park in Pigeon Forge, Tennessee, near the hills where she grew up. In 1995, she started the Imagination Library, which mails free books to young children, and it has since given away well over two hundred million books around the world. But the secret that ties it all together is this. From the beginning, Dolly understood that songwriters who keep their publishing keep their future. Through her own companies, including Velvet Apple Music, she retained the rights to much of her catalog. Every time "Jolene" is played on the radio, every time a new artist covers "I Will Always Love You," every time "Nine to Five" is used in a movie or a commercial, the money flows back to the girl who wrote it. Record labels came and went. RCA, Columbia, and others all released her records. But the songs remained hers. In 1999, she was inducted into the Country Music Hall of Fame. In 2005, she received the National Medal of Arts. And in 2022, she was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame, an honor she first tried to decline because she felt she had not earned it, before accepting it graciously. She even went on to record a full rock album, "Rockstar," released in 2023, with guests including Paul McCartney, Ringo Starr, Elton John, and Stevie Nicks. Not bad for a girl who once lived on crackers in a Nashville boarding house. And through it all, she has kept giving. During the pandemic in 2020, she donated one million dollars to Vanderbilt University Medical Center for coronavirus research, money that helped fund work on one of the vaccines. When wildfires swept through the Smoky Mountains near Gatlinburg in 2016, her foundation gave monthly payments to families who had lost their homes. She has said many times that she never forgot what it felt like to have nothing. That is the real secret of Dolly Parton. Behind the wigs and the laughter was one of the sharpest business minds the music industry has ever seen, and a heart big enough to give so much of it away.

And there you have it, eight secrets from Dolly Parton's journey with RCA, from a laundromat in Nashville to a coat of many colors, from a goodbye to Porter to a fortune built on her own pen. She once said that it costs a lot of money to look this cheap. But the truth is, everything that mattered about Dolly was real.
