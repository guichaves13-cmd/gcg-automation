He had more number one records than almost anyone who ever sang country music. He could make a whole room of grown women go quiet with three words: hello, darlin'. And yet, for all the years he spent on the radio, on the road, and on the stage, most fans never really knew the man behind the voice. They never knew where his name came from. They never knew about the baseball career he walked away from, the hamburger chain that nearly sank him, or the song that radio stations refused to play. Tonight, we're going to pull back the curtain on one of the most beloved and most private stars Nashville ever produced. These are eight Conway Twitty secrets the record company never put on the album jacket. And the last one, the very last one, still breaks hearts more than thirty years later. So pour yourself a cup of coffee, get comfortable, and let's go back in time together.

Before we start, think about where you were the first time you heard that voice. Maybe it was on a car radio on a summer night, maybe it was on a jukebox in a diner off the highway, maybe it was on a record your mother played over and over in the kitchen. For millions of Americans, Conway Twitty was not just a singer. He was part of the furniture of life. And that's exactly why these stories matter.

Secret number eight. Conway Twitty was never his real name, and the name he was born with came from the movies.

The man we know as Conway Twitty was born Harold Lloyd Jenkins on September first, 1933, in Friars Point, Mississippi, a little river town in the Delta. His family named him after Harold Lloyd, the silent film comedian famous for hanging from the hands of a giant clock high above a city street. It's a funny thing to think about, isn't it? The smoothest ballad singer in country music, named after a slapstick comic from the days of silent pictures.

His father worked as a riverboat pilot, and when Harold was still a boy, the family moved across the Mississippi to Helena, Arkansas. Helena was a music town in those days. The radio carried the blues from local stations, and young Harold soaked up everything he heard, from gospel in church to country on the Opry broadcasts to the rhythm and blues that drifted out of juke joints along the river. By the time he was ten years old he had formed his first little band.

But Harold Jenkins did not sound like a star's name, at least not to the music business of the 1950s. So in 1957, as the story goes, he and his manager sat down with a road map. They looked for something unusual, something people would remember. His eye landed on Conway, Arkansas. Then it landed on Twitty, Texas. Put them together and you get a name that sounds like nobody else on earth. Conway Twitty. Some people laughed at it at first. Nobody was laughing a few years later, when that strange new name was sitting at the top of the charts.

Here's the part many fans never realized. Even after decades of fame, the people closest to him still called him Harold, or by family nicknames. The stage name belonged to the public. The man behind it kept a part of himself for the folks back home.

Secret number seven. Before music, Conway Twitty was reportedly headed for a career in professional baseball.

Long before he ever stood in front of a microphone, young Harold Jenkins was a gifted ballplayer. He played the game hard growing up in Arkansas, and according to many accounts of his life, he was good enough to draw the attention of the Philadelphia Phillies. The story told for years is that the Phillies offered him a contract after he finished high school.

Then history stepped in, the way it did for so many young men of that generation. Uncle Sam came calling. Harold Jenkins was drafted into the United States Army and served overseas, including time in Japan. And it was there, far from the cotton fields and the river, that the music started to take over. He put together a band with other servicemen, a group that played for the troops, and he discovered what it felt like to hold an audience in the palm of his hand.

When he came home, he had a choice to make. Baseball, or music. Now, imagine what that must have felt like for a young man from a working family in the South. A professional baseball contract was a real opportunity, a steady path. Music was a gamble. But around that same time, something was happening down the road in Memphis. A young truck driver named Elvis Presley had walked into Sun Records, and the whole world was about to change. Harold Jenkins heard that sound, and reportedly said he knew right then what he was meant to do.

He loved baseball his whole life. In fact, years later he was involved with a minor league team in Nashville, the Sounds, as one of a group of country music investors. But he chose the guitar over the glove. And country music has been grateful ever since.

Secret number six. Conway Twitty's first great hit wasn't country at all, and it was written in a matter of minutes.

Here's something that surprises younger fans and even some longtime ones. Conway Twitty became famous as a rock and roll singer. Not a country singer. A rock and roller, in the same lane as Elvis, Jerry Lee Lewis, and Carl Perkins.

He did go to Sun Records in Memphis, the same studio where Elvis got his start, and he cut some recordings there with Sam Phillips. But those early sides didn't take off. It was only after he signed with MGM Records that lightning struck.

In 1958, Conway and his drummer, Jack Nance, were playing a club in Hamilton, Ontario, up in Canada. During a break between sets, the two of them sat down and wrote a song. By most accounts, it took them only a few minutes. That song was "It's Only Make Believe." It went on to become a number one record on the Billboard Hot 100, and it sold millions of copies around the world. Think about that. Something written on a break in a Canadian nightclub became one of the defining records of the entire decade.

That song was so powerful, and the voice was so unusual, that many listeners at first thought they were hearing Elvis Presley under a different name. Conway had a growl, a catch, a little quiver in his voice that sounded a lot like the King to some ears. It was a rumor that followed him for a while, and he didn't mind the attention one bit.

He went on to more pop success, including "Lonely Blue Boy" and "Danny Boy." He even appeared in a handful of teen movies of the era. And here's a fun bit of trivia. The character Conrad Birdie in the Broadway musical "Bye Bye Birdie" is widely understood to have been a play on Elvis, but the name itself was a playful nod to Conway Twitty. Conrad Birdie, Conway Twitty. Say them both out loud and you'll hear it.

But inside, Conway was restless. The rock and roll business was changing fast, and he felt like he was playing a part. He had always loved country music. And in the middle of the 1960s, he made a decision that people in the business thought was crazy.

Secret number five. Some country radio stations reportedly refused to play Conway Twitty at first, because they didn't trust a rock and roll star.

When Conway announced he was leaving rock and roll behind to sing country music, a lot of people in Nashville were suspicious. He had been a teen idol with slicked back hair. Country disc jockeys of that era were protective of their music, and some of them reportedly took one look at the name Conway Twitty and decided he was just another pop singer trying to cash in.

There is a well known story, told in several accounts of his career, that some stations would not play his early country records at all. Conway had to start over, practically from the bottom. He worked the small clubs and the long highways. He signed with Decca Records, the label that would later become MCA, and he went to work with one of the greatest producers Nashville ever knew, Owen Bradley. Bradley had produced Patsy Cline, Brenda Lee, and Loretta Lynn. He understood how to frame a voice, and he understood Conway.

It took time. But in 1968 Conway reached number one on the country charts with "Next in Line." And then, in 1970, came the song that changed everything. "Hello Darlin'." He wrote it himself. It spent weeks at the top of the country chart, and it became his signature, the song he opened with, the song people requested at weddings and anniversaries for the next fifty years. That spoken opening, that soft "hello darlin', nice to see you," became one of the most recognizable moments in all of country music.

Here's the remarkable part. "Hello Darlin'" was so beloved that years later, in 1975, a recording of it was reportedly sent into space. Copies were given to the astronauts and cosmonauts in connection with the Apollo-Soyuz mission, the joint American and Soviet flight, and Conway even recorded a version with a verse in Russian as a gesture of friendship. A man who had been rejected by some country stations ended up with his song floating above the Earth.

From then on, the hits came like rain. Conway Twitty would go on to collect more than forty number one hits on the Billboard country chart, and depending on which charts you count, his own camp liked to say the number was fifty-five. Either way, it was one of the most remarkable runs in the history of popular music.

Secret number four. The Twitty Burger, the restaurant chain Conway believed would make him rich.

Now here's a story that a lot of fans have never heard, and it's one of the most human stories in his whole career.

In the early 1970s, with his fame at its peak, Conway Twitty decided to go into the fast food business. He started a hamburger chain called Twitty Burger. The idea was simple. Fast food was booming across America, and celebrity names were being put on everything. Conway had his own signature burger in mind, reportedly topped with a pineapple ring, and he had big dreams of seeing Twitty Burger signs all across the country.

He raised money from people who believed in him, and according to many accounts, a number of those investors were friends and fellow musicians. A few restaurants opened. But the business never caught on the way he hoped. Within a short time, the chain had failed.

And here is the part of the story that tells you about the man. Conway reportedly felt so badly about the people who had trusted him with their money that he set out to make it right. The story told by those around him is that he paid his investors back. And he did it, as the legend goes, the only way a singer can. With his music. Conway Twitty reportedly recorded and wrote with the idea of making good on every dollar, and he worked for years to put that failed venture behind him.

Whether every detail of that story is exact, we may never know. But it's been told so often, by so many who knew him, that it has become part of how people remember Conway. A man who failed in business but refused to fail his friends. In an industry full of broken promises, that counts for something.

The Twitty Burger also became a bit of a joke later on, even showing up in a famous gag on an animated television show decades afterward. But for Conway, it was never funny. It was a lesson he carried for the rest of his life.

Secret number three. One of his biggest hits was reportedly banned by some radio stations for being too suggestive.

Today, when we think of Conway Twitty, we think of romance. Love songs sung so softly it felt like he was singing right to you. But in his own day, some of those love songs raised eyebrows. More than raised eyebrows. They raised complaints.

In 1973, Conway released a song he wrote called "You've Never Been This Far Before." It's a tender, slow song about two people sharing a moment of intimacy for the first time. By today's standards, it's mild. But in 1973, on country radio, it was a sensation of a different kind. Some stations reportedly pulled it from the air, believing it was too suggestive for family listeners. Some program directors received letters from offended listeners.

And yet, the public didn't care what the program directors thought. The song shot to number one on the country chart and even crossed over onto the pop chart. Conway Twitty had figured out something that many of his peers hadn't. The women who bought country records wanted to hear a man sing to them honestly about love and longing, not just about trucks and heartache and drinking.

He became known as "The High Priest of Country Music," a nickname that came from his serious, almost reverent way of delivering a love song. He would stand nearly still at the microphone, eyes closed, letting the words do the work. No wild stage moves. Just that voice. Many observers noted that he hardly spoke to the audience between songs, either. He believed the songs should speak for themselves.

Later hits continued that tradition. "Linda on My Mind." "I'd Love to Lay You Down." "Tight Fittin' Jeans." "Slow Hand." "I See the Want To in Your Eyes." Each one walked right up to the line of what country radio considered acceptable, and each one found a huge audience. And it's worth remembering that some of his biggest hits of the early 1980s came while he had moved over to Elektra and then Warner Bros. Records, before he eventually returned home to MCA later in the decade. The business side of his career was more complicated than fans ever saw.

Secret number two. The legendary partnership with Loretta Lynn, and the rumors they had to live with.

You can't tell the story of Conway Twitty without Loretta Lynn. Together they were one of the greatest duet teams in the history of American music.

They were both on the Decca label, both produced by Owen Bradley, and in 1971 they released "After the Fire Is Gone." It went to number one, and it won them a Grammy Award for Best Country Vocal Performance by a Duo or Group. Then came "Lead Me On." Then "Louisiana Woman, Mississippi Man," a song that played on their home states and became a classic. Then "As Soon as I Hang Up the Phone," "Feelins'," and many more. The Country Music Association named them Vocal Duo of the Year four years in a row, from 1972 through 1975.

Their chemistry was so believable that many fans were absolutely convinced they had to be in love in real life. Rumors swirled for years. People would show up to concerts asking if they were married. Some fans reportedly thought that Loretta was Conway's wife, and others wrote letters with questions they had no business asking.

But according to Loretta Lynn herself, who wrote about him with great affection, the truth was simpler and in some ways more beautiful. They were the best of friends. Loretta was married to her husband Doolittle, called Doo, for nearly fifty years, and Conway had his own family. What they shared was trust, deep respect, and a musical connection you can't manufacture.

Loretta said publicly that the two of them were careful about how they behaved off stage, precisely because of the rumors. She reportedly said they knew that the public saw them as a couple in song, and they didn't want to give anyone a reason to doubt the families waiting for them at home. She also wrote a book about their friendship, years after he passed, and in it she made clear how much she missed her singing partner. When Conway Twitty died, Loretta said she lost one of the most important people in her life.

Here's another detail many fans didn't know. Conway and Loretta were also business partners. They worked together on a booking agency, United Talent, and they made decisions together about their careers that went beyond just stepping in front of a microphone. Their friendship was built on respect, and it lasted until the very end.

Before we get to our number one secret, there's one more story worth telling, because it shows how much Conway Twitty loved his fans. In 1982, he opened a place called Twitty City in Hendersonville, Tennessee, just outside Nashville. It was a tourist attraction built around his home, with gardens, a museum, gift shops, and Christmas lights that drew crowds every holiday season. Fans could walk the grounds where their hero actually lived. He reportedly enjoyed seeing the visitors come and go, and Twitty City became one of the busiest stops for country fans visiting the Nashville area. After he passed, the property was sold, and it later became the home of a Christian broadcasting network. But for a few golden years, it was a place where fans felt like they were being welcomed into his front yard.

And now, secret number one. Conway Twitty's sudden death, and the battle that followed it.

In the spring of 1993, Conway Twitty was fifty-nine years old and still working hard. He was still touring, still recording, still filling theaters. He had just finished work on a new album, which would be titled "Final Touches." Nobody knew how fitting that title would turn out to be.

On June fourth, 1993, he performed at the Jim Stafford Theatre in Branson, Missouri. By all accounts, it was a good show. Afterward, he climbed aboard his tour bus. And somewhere on that bus, in the hours after the show, he collapsed in pain. He was rushed to a hospital in Springfield, Missouri. Doctors found he had suffered an abdominal aortic aneurysm, a ruptured blood vessel. They operated, but they could not save him. Conway Twitty died early on the morning of June fifth, 1993.

The news shocked the country music world. Fans gathered outside Twitty City. Radio stations played "Hello Darlin'" over and over. His funeral drew stars, friends, and thousands of mourners. Loretta Lynn was devastated. Reportedly, he had been singing to the very end, and that's how he would have wanted it.

But what followed was painful, and many fans never knew the details. Conway Twitty had not left a will that settled everything clearly, at least not one that reflected his whole life and his family at the time of his death. He had married Dee Henry only a few years before he passed, and he had four grown children from earlier marriages. What came next was a long, public legal dispute over his estate between his widow and his children. It was covered by newspapers, and it involved Twitty City, his music catalog, and his personal belongings. Eventually, the matter went to court, and many of his possessions were sold at auction. Fans were able to buy items that had once belonged to their hero, but the whole thing was heartbreaking to watch.

We won't take sides here, because families go through grief in their own ways, and there were real people with real feelings on every side of that dispute. But it's a reminder that even the brightest stars leave behind ordinary human complications.

In 1999, Conway Twitty was inducted into the Country Music Hall of Fame. His children have worked over the years to keep his music alive, and his voice still plays on classic country stations and satellite radio every single day. His grandchildren and fans have kept his memory warm. And somewhere, every night, someone puts on "Hello Darlin'" and remembers a first dance, a lost love, or a long drive home.

He was born Harold Lloyd Jenkins, named for a silent film comedian. He gave up baseball for a guitar. He had a number one rock and roll hit before most country fans ever knew his name. He was turned away by some radio stations, and then he outlasted them all. He lost money on hamburgers and reportedly paid his friends back with songs. He sang love songs so honestly some stations wouldn't play them. He shared a stage and a lifelong friendship with Loretta Lynn. And he left this world far too soon, on a tour bus, doing what he loved.

That's the Conway Twitty the album jackets never showed you.
