It was the corniest show on television, and it was a ratings smash. It was canceled by a network that was embarrassed by it, and then it ran for more than twenty years anyway. Behind those painted barn walls and the cornfield jokes, there were secrets most viewers never imagined. A whole season filmed in just a few weeks. A beloved cast member who reportedly could barely read the cue cards. A phone number that became a legend. And a tragedy so dark that it still haunts Nashville to this day. Tonight, we're heading back to Kornfield Kounty. These are eight Hee Haw secrets CBS didn't want you to know. Stick around until the end, because our number one secret is the one that changed the show forever.

If you grew up in America in the 1970s or 1980s, you probably remember Saturday nights with Hee Haw. Maybe your grandparents watched it. Maybe your whole family gathered around the set. You remember the cartoon donkey, the pickin' and grinnin', the Hee Haw Honeys, and Grandpa Jones asking what's for supper. It felt simple. It felt like home. But the story behind it was anything but simple.

Secret number eight. Hee Haw was created by two Canadians, not by country folks from the South.

Here's something that surprises nearly everyone. The most country show in television history was dreamed up by two men from Canada. Their names were Frank Peppiatt and John Aylesworth, and they were experienced comedy writers who had worked on big network variety shows. They had never lived in a cornfield. They had never milked a cow on camera. But they knew television, and they had noticed something.

In the late 1960s, one of the hottest shows on TV was "Rowan and Martin's Laugh-In," a fast-paced comedy show built on quick jokes, blackout sketches, and a cast of regulars popping out of windows in a joke wall. Peppiatt and Aylesworth looked at that format and had an idea. What if you took the same rapid-fire style and moved it to the country? Swap the go-go dancers for farm girls in gingham. Swap the joke wall for a cornfield. And add something Laugh-In never had: real country music by some of the best musicians in America.

They also noticed that rural audiences were watching a lot of television, and that country music stars were drawing big crowds. The idea of a country version of Laugh-In seemed like a natural fit. They brought it to CBS. And CBS was looking for something to fill a gap.

So the next time someone tells you Hee Haw was a homegrown hillbilly production, you can smile and tell them it was born in the minds of two sharp Canadian writers who knew exactly what they were doing.

Secret number seven. Hee Haw only got on the air because of a controversy over another show.

In 1969, CBS had a big problem. One of its shows was "The Smothers Brothers Comedy Hour," starring Tom and Dick Smothers. It was popular with younger viewers, but it was constantly clashing with the network over political humor and content. In the spring of 1969, CBS abruptly pulled the plug on the Smothers Brothers. Suddenly, there was a hole in the schedule.

CBS needed a summer replacement, and Hee Haw got the slot. It premiered on June fifteenth, 1969. Think about the irony here. One of the most controversial, edgy shows of the era was replaced by one of the gentlest, most wholesome shows ever made. Out went political satire. In came banjo pickers and jokes about the outhouse.

The network didn't expect much. It was just meant to fill time over the summer. But something unexpected happened. People watched. A lot of people. The ratings were strong right out of the gate, and CBS brought the show back as a regular series.

To host it, the producers chose two of the finest musicians in country music. Buck Owens, the king of the Bakersfield sound, with his bright Telecaster twang and his hits like "Act Naturally" and "Together Again." And Roy Clark, a dazzling guitar and banjo player who could play just about anything with strings and make you laugh while he did it. Together, Buck and Roy gave Hee Haw something the critics overlooked. Real musical credibility. Those two men could play, and everybody in Nashville knew it.

Secret number six. CBS canceled Hee Haw while it was still a hit, in what became known as the rural purge.

This is the secret that the network would probably prefer you forget. By 1971, Hee Haw was doing well in the ratings. It had a loyal audience. And CBS canceled it anyway.

Why would a network cancel a successful show? The answer had to do with advertising. In those years, the networks began paying closer attention not just to how many people watched, but to who was watching. Advertisers wanted younger viewers, city viewers, viewers with money to spend on the newest products. Shows that were popular with rural and older audiences suddenly looked less valuable on paper, even when millions of people tuned in.

So, in 1971, CBS cleaned house. It canceled a whole group of country-flavored and rural shows, including "The Beverly Hillbillies," "Green Acres," "Mayberry R.F.D.," and "Hee Haw." "Petticoat Junction" had already been shown the door the year before. The episode has gone down in television history under a nickname. The rural purge. One comic famously joked that CBS canceled everything with a tree in it.

For loyal viewers in small towns across America, it felt like an insult. Their shows, the ones that reflected their lives, were being taken away because they weren't the right kind of audience.

But the producers of Hee Haw didn't give up. They made a bold move. Instead of going away, they took the show into first-run syndication. That meant selling it directly to individual local stations across the country, instead of relying on a network. It was a risky strategy in those days. And it worked better than anyone dreamed. Hee Haw went on to run in syndication from 1971 all the way to the early 1990s, airing on hundreds of stations. It outlived many of the shows that had replaced it on CBS. Talk about having the last laugh.

Secret number five. An entire season of Hee Haw was filmed in just a few weeks, and the stars shot their parts out of order.

Here's a behind-the-scenes secret that explains a lot about how Hee Haw worked. When you watched the show, it felt like the cast had gathered in the cornfield every week. In reality, the production was a remarkably efficient machine.

Hee Haw was taped in Nashville, first at the studios of WLAC-TV and later at facilities at Opryland. And the show was shot in big blocks. According to the show's longtime producer, Sam Lovullo, who wrote about his years with the program, the team would tape a whole season's worth of material in a couple of concentrated sessions each year, often over just a few weeks at a time.

How was that possible? Because the show was built from short pieces. The jokes, the sketches, the songs, the cornfield gags, the barbershop scenes, the pickin' and grinnin' segments. Each one could be filmed separately and then edited together later into different episodes. So a cast member might film dozens of jokes in a single afternoon, wearing the same overalls, then change into a different shirt and film dozens more. Musical guests would come in, tape their songs, and leave. The editors later mixed and matched everything into complete shows.

This was especially convenient for Buck Owens, who had a busy recording and touring career and a business empire back in Bakersfield, California. Buck reportedly would fly in, tape all his segments for the season in a short burst, and fly home. Many of the guest stars did the same. It's one reason so many big names were willing to appear. It didn't take up much of their time.

So the next time you see an old episode, pay attention. You might notice the same jokes and the same outfits turning up in different episodes. Now you know why.

That efficiency also shaped the look of the show. The famous cornfield, where cast members popped up between the stalks to deliver a one-liner, was a set built inside a studio. The barn, the general store, the barbershop, and the front porch were all sets too, arranged so the crew could move quickly from one to the next. The cast members would line up, step into their marks, deliver their jokes one after another, and move on. Some of the performers reportedly said it felt less like making a television show and more like working an assembly line, only a very friendly one, full of laughter and coffee and old friends catching up between takes.

And then there were the Hee Haw Honeys. Dressed in gingham and calico, they became one of the show's trademarks. Over the years, the group included performers like Misty Rowe, Gunilla Hutton, Lisa Todd, Linda Thompson, Marianne Gordon, and Barbi Benton, among others. Many people forget that some of those women were talented singers, actresses, and comedians who went on to other careers. Gunilla Hutton, for example, had already played one of the Bradley daughters on "Petticoat Junction" before she joined Hee Haw, where she also played the nurse in Doc Campbell's office sketches. Linda Thompson, who had dated Elvis Presley for several years in the 1970s, later became a successful songwriter. The Honeys were part of the fun, and they were a big part of why the show felt like a family reunion every week.

Secret number four. Junior Samples, one of the most beloved stars, reportedly struggled to read his cue cards, and the show worked around it.

Of all the characters on Hee Haw, few were more lovable than Junior Samples. He was a big, slow-talking fellow from Georgia with an easy grin and a voice like molasses. He wasn't an actor by training. In fact, before Hee Haw, he had worked in the sawmill business in Georgia.

His road to television came from a fishing story. Junior Samples was interviewed by a Georgia game warden about a fish he'd reportedly caught, and he told a tall tale about a giant bass in such a funny, rambling way that a recording of it got passed around and eventually released as a comedy record. People in Nashville heard it and fell in love with his voice. And that's how a sawmill worker ended up on national television.

But there was a challenge. According to many accounts, including those from people who worked on the show, Junior had difficulty reading the cue cards. He had limited formal schooling. The writers and crew reportedly adapted, writing his lines in large letters, keeping his jokes short, and letting him deliver them in his own natural style. Sometimes he'd stumble, and they'd keep it in, because the stumbles were part of his charm.

His most famous bit was the used car salesman sketch. Junior would sit in front of a beat-up car lot and tell viewers to call him at BR-549. That phone number became a pop culture legend. It was so famous that, years later, a Nashville honky-tonk band named themselves BR549 in tribute.

Junior Samples passed away in 1983, but generations of fans still smile when they hear those three letters and three numbers. Sometimes the people with the least polish leave the biggest mark.

Secret number three. Many of Hee Haw's cast members were serious musicians and Grand Ole Opry legends, not just comedians.

When people make fun of Hee Haw, they tend to remember the corny jokes and the overalls. What they forget is that the show was packed with some of the most important figures in country music history.

Take Grandpa Jones. Viewers knew him as the old fellow in suspenders who'd shout from the kitchen, asking what was for supper, while the cast answered with a rhyming menu. But Grandpa Jones, whose real name was Louis Marshall Jones, was a respected banjo player and singer, a longtime member of the Grand Ole Opry, and he was inducted into the Country Music Hall of Fame. He'd been playing the part of an old man since he was in his twenties.

Then there was Minnie Pearl, with her straw hat and its dangling price tag, and her famous greeting, "How-DEE! I'm just so proud to be here!" Off stage, she was Sarah Ophelia Colley Cannon, a well-educated woman from a prominent family in Centerville, Tennessee, who had studied theater. Minnie Pearl was a character she created and perfected over decades on the Opry.

Archie Campbell, who played the barber and told those wonderfully tangled stories like his famous "Rindercella," was also a major writer on the show and a talented performer in his own right. Gordie Tapp, another Canadian, wrote and performed for the show for many years. Lulu Roman brought laughter, and later in life, a gospel music career. And Roy Clark himself was named the Country Music Association's Entertainer of the Year in 1973, a top honor in the business.

The guest list was extraordinary too. Over the years, stars like Loretta Lynn, Johnny Cash, Tammy Wynette, Merle Haggard, Dolly Parton, Charley Pride, the Statler Brothers, Reba McEntire, and Garth Brooks appeared on the show. For many younger artists, an appearance on Hee Haw meant they had arrived. The comedy was the wrapping paper. The music was the gift.

Some of the show's most famous musical bits were really comedy wrapped in harmony. Do you remember the gloomy group of fellows sitting slumped together, singing "Gloom, despair, and agony on me"? Or the cast sitting on a hay wagon, singing "Pfft! You Was Gone" before someone got swatted? Those little numbers were written to be catchy, and they stuck in viewers' heads for decades. And then there was the gospel quartet. Near the end of many episodes, a group of cast members would gather, often including Buck Owens, Roy Clark, Grandpa Jones, and Kenny Price, and they would sing an old hymn, straight and sincere, with no jokes at all. For a lot of viewers, that was the moment they waited for all week. After half an hour of silliness, the show would pause and honor the church music so many rural families had grown up with. It's one of the clearest signs that the people making Hee Haw understood their audience, and respected them.

Kenny Price, by the way, was known as "The Round Mound of Sound," a fine singer with a string of country hits of his own. Like so many on that stage, he was far more than a punchline.

Secret number two. Buck Owens reportedly came to regret his years on Hee Haw.

Here's a secret that may surprise devoted fans. Buck Owens, one of the two faces most associated with Hee Haw, reportedly had mixed feelings about the show for much of his later life.

Before Hee Haw, Buck Owens was one of the hottest recording artists in country music. Through the 1960s, he and his band, the Buckaroos, had a long string of number one country hits. His sound, raw and electric and full of energy, was a direct challenge to the smooth, string-filled productions coming out of Nashville. He was a musician's musician. The Beatles even recorded his song "Act Naturally."

But as the years of Hee Haw went on, many people came to know Buck more as the grinning fellow in the overalls telling jokes than as the groundbreaking musician. His record sales slowed during the 1970s, and the tragic death of his close friend and guitarist Don Rich in a motorcycle accident in 1974 hit him very hard. In interviews later in life, Buck reportedly said that the show had affected how people saw him as an artist, and that he felt it overshadowed his music. He left Hee Haw in 1986.

Then something wonderful happened. In 1988, a young star named Dwight Yoakam, who idolized Buck, convinced him to come out of semi-retirement. They recorded "Streets of Bakersfield" together, and it went to number one. A whole new generation discovered Buck Owens the musician. He was inducted into the Country Music Hall of Fame in 1996, and he kept performing at his Crystal Palace in Bakersfield until his death in 2006.

Roy Clark, on the other hand, embraced Hee Haw. He stayed with the show to the very end and often spoke warmly about it. Two hosts, two very different feelings about the same cornfield.

Before our number one secret, a quick word about how it all ended. In 1981, the show was bought by Gaylord Broadcasting, the company connected to the Grand Ole Opry and Opryland. In the early 1990s, the producers tried to modernize the show, dropping some of the cornfield and moving it toward a more urban setting, and it was retitled "Hee Haw Silver" for a time. Longtime fans didn't like the changes. The show stopped producing new episodes in the early 1990s, though reruns continued to air for years afterward. A show that had been canceled by a network for being too country ended up being hurt most when it tried to be less country.

And now, secret number one. The tragedy of Stringbean, the Hee Haw star who was murdered.

This is the hardest story we'll tell today, and many younger fans have never heard it.

David Akeman was known to the world as Stringbean. He was a tall, lanky banjo player with a long shirt and short pants that made him look even taller. He was a member of the Grand Ole Opry, a disciple of the old-time banjo tradition who had played with the great Bill Monroe early in his career. On Hee Haw, he was a beloved regular. He would recite letters from home in the cornfield and deliver gentle, old-fashioned humor that reminded people of the country stores and front porches they grew up with.

Stringbean was famously cautious about money. He had lived through the Great Depression, and it was widely reported that he didn't trust banks. Word got around that he kept large amounts of cash with him or at his home.

On the night of November tenth, 1973, Stringbean played the Grand Ole Opry at the Ryman Auditorium. Afterward, he and his wife, Estelle, drove home to their small farmhouse in Ridgetop, Tennessee, north of Nashville. Waiting for them were two men who had broken in, hoping to find that hidden cash. When the Akemans arrived, they were both shot and killed.

Their bodies were discovered the next morning by their friend and neighbor, Grandpa Jones, who had come by to pick up Stringbean for a hunting trip. It was a devastating moment for Grandpa Jones and for the entire country music family.

Two cousins, John and Doug Brown, were arrested, tried, and convicted of the murders, and both received life sentences. And here's the final, heartbreaking twist. According to reports, decades later, in 1996, a large amount of cash was discovered hidden inside a wall of the old farmhouse, so old and damaged that it reportedly had rotted away. The money the killers had come for may have been there the whole time.

Hee Haw went on, but the cast never forgot their friend. Stringbean's death was a reminder that behind the laughter and the cornfield jokes were real people with real lives, and real losses.

So there you have it. A show created by two Canadians. A summer replacement that outlived its network. A season shot in a few weeks. A sawmill worker who became a star. A cast of true legends. A host who wished people remembered his music. And a tragedy that broke Nashville's heart. Hee Haw was corny, yes. But it was also something rare. It was a show that looked rural America in the eye and said, you matter.
