For one hundred years, it has been called the show that made country music famous. Every Saturday night, families from Maine to Texas turned the dial to a little radio station in Nashville and heard the sound of home. But the Grand Ole Opry has secrets. It once banned the drums. It fired Hank Williams. It reportedly told a young Elvis Presley he should go back to driving a truck. Its very first star was a Black harmonica player whose story was nearly erased. And the radio station behind it all had a name that was really an insurance slogan. Tonight, we're stepping into the circle. These are eight Grand Ole Opry secrets WSM didn't want you to know. And stay with me until the end, because the number one secret is the one that almost washed it all away.

Close your eyes for a moment and picture it. A warm Saturday night. The windows open. The old radio glowing on the kitchen counter. And then that voice comes through the static, followed by a fiddle, a banjo, and a crowd clapping along. For generations of Americans, that was the Grand Ole Opry. It wasn't just a radio show. It was a weekly visit with family.

Secret number eight. WSM, the station that created the Opry, got its name from an insurance company slogan.

The Grand Ole Opry was born on a radio station called WSM in Nashville, Tennessee. Now, those call letters may look random, but they weren't. WSM was owned by the National Life and Accident Insurance Company, one of the biggest businesses in Nashville. The company's motto was "We Shield Millions." W, S, M.

That's right. The most famous country music radio show in the world began, in part, as a way to sell insurance. National Life put the station on the air in October of 1925, and the idea was simple. If people across the South were listening to WSM, they'd hear the company's name, trust it, and maybe buy a policy from one of its salesmen when he knocked on their door. And it worked. Insurance agents reportedly found that saying they represented the company behind the Opry opened doors all over the countryside.

Just weeks after the station went on the air, on November twenty-eighth, 1925, WSM broadcast a program featuring an elderly fiddler named Uncle Jimmy Thompson. He was in his late seventies, and he reportedly claimed he could play a thousand fiddle tunes. The man who put him on the air was George D. Hay, a young announcer who called himself "The Solemn Old Judge." Hay blew a steamboat whistle to open the show. The program was called the WSM Barn Dance, and listeners went wild for it. Letters and telegrams poured in asking for more. And from that night on, Saturday nights in Nashville would never be the same.

Secret number seven. The name Grand Ole Opry started as a joke.

For its first couple of years, the show was simply called the WSM Barn Dance. The name we all know came from an off-the-cuff remark, and it was a little bit of a jab.

In those days, WSM carried programs from the NBC radio network. One of them, which aired right before the Barn Dance, was a classical music program hosted by the conductor Walter Damrosch, devoted to grand opera and symphonies. As the story goes, one night in 1927, Damrosch reportedly made a comment that there was no place in classical music for realism, and then the network signed off and Nashville took over.

George D. Hay stepped to the microphone and, according to the legend, said something like this. For the past hour, we have been listening to music taken largely from grand opera. But from now on, we will present the Grand Ole Opry. And then he brought on DeFord Bailey to play a harmonica tune that imitated a train.

The name stuck immediately. It was funny, it was proud, and it was a little bit of a poke at the big city folks who looked down on country music. Grand opera for the rich. The Grand Ole Opry for everybody else. Nearly a century later, that playful little joke is still the name of one of the most famous stages on earth. And that brings us to the man who played right after it was spoken.

Secret number six. The Opry's first big star was a Black harmonica player, and his story was nearly forgotten.

His name was DeFord Bailey. He was born in Tennessee in 1899, and as a small child he was stricken with polio. While he was recovering, his family gave him a harmonica. He learned to make it imitate everything he heard around him: trains, fox hunts, barking dogs, the wind. By the time he was a young man, he could make that little instrument do things nobody had ever heard.

George D. Hay called him "the Harmonica Wizard," and in the early years of the Opry, DeFord Bailey was one of its most popular performers. His signature song was "Pan American Blues," named for a famous train, and it was the tune he played the night the Grand Ole Opry got its name. In some of those early years, he reportedly appeared on the show more often than any other performer.

But this was the segregated South of the 1920s and 1930s. Even as a star, DeFord Bailey often could not stay in the same hotels or eat in the same restaurants as his fellow performers when they toured. Accounts say that some of the white musicians he traveled with tried to help him, sometimes claiming he was their valet or servant so that he could get a room. It's a painful thing to think about.

In 1941, DeFord Bailey was let go from the Opry. The official reason had to do with a big dispute that year between radio stations and the music licensing organization ASCAP. Stations couldn't play songs licensed by ASCAP, and Bailey's best known tunes were caught up in that fight. He couldn't play the material audiences wanted most, and the Opry dismissed him. In later years, Bailey said he felt he had been treated unfairly, and many historians agree that his treatment reflected the racial attitudes of the time.

After that, DeFord Bailey mostly left the music business. He ran a shoeshine stand in Nashville for many years. He made a few appearances at the Opry later in life, including old-timers' shows in the 1970s. He passed away in 1982. And in 2005, long after his death, he was inducted into the Country Music Hall of Fame. It was an honor that came far too late, but it finally put his name where it belonged, among the founders of country music.

Secret number five. The Opry moved so many times that it once charged admission just to keep the crowds away.

Today we picture the Opry in one of two places. The famous Ryman Auditorium downtown, or the big Grand Ole Opry House out at Opryland. But before it settled down, the show had a wandering life, and the reason was simple. It was too popular.

In the early days, the Barn Dance was broadcast from a small studio at the National Life building. Fans started showing up to watch, crowding the hallways, so WSM built a bigger studio with room for an audience. That filled up too. The show moved to the Hillsboro Theatre, then to a rough tent-like building in East Nashville called the Dixie Tabernacle, where fans sat on wooden benches and sawdust floors. Then in 1939, it moved to the War Memorial Auditorium downtown.

And here's the secret. At the War Memorial, the crowds were so big that the Opry started charging admission, reportedly a quarter, partly just to keep the number of people under control. Imagine that. A show so popular that charging money was a way to keep folks out.

In 1943, the Opry moved into the Ryman Auditorium. The Ryman had been built in 1892 as the Union Gospel Tabernacle by a riverboat captain named Thomas Ryman, who had a change of heart after hearing the famous evangelist Sam Jones preach at a tent revival. He reportedly came to the revival intending to heckle, and he left a changed man. He built the tabernacle so that Jones and others would have a proper place to preach. Its wooden pews and stained glass windows gave it the feeling of a church, and that's exactly why people came to call it "the Mother Church of Country Music."

But the Ryman had no air conditioning. In summer, it was sweltering. Performers and audiences fanned themselves with paper fans, and stars sometimes waited backstage in tiny dressing rooms, or across the alley at Tootsie's Orchid Lounge, which became a famous hangout for Opry musicians. Those Ryman years were the golden age for many fans. It was there that Minnie Pearl, who had joined the Opry in 1940, shouted her famous "How-DEE!" to packed houses. It was there that Roy Acuff sang "Wabash Cannonball" and balanced his fiddle bow on his nose. It was there that Bill Monroe and his Blue Grass Boys, with Lester Flatt and Earl Scruggs in the lineup in the mid-1940s, helped shape the sound we now call bluegrass. And it was there that a young Ernest Tubb helped make the electric guitar acceptable on country radio, even as the Opry's old guard grumbled about it. The Opry stayed at the Ryman for more than thirty years, until 1974, when it moved to the new Grand Ole Opry House. And they did something beautiful before they left. They cut a six-foot circle of oak from the Ryman stage and set it into the new stage, so that every performer since has stood on the same wood where the legends once stood.

Opening night at the new Opry House, on March sixteenth, 1974, had a very unusual guest. The President of the United States, Richard Nixon, came to Nashville. He played a few songs on the piano, including "God Bless America," and Roy Acuff even tried to teach him how to work a yo-yo, which was Acuff's favorite trick. It remains one of the strangest and most memorable nights in Opry history.

Secret number four. The Opry fought for years against drums on its stage.

This one sounds hard to believe today, but for decades, drums were treated as practically forbidden on the Grand Ole Opry.

The Opry's leaders, especially in the early years, saw the show as a home for traditional string band music. Fiddles, banjos, guitars, mandolins, the upright bass. Drums were associated with jazz, swing, and dance halls, and George D. Hay and others reportedly wanted to keep the Opry sound "down to earth." Electric instruments and drums were viewed with suspicion.

The most famous clash came in the 1940s when Bob Wills and his Texas Playboys came to the Opry. Wills played Western swing, and his band used drums. According to the well known story, the Opry told him the drums had to be hidden behind a curtain. Wills reportedly refused, and the drums came out onstage anyway. It was a moment of open rebellion on the Opry stage.

Years later, Opry performers still had to work around the restrictions. Some drummers were allowed to play with only a snare drum and brushes. Some bands had to keep the drummer offstage or out of sight. It wasn't until the 1970s that a full drum kit became a normal part of the Opry. Today, it's hard to imagine a country show without drums. But for much of the Opry's history, that steady beat was something you weren't supposed to see.

Secret number three. The Opry fired Hank Williams, and reportedly told Elvis Presley he'd never make it.

The Grand Ole Opry made stars. But it also made some decisions it might like to take back.

On June eleventh, 1949, a skinny young singer from Alabama named Hank Williams made his Opry debut. He sang "Lovesick Blues," and the crowd went out of its mind. By most accounts, he was called back for encore after encore, six of them by the most common telling. It was one of the most famous debuts in the history of the show. Hank Williams became the biggest star in country music almost overnight.

But Hank had troubles. He suffered from serious back pain, he drank heavily, and he began missing shows. In August of 1952, the Opry fired him. Just a few months later, on New Year's Day of 1953, Hank Williams died in the back seat of a car on the way to a show in Ohio. He was only twenty-nine years old. Over the years, fans have repeatedly petitioned the Opry to reinstate Hank Williams as a member, a symbolic gesture of forgiveness and respect for the man whose songs helped define the music.

Then there was Elvis. On October second, 1954, a nineteen-year-old Elvis Presley appeared on the Opry, just once. He performed "Blue Moon of Kentucky," Bill Monroe's bluegrass song, but in a fast, rocking style. The audience's response was reportedly polite but cool. And according to a story that has been repeated for decades, the Opry's talent manager at the time, Jim Denny, reportedly told Elvis that he ought to go back to driving a truck. Some historians question whether those exact words were ever spoken, but Elvis himself reportedly felt hurt by the experience, and he never played the Opry again.

Johnny Cash had his own famous moment. In 1965, during a difficult period in his life, Cash reportedly became frustrated onstage and dragged his microphone stand across the footlights, smashing dozens of them and sending glass flying over the audience. Cash later said he was asked not to come back for a while. Of course, he eventually returned, and he was welcomed home.

Secret number two. In 1964, the Opry dropped a dozen of its own stars for not showing up enough.

Being a member of the Grand Ole Opry has always been a great honor. But it has also come with obligations. And in the early 1960s, the Opry decided to enforce them.

The rule was that members had to appear on the Opry a certain number of Saturday nights each year. In the 1960s, that number reportedly rose to twenty-six. That's half the Saturdays in a year. For stars who were making far more money touring around the country on weekends, that requirement was a serious burden. Playing the Opry paid only a small amount compared to a concert in a big city.

So, in late 1963 and early 1964, the Opry announced that a dozen of its members were being dropped for failing to meet their appearance requirements. The list reportedly included some of the biggest names of the day, with stars such as Ray Price, Faron Young, and Kitty Wells among those named in press accounts at the time. It was front page news in Nashville. Some of those artists later returned to the Opry, and some did not.

The episode showed a side of the Opry that fans rarely saw. Behind the friendly radio voice and the warm welcome, it was a business. The Opry needed stars on its stage every Saturday night, and the stars needed to make a living on the road. That tension has never fully gone away. Over the years, the attendance requirements were relaxed, and today the Opry works with its members in a more flexible way. But in 1964, it drew a hard line, and Nashville never forgot it.

But the Opry also opened its doors to new generations, and some of those invitations changed music forever. In January of 1960, a young singer from Winchester, Virginia, named Patsy Cline became a member. She had been told early on that her voice was too big and too bluesy for country, and she proved everyone wrong with songs like "Walkin' After Midnight," "I Fall to Pieces," and "Crazy." Patsy reportedly took younger women under her wing at the Opry, including a newcomer from Kentucky named Loretta Lynn, who joined in 1962. Patsy's life was cut short in a plane crash in March of 1963, along with fellow Opry stars Cowboy Copas and Hawkshaw Hawkins, and her manager, pilot Randy Hughes. Just days later, Opry star Jack Anglin was killed in a car accident on his way to a memorial service. It was one of the darkest weeks the Opry family ever endured.

A few years later, in 1969, a bright young singer from the Smoky Mountains named Dolly Parton became a member. Decades on, Dolly is still one of the Opry's most beloved figures. Through all those years, the faces changed, but the circle stayed the same. Every newcomer stood where the legends had stood, and every one of them reportedly felt the weight of that moment the first time they heard the announcer say their name.

Before we get to our number one secret, here's one more remarkable fact. The Grand Ole Opry is widely recognized as the longest-running radio broadcast in American history. It has gone out over the air every week since 1925. Through the Great Depression, through World War Two, through every president from Calvin Coolidge onward, the Opry has kept broadcasting. During the war, performers like Roy Acuff became so famous that, according to a widely repeated story, Japanese soldiers in the Pacific reportedly shouted insults about Roy Acuff at American Marines, because they knew how much he meant to American troops. That's how far that little Nashville radio show had traveled.

And now, secret number one. The day the Grand Ole Opry went underwater.

In early May of 2010, a storm system parked itself over Middle Tennessee. Over two days, the rain poured down in amounts that had rarely been seen in Nashville's history. The Cumberland River rose. Creeks overflowed. Neighborhoods flooded. And out at the Opryland complex, the water kept rising until it poured into the Grand Ole Opry House itself.

The pictures shocked people across the country. The Opry stage, the heart of country music, sat under several feet of brown floodwater. Seats were submerged. Instruments, costumes, and priceless memorabilia stored backstage were damaged. The Gaylord Opryland Resort next door was flooded as well, and thousands of guests had to be evacuated.

Many feared the worst. What about the circle? That six-foot piece of oak from the Ryman stage, the wood where Hank and Patsy and Roy Acuff and Minnie Pearl had stood. It had been under the water too.

But here's the part of the story that sounds almost like a miracle. The Opry didn't stop. It never missed a show. That very weekend, and in the months that followed, the Grand Ole Opry kept broadcasting from other locations around Nashville, including its old home at the Ryman Auditorium, and other venues around town. The longest-running radio show in America kept its streak alive, even with its home under water.

And the circle? It was carefully removed, restored, and put back into place. When the Opry House reopened in September of 2010, the circle was right there in the middle of the stage where it belonged. Performers who stepped into it that night said they could feel the history under their boots.

So there you have it. An insurance slogan for a name. A joke that became a legend. A forgotten harmonica wizard. A show that charged admission just to control the crowds. A war against drums. The firing of Hank Williams and a cold welcome for Elvis. A dozen stars dropped in a single sweep. And a flood that could not stop the music.

For one hundred years, the Grand Ole Opry has been more than a radio show. It's been a promise. That every Saturday night, no matter what's happening in the world, there will be a fiddle, a guitar, a voice, and a crowd. And somewhere out there, a family will be listening.
