Thousands of Canadian acoustic players are walking away from Taylor in 2026. Not loudly. Nobody is smashing guitars on TikTok. They're just walking into a shop in Calgary or Halifax or Kitchener with a Taylor in a case, trading it in, and walking out with something built a few hours east of Montreal. And I know this because I'm the guy they bring both guitars to.

I run a repair bench here in Canada. I do setups, fret work, saddle shaves, crack repairs, the whole thing. And over the last couple of years the mix of guitars landing on my bench has changed in a way I didn't expect. Fewer shiny American dreadnoughts and grand auditoriums. More guitars with little labels inside that say things like La Patrie, Quebec, or Princeville, Quebec. Simon and Patrick. Norman. Art and Lutherie. Brands a lot of players walked right past in the store ten years ago.

So today I want to be honest about why that's happening. Not a commercial. Nobody's paying me for this. I'm going to tell you what I see on the bench, what the numbers look like in Canadian dollars, where Taylor still wins, because it absolutely still wins in places, and who should and shouldn't make this switch. Grab a coffee. Let's get into it.

Let me start with the thing that every Canadian player feels first, before they ever touch a guitar. The price tag.

When you buy an American-made acoustic in Canada, you aren't really paying the American price. You're paying the American price, converted through an exchange rate that has not been kind to us, plus freight across the border, plus distribution margins, plus GST or HST depending on your province. The Canadian dollar has spent most of the last few years floating somewhere around seventy to seventy-four US cents. That means every hundred US dollars of guitar costs us something like a hundred and thirty-five to a hundred and forty Canadian dollars before anybody adds a single cent of margin.

So a guitar that an American player sees at, say, two thousand US dollars, very easily turns into a twenty-eight hundred, three thousand dollar guitar on a Canadian wall, and then the tax lands on top of that. In Ontario that's thirteen percent HST. In Nova Scotia it's fourteen. Suddenly you're well past three grand for a mid-range American acoustic, and that's for a guitar that, let's be honest, a lot of people are going to play in their living room and at the occasional open mic.

And then 2025 happened. The trade fight between Canada and the United States put tariffs on the front page of every newspaper in this country. Canada published counter-tariff lists on American goods. Retailers started warning customers that prices on US-made products could move. Some of that was real, some of it was confusion, and honestly, a lot of guitar players didn't know exactly what applied to instruments and what didn't. But here's the thing I noticed on the bench. It didn't matter whether a specific guitar got hit by a specific tariff. What mattered was the feeling. Canadians started reading labels. People started asking me, in the middle of a setup, "Is this thing made here?" I'd never heard that question so much in my life.

And that's the moment a lot of players discovered something that we, as Canadians, kind of forgot to brag about. We have one of the most serious acoustic guitar manufacturing operations in North America, and it's sitting in rural Quebec.

Let me give you the quick history, because it matters for understanding why these guitars are good. Robert Godin started building guitars in Quebec in the early nineteen seventies. Out of that work came a family of brands. Norman, which actually dates back to 1968 and was started by Norman Boucher, became part of the group. Seagull arrived in 1982. Simon and Patrick, named after Robert Godin's sons. Art and Lutherie, built around the idea of making honest, affordable guitars out of Canadian wild cherry and other local woods. These guitars are built in towns like La Patrie and Princeville, in the Eastern Townships and central Quebec, by people who in many cases have been doing this for decades.

I'm deliberately not talking about Seagull too much today. I've done that one, and you've probably heard the Seagull story a hundred times. Today I want to talk about the brands sitting right beside it that I think are the real reason people are trading in Taylors. Simon and Patrick, Norman, and Art and Lutherie.

So let's talk about what actually happens when a Taylor and a Simon and Patrick are both sitting on my bench.

First, let me give Taylor its due. Taylor is a brilliant company. Bob Taylor changed acoustic guitar manufacturing. The bolt-on NT neck is one of the smartest ideas in the industry, because it makes neck resets fast and cheap compared to a traditional dovetail. Their finish quality is incredibly consistent. Their necks are comfortable and slim. Their Expression System electronics are clean and easy. And Andy Powers' V-Class bracing gave a lot of their guitars better sustain and more consistent intonation up the neck. When a Taylor comes in, I usually know exactly what I'm going to find, and that's a compliment.

But here's what I also usually find. A lot of Canadian Taylor owners didn't buy the Taylor because of V-Class bracing. They bought it because it was the name on the wall at the front of the store, and because their favourite songwriter played one. And when I ask them what they actually want from their guitar, the answer is almost always the same. "I want it to sound big, I want it to stay in tune, I want it to plug in at a gig, and I don't want to worry about it in February."

And that last one is where Canada changes the whole conversation.

Let's talk about humidity, because nobody south of the border understands what we put guitars through. In a Canadian winter, the outdoor air is freezing, and when you heat that air inside your house, the relative humidity can drop into the twenties or even the teens. A solid wood acoustic built and seasoned somewhere with milder, more humid air is now sitting in a room that's basically a desert. Wood gives up moisture and shrinks. Tops sink. Fret ends poke out the sides of the fingerboard. Actions drop and you get buzzing. And worst case, the top or back cracks.

Every January and February my bench is full of what I call winter guitars. Sharp fret ends, low action, cracks along the grain beside the fingerboard extension or the pickguard. I see it on every brand. I want to be really clear about that. A Quebec guitar is not magic. If you leave any solid wood guitar on a stand next to a forced air vent in Winnipeg in January, it's going to suffer.

But there is something worth noticing. Guitars built in Quebec are built in Quebec. The wood is acclimatized and seasoned in a place that has the same miserable winters as the rest of us. The factories know these guitars are going to live in Canadian houses. And in my experience, which is one tech's bench, not a lab study, the Quebec-built guitars I see tend to handle our winters with a little less drama. Less fret sprout, fewer surprise cracks. That's not a promise. It's a pattern. And it's a pattern that a lot of other Canadian techs I talk to have noticed too.

Now let's get into the specific guitars, because that's what you actually came for.

Simon and Patrick. This is the brand I think is the most underrated acoustic in Canada right now. The guitars usually come with solid tops, often solid spruce or solid cedar, and on the higher models you get solid backs and sides too. Cedar tops are a big part of the Simon and Patrick sound. If you've never played a cedar top, it's warmer, it speaks quicker under a light touch, and it's fantastic for fingerstyle and for people who sing quietly over their guitar. Spruce tops are a bit brighter and handle a heavy strumming hand better.

The thing I notice on the bench with Simon and Patrick is how much guitar you get for the money in Canadian dollars. Depending on the model and where you shop, you can find solid-top Simon and Patricks in that sort of eight hundred to fourteen hundred dollar Canadian zone, with all-solid models climbing from there. Prices move, sales come and go, so check your local shop. But compare that to what you pay in Canada for an all-solid American guitar, and the gap is not small. It's often well over a thousand dollars.

And what's that gap buying you on the Taylor side? Some of it is real. Better finish detail, slick electronics, brand resale value. But some of it is just the exchange rate and the border. You're not buying more guitar. You're buying more distance.

Norman. Norman is the old soul of the family. The brand has been around since 1968, which makes it older than a lot of the people watching this. Norman guitars tend to have a more traditional voice. Think of a slightly woody, classic folk tone, the kind of thing that sits really nicely behind a voice in a coffee house. When I set up a Norman, the necks are usually comfortable, a little fuller than a Taylor, and the build is straightforward, no fuss. They're the guitar I recommend to people who say, "I don't want shiny, I want a guitar that sounds like a record from 1974."

Art and Lutherie. This is the sleeper. Art and Lutherie built its identity around Canadian wild cherry back and sides, and on a lot of models, laminated tops and bodies that are tough as nails. Before anybody in the comments says "laminate is bad," let me stop you. Laminated wood, in a Canadian climate, is a gift. It moves far less with humidity changes. It cracks far less. It's the guitar you can take camping in Algonquin, leave in a cottage over the winter, take to a bush party, take to a church basement gig, and it's going to come back alive. And the Art and Lutherie Roadhouse, the parlour-sized one, has become a cult favourite with blues players and slide players because it has that boxy, punchy, honky little voice that just records beautifully.

Now, here's where I want to get specific about the guitars people are actually trading.

The most common trade I've seen is somebody with a Taylor 100 or 200 series guitar. These are good guitars. But let me tell you something people don't always realize. Many of the lower series Taylors aren't built in El Cajon, California. Taylor builds a lot of its more affordable models at its factory in Tecate, Mexico. That's a very good factory, by the way. I've had great Tecate Taylors on my bench. But if part of why you bought a Taylor was "American made," you might want to check the label inside. And a lot of my customers, when they look, are surprised.

So when that person starts comparing a Mexican-built Taylor with laminated back and sides, at Canadian prices, to a Simon and Patrick with a solid top built in Quebec, at Canadian prices, the comparison gets really interesting really fast. And that's the exact moment I watch people walk away from the Taylor.

The second most common trade is the person who owns a higher-end Taylor, a 300, 400, or 500 series, and who's starting to think about what they actually use the guitar for. If you've got three thousand dollars Canadian tied up in a guitar that lives in its case because you're scared to take it to the campfire, that's a problem. A lot of those players trade it in, grab a really good Simon and Patrick or Norman as their main guitar, and an Art and Lutherie as a beater, and walk out with money left over. That's not a downgrade. That's a smarter guitar life.

Okay. Let's talk electronics, because this matters if you gig.

Taylor's Expression System 2 is genuinely good. It uses a behind-the-saddle pickup design and it's quite natural sounding, especially through a decent PA. The Quebec brands use various systems depending on the model, including Godin's own designs and other well-known preamps. On the bench, what I care about is three things. Is it easy to service? Does the battery live somewhere I can get to it? And does it sound decent through a basic PA at a pub in Sudbury where the sound guy has eleven minutes to get you sorted?

And honestly, for most players at most gigs, the difference between a good Quebec acoustic electric and a Taylor through a small PA is far smaller than the difference in price. The room is usually the problem. The sound guy is usually the problem. The cheap DI is usually the problem. Your pickup is rarely the reason the show sounded bad.

Now let me talk about repairs and long term ownership, because this is where a tech has something to say that a salesperson won't.

Taylor's bolt-on neck is a real advantage. A neck reset on a Taylor is fairly straightforward. That's a genuine plus, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. On the Quebec guitars, the neck construction varies by brand and model, and some of the Godin family guitars also use neck designs that make adjustments easier than a traditional dovetail. Ask the shop when you're buying.

But here's what I really want you to hear. Parts and support for a Quebec guitar are, well, in Quebec. If I need a part, a bridge, a saddle, an electronic component, I'm dealing with a Canadian company in a Canadian time zone, shipping inside Canada. No customs paperwork. No surprise brokerage fees. Anybody who's ever ordered a small part from the United States and gotten a brokerage bill bigger than the part knows exactly what I'm talking about.

And that's a big deal for warranty work too. Warranties exist on both sides, and Taylor's warranty is good. But the practical reality of shipping a guitar across the border for warranty service, or waiting for an authorized repair centre, is a different thing from dealing with a company a day's drive away.

Let's talk about resale, because this is the strongest argument for Taylor, and I want to be fair.

A Taylor holds its value well. The name is recognized everywhere. If you buy a used Taylor and sell it two years later, you often lose very little. The Quebec brands, other than Seagull, don't have quite the same recognition on the used market. A used Simon and Patrick might take a little longer to sell on Kijiji or Facebook Marketplace, and you might have to explain to the buyer what it is.

But flip that around. That's exactly why used Simon and Patricks and Normans are some of the best deals in the Canadian used market right now. People don't know what they are. I see solid-top Quebec guitars going used for prices that would barely buy you a laminate guitar from a big American brand. If you're patient, that's where the real value is. And that's part of why this shift is quiet. People aren't making announcements. They're just buying the good stuff while nobody's looking.

Now let's talk about the part of this story that's not about money at all. Pride.

There is something happening in Canada right now that's bigger than guitars. In 2025, a lot of Canadians started changing what they buy. Grocery stores put little Canadian flags on shelf labels. People cancelled US trips. Folks started asking where things are made. And guitar players are no different. When a player picks up a guitar, and sees a label that says it was built in La Patrie, Quebec, by people whose families have been building guitars for two or three generations, that feels good. That feels like something.

And I'll be honest with you. As a tech, I like that feeling too. When I set up a Quebec guitar, I'm working on something built by people who have the same winters as me, the same hockey arguments, the same weather complaints. There's something to that. It's not the reason you should buy a guitar. Sound and feel come first. Always. But when sound and feel are close, and the price is lower, and the guitar was built down the road instead of across the border, you'd be surprised how quickly people make the decision.

Okay. Let me put my honest tech hat on and tell you who should NOT walk away from their Taylor.

If you love the Taylor sound. That bright, clear, articulate, modern sound that cuts through a mix. If that's your sound, keep your Taylor. That's a real thing, and Quebec guitars, especially the cedar tops, often sound warmer and rounder. Different isn't better. It's different.

If you play a lot of high, complicated chord work up the neck and you love that slim, fast Taylor neck. Keep it. Some of the Quebec necks are chunkier, and some players with smaller hands find that less comfortable.

If you bought your Taylor used at a great price, it's set up well, and you love it. Please don't sell it because some guy on YouTube told you to. Keep the guitar you love. Play it more. That's the whole point.

And if you're a working pro who needs a very specific, consistent stage sound out of a specific Taylor model with a specific pickup. Keep it. Your tools are your tools.

Now, who SHOULD think about switching?

If you're shopping for your first serious acoustic in Canada, and you have between eight hundred and two thousand dollars to spend, you owe it to yourself to play the Quebec guitars before you buy anything else. Put a Simon and Patrick next to whatever you were going to buy and play them both with your eyes closed. Not kidding. Close your eyes. A lot of people pick the Quebec guitar, and a lot of them are surprised.

If you've got a mid-range American guitar you're afraid to take anywhere. Trade it. Get a guitar you'll actually play outdoors, at the cottage, at the campfire, at the open mic. A guitar that lives in a case is a very expensive piece of furniture.

If you're a fingerstyle player or a quiet singer songwriter. Try a cedar top Simon and Patrick or Norman. It might be the sound you've been looking for and never knew existed.

And if you want a second guitar for slide or blues. Play an Art and Lutherie Roadhouse. Just do it. Thank me later.

Let me give you a few bench tips before we wrap up, because whatever you buy, I want it to survive.

Number one. Humidify your guitar in the winter. Every Canadian guitar player needs a humidifier. A soundhole humidifier, a case humidifier, or a room humidifier keeping your guitar room around forty to fifty percent relative humidity. Buy a cheap digital hygrometer. They're inexpensive and they'll save you hundreds of dollars in repairs.

Number two. Keep the guitar in its case in the winter if your room is dry. Stands look nice. Cracks don't.

Number three. Get a seasonal setup. A lot of Canadian guitars need a small truss rod adjustment between summer and winter. That's normal. It's not a defect. Your guitar is a piece of wood, and wood moves.

Number four. When you shop, buy from a Canadian shop that will do a setup. A good setup makes a twelve hundred dollar guitar play like a three thousand dollar guitar. A bad setup makes a three thousand dollar guitar play like a twelve hundred dollar guitar.

And number five. Don't be afraid of laminate. Especially if your guitar is going to travel, or live somewhere without climate control. A good laminate guitar played often beats a solid wood guitar sitting in a closet.
