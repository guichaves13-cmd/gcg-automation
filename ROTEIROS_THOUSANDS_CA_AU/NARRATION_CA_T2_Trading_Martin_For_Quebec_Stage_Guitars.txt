There's a guitar case that keeps showing up at my bench lately, and every time I open it, I already know the story. Inside is a Martin. Usually a Road Series or an X Series, sometimes a 15 series mahogany. The owner plays out. Bars, legion halls, wedding gigs, brewery patios in the summer. And they're not here for a setup. They're here because they want me to look at the guitar before they trade it in. And nine times out of ten, the guitar they're trading it in for was built in Quebec, and it doesn't even look like a traditional acoustic.

Thousands of gigging Canadian players are trading in their Martins in 2026. Not because Martin makes bad guitars. Martin makes some of the most important guitars in history. They're doing it because of something nobody talks about in the guitar store. Feedback, stage volume, cold vans, Canadian dollars, and a family of guitars from Quebec that were designed from day one to be played loud on a stage.

I'm a guitar tech. I've been fixing guitars for working musicians in this country for a long time. Nobody's sponsoring this. I'm going to tell you exactly what I see, where Martin still wins, and who should never, ever sell their Martin. Let's go.

First, let me be clear about Martin, because it deserves respect. C.F. Martin and Company has been building guitars since 1833. They basically invented the modern dreadnought. The D-28 is one of the most recorded acoustic guitars ever made. When you hear a strummed acoustic on a classic folk or country record, there's a very good chance it's a Martin. That's not marketing. That's history.

But here's the thing. Most of the Martins I see on gigging players in Canada aren't D-28s. They're the more affordable models. And many of those, the X Series and the Road Series, are built at Martin's factory in Navojoa, Mexico. The X Series uses high pressure laminate, which Martin calls HPL, on a lot of models. That's a durable material and a perfectly fine guitar for a lot of people. But it means many players are paying a premium Canadian price for the Martin name on a guitar that isn't the Martin they imagined when they bought it.

Now, let's do the money, in Canadian dollars, the way it actually hits us.

When the Canadian dollar sits around seventy to seventy-four US cents, which is where it's been hovering for a good chunk of the last few years, every American price gets marked up by roughly thirty-five to forty percent before any Canadian margin, freight, or tax. Then you add HST or GST and PST depending on your province. Then, in 2025, the trade fight between Ottawa and Washington made every Canadian retailer nervous about American-made goods. Counter-tariff lists, supplier price notices, a lot of uncertainty. Whether or not any particular guitar was hit, the result was the same at the shop counter. Prices on American brands felt unpredictable, and players started asking what else was out there.

So now picture a working bar musician in Thunder Bay. They play three nights a week. They need a guitar that plugs in, doesn't feed back, survives a minus thirty load-out into a cold van, and doesn't cost them a month of gig money. That person isn't shopping for history. That person is shopping for a tool. And that's where the Godin family of guitars comes in.

Let me give you the background. Robert Godin started building guitars in Quebec in the early seventies, and the Godin company grew into one of the largest guitar builders in North America, with workshops in Quebec towns like La Patrie and Princeville, plus a facility in New Hampshire. You probably know some of their acoustic brands. I'm not going to talk about Seagull today, I've done that one. Today I want to talk about the Godin-branded stage guitars that working players are switching to. The Godin A6 Ultra. The Multiac. And the 5th Avenue archtops. Plus Norman and Art and Lutherie acoustics for the folks who still want a traditional box.

Let's talk about the problem first, because this is where the bench gives me a front row seat.

The traditional acoustic guitar is a big hollow box designed to project sound into a room with no amplification. That's beautiful in a living room. But on a loud stage, with a drummer, a bass amp, and stage monitors pointed at you, that big hollow box becomes a feedback machine. The top vibrates in sympathy with the low frequencies coming out of the monitors and the PA. You get that howl, that boomy ringing, that woof at certain notes. And the sound person's solution is usually to cut your volume, which means nobody in the room hears your guitar.

I see the results on my bench all the time. Soundhole covers, foam stuffed in the body, notch filters, players asking me to install a different pickup, or a second pickup, or a magnetic soundhole pickup. Hundreds of dollars spent trying to turn a living room guitar into a stage guitar.

Now, Godin's answer to that problem is clever, and it's been around for a long time. Take the A6 Ultra. It's a thin, chambered body made largely from silver leaf maple, and it's built more like a solid body electric than a traditional acoustic. It has an acoustic pickup system and, on the Ultra, a magnetic pickup too, so you can blend acoustic and electric sounds. Because the body is thin and chambered instead of a big open box, it's dramatically more resistant to feedback. You can turn it up. You can stand in front of a monitor. You can play with a loud drummer. And it still sounds like an acoustic through the PA.

The Multiac is the more upscale version of that idea. Chambered body, thin profile, a really sophisticated preamp, and models available in steel string and nylon string. Nylon string Multiacs are hugely popular with players who do Latin music, bossa nova, flamenco-influenced stuff, and wedding gigs. They're also very popular with players who want nylon for some songs and don't want to drag a classical guitar onto a loud stage.

And the 5th Avenue. A hollow body archtop built in Quebec, with a vibe that's straight out of the jazz and blues clubs of the forties and fifties. It's not a stage acoustic in the same way, but I see a lot of players in roots and Americana bands using a 5th Avenue Kingpin with a P-90 as their main instrument, and leaving the dreadnought at home.

So what does the trade actually look like? Let me give you the most common scenario I've seen.

A player brings in a Martin Road Series dreadnought with a pickup, bought new a few years ago. It's a good guitar. It sounds great unplugged. But at gigs, it feeds back, it gets boomy, and it's always the loudest problem on stage. They trade it in and buy a Godin A6 Ultra. Depending on where you buy and whether there's a sale on, at the time I'm recording, you can usually find an A6 Ultra somewhere in the range of a thousand to fourteen hundred dollars Canadian. Prices change, check your local store. And suddenly, the player has a guitar that sits in the mix, doesn't fight the monitors, and covers both acoustic and electric tones. Often with some money left over.

And here's what I hear from those players a month later. "I can actually hear myself." That's it. That's the review. And it's the most important review there is.

Now let me talk about the cold, because if you gig in Canada, cold is part of your job.

Every winter, I see guitars that were loaded into a van or a car trunk at minus twenty, driven across town, and then opened up in a hot, crowded bar. That kind of rapid temperature change is brutal on any guitar. The finish can check, which means it develops those little cracks in the lacquer. Solid wood tops can crack. Glue joints can stress. And thick, glossy nitrocellulose finishes are especially prone to finish checking under rapid temperature shock.

My first piece of advice, whatever guitar you play: leave it in the case for a while when you get inside. Let the case warm up slowly before you open it. Twenty minutes, half an hour if you can. That one habit will save you more money than any guitar purchase.

But here's what I've noticed. The Godin stage guitars, with their chambered maple bodies and their sturdier construction, tend to handle Canadian gig life without much drama. They're built in a place that has the same winters we have. They're designed for the road. And the laminated Art and Lutherie acoustics, for the players who want a traditional box, are probably the toughest acoustics I see come across my bench. Laminate doesn't care much about humidity. It just keeps going.

Now let's talk about Norman and Art and Lutherie for a minute, because not every gigging player wants a thin body guitar.

If you play solo at quieter gigs, coffee shops, farmers markets, house concerts, and you want a traditional acoustic tone, a Norman or an Art and Lutherie with a pickup is a great alternative to an entry level Martin. Norman goes all the way back to 1968, founded by Norman Boucher, and it has that classic, woody, folk voice. Art and Lutherie built its name on Canadian wild cherry, and its laminated models are tough enough to survive a summer of outdoor festivals and a winter of Legion hall gigs. In Canadian dollars, a lot of these guitars sit at prices well below what you'd pay for a comparable Martin here. Again, prices change, so check.

Now, I don't want you to just take my word for any of this. So let me walk you through the test I actually do on the bench when a customer can't decide between keeping their Martin and switching to a Godin stage guitar. You can do this yourself at a rehearsal or in a shop with a small PA.

Step one. Plug both guitars into the same DI, the same channel, the same speaker. No pedals. Flat EQ. Set them to roughly the same volume. This matters, because the louder guitar always sounds better to our ears for the first few seconds, and that fools everybody.

Step two. Stand about where you'd stand on stage, with the speaker pointed back at you like a wedge monitor. Strum an open G chord and slowly bring the volume up. Listen for the moment the low end starts to bloom and ring on its own. That's the edge of feedback. On a lot of traditional dreadnoughts with an under-saddle pickup, that moment comes surprisingly early. On a chambered guitar like the A6 Ultra or the Multiac, it usually comes much, much later. That gap between the two is your stage headroom. And headroom is the thing a working player is really buying.

Step three. Play your actual set. Not scales. Not the riff you always play in shops. Play the songs you play at a gig, at the volume you play them. The quiet ballad, the loud singalong, the one where you hit the guitar like it owes you money. Some players discover the Martin is fine for everything. Some discover the stage guitar just disappears into the mix in a good way. Some find they hate the feel of a thin body against their ribs. All of those are valid results.

Step four. Unplug them both and play them acoustically. This is where the Martin is going to win, and you should hear that too. The question is how much of your playing life actually happens unplugged.

I've watched dozens of players go through that test. And I'd say the honest split is something like this. Players who mostly play loud band gigs almost always leave with the stage guitar. Solo players at quiet rooms are split down the middle. And players who play at home and record usually keep the Martin and decide they never had a feedback problem in the first place. That's a good outcome too.

Okay. Let me put on my honest tech hat and tell you where Martin wins. Because it does.

Unplugged tone. A good solid wood Martin dreadnought, especially the higher end ones built in Nazareth, Pennsylvania, has a voice that a chambered stage guitar is never going to have. The bass, the power, the way it fills a room unplugged. If you play unplugged, record in a studio, or play at campfires and porches, a great Martin is still one of the best guitars in the world. Resale. Martin guitars hold their value really well, especially the US-built models. If you buy a good Martin used, you probably won't lose much money on it. A Godin A6 Ultra won't hold value in quite the same way. That's a real trade off.

Brand recognition. If you're a pro playing big stages, sometimes the look of the guitar matters. A dreadnought on stage reads as acoustic guitar to an audience instantly. A thin body Godin might make some people look twice. That's not a big deal for most of us, but it's real.

And repair tradition. Martins are built in a very traditional way, and every good repair tech in the country knows how to work on them. That's not to say Godin is hard to service, because it isn't, but if you're touring and need a quick repair in a random town, Martin familiarity is a small plus.

Now, who should NOT trade in their Martin?

If you have a US-built Martin, a D-18, D-28, 000-28, something from Nazareth, and you love how it sounds. Do not sell it. Get a cheap stage guitar for loud gigs and keep the Martin for the studio and the porch. That's the smartest move I can suggest.

If you mainly play unplugged. Keep the Martin. A chambered guitar is not the right tool for you.

If the guitar has sentimental value. Your grandfather's guitar, the one you wrote your first song on, the one you bought with your first paycheque. Don't sell it. There are things more important than gig efficiency.

And if your Martin already works on stage for you. If it doesn't feed back, the sound person loves it, and you're happy. Great. Don't fix what isn't broken.

Who SHOULD consider the trade?

If you play loud bar gigs with a full band and your acoustic is always fighting feedback. Try a Godin A6 Ultra or a Multiac. Seriously. Bring it to a rehearsal and play it at band volume. You'll know in ten minutes.

If you're a wedding or corporate player who needs both steel string and nylon tones. A nylon string Multiac or a Godin nylon stage guitar can save your back and your setlist.

If you're spending more money on feedback fixes than on strings. Stop. You don't need another gadget. You might need a different guitar.

And if you just want a guitar built in Canada, by Canadians, that's designed for the life you actually live as a gigging musician here. Then you already know the answer.

Before you go, let me give you a few tech tips for gigging guitars in Canada, whatever you play.

One. Invest in a good hard case. Not a gig bag, a real case. Temperature shock and van bumps are the two biggest killers of gigging guitars.

Two. Change your strings regularly. Bar air, sweat, and humidity changes kill strings fast. Dead strings make any pickup sound bad.

Three. Keep a spare nine volt battery or a charging cable in your case. I can't tell you how many gigs I've seen rescued by a spare battery, and how many have been ruined by forgetting one.

Four. Get a seasonal setup. Your guitar's neck relief and action will change between summer and winter. A quick truss rod tweak is normal and inexpensive.

Five. Use a good DI or acoustic preamp. Many feedback problems at gigs come from a poor signal chain, not just the guitar. A quality DI with a phase switch and a notch filter can make a huge difference.

And six. Talk to your sound person. Be kind to them. Tell them what you need. They'll work harder for you. Trust me on this one.
