First, the price. With the Australian dollar sitting somewhere in the sixties in US cents for a good while now, every American guitar costs us a lot more than it costs an American. Second, the finish. Gibson uses nitrocellulose lacquer on a lot of its acoustics. Nitro is beautiful. It's thin, it ages nicely, and a lot of people believe it lets the top breathe. But nitro is also more sensitive to heat than modern poly finishes. And in Australia, heat is not optional.

Here's a story I hear every summer. A player finishes a gig, puts the guitar in the case, and puts the case in the back of the car. Next day, they've got errands. And third, the pickup. A lot of J-45s come with an under-saddle pickup system. It's fine. Now, I don't want you to trust me blindly. Here's the exact test I run on the bench when a pub player can't decide between keeping their J-45 and moving to a Maton. You can do this in a shop or at a rehearsal.

Step one. Same DI box, same channel, same PA speaker. EQ flat, no reverb, no effects. Reverb makes everything sound expensive. Match the volume as closely as you can, because the louder guitar always wins the first ten seconds.

Step two. Play your actual set. Your loud singalongs, your quiet ballad, the Cold Chisel song everybody requests at eleven o'clock. Not the fancy run you only ever play in guitar shops.

Step three. Point the speaker back at yourself like a foldback wedge and slowly push the volume. Listen for the boom, the ring, the howl. That point is your stage headroom. On a lot of dreadnoughts with a basic under-saddle pickup, it arrives quickly. Step four. Unplug both and play them acoustically in the room. This is where a J-45 usually wins, and you should hear that too. Then ask yourself honestly how many hours a week you play unplugged versus plugged in.

Now, a lot of you are going to look at the used market, so let me tell you what I check when someone brings me a second-hand Maton before they buy it. Because a used 808 can be one of the best deals in Australian guitar, or a headache.

First, look down the neck and check the relief. Matons are generally stable, but a guitar that's lived in a hot car or a damp shed can still move. Make sure the truss rod still has adjustment left. Ask the seller if you can do a quarter turn, or bring it to a tech.

Second, check the bridge. Slide a thin piece of paper under the back edge of the bridge. If it slides under, the bridge is starting to lift. That's usually a heat story, and it's a repair you want to price in before you hand over the cash.

Third, check the top behind the bridge for bellying. A little bit is normal on any acoustic. A big hump means the guitar's been stressed, often by heat or high humidity.

Fourth, plug it in. Always plug it in. Test both the microphone and the under-saddle side of the AP5 system, run through the controls, and wiggle the output jack. Scratchy pots and a loose jack are cheap fixes, but a dead preamp costs more.

Fifth, check the saddle height. If the previous owner has had the action lowered a lot, there might not be much saddle left to work with, which can limit future setups.

And sixth, look at the frets. Working players play hard, and a used gigging guitar might need a fret level or a dress. That's not a dealbreaker. It's just part of the price.

If all of that checks out, a used Maton from a careful owner is a fantastic buy. They tend to hold their value well here, so you're not likely to lose much if you ever sell it on.

And a quick word on the J-45 used market while I'm at it. If you're keeping your Gibson, or buying one used, check the same things, especially the bridge and the finish. Nitro that's been heat-stressed often shows fine checking lines or a soft, sticky feel in spots. It doesn't always mean structural trouble, but it tells you how the guitar has lived.

Before you go, a few tech tips for gigging players in Australia. Whatever you play.

Number one. Do not leave your guitar in a hot car. I know I say this in every video. I'll keep saying it until I stop seeing lifted bridges on my bench.

Number two. Watch the humidity. In the tropics, keep your guitar in its case during the wet season and think about humidity control packs. In dry areas, use a humidifier. A cheap digital hygrometer in the case is the best twenty dollars you'll ever spend.

Number three. Get your pickup system set up properly. A lot of people never adjust the blend on a dual-source system. Spend ten minutes with your amp or your PA finding the right blend between microphone and under-saddle. It can make a huge difference.

Number four. Keep a spare battery in your case. It's always the battery. Every time.

Number five. Change your strings more often than you think. Sweaty pub gigs and coastal salt air kill strings fast, and dead strings make any pickup sound bad.

And number six. Get a setup at least once or twice a year. Australian guitars go through big seasonal swings, and a quick adjustment keeps everything playing right.
