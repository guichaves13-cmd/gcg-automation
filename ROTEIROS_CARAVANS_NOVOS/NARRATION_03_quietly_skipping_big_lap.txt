There's a map pinned to the wall of a garage in Bendigo. It's a big laminated map of Australia, the kind you buy at a newsagent, and somebody has drawn a red line all the way around the edge of it. Melbourne to Adelaide. Across the Nullarbor to Perth. Up the west coast to Broome. Across the Kimberley to Darwin. Down through Queensland and home. Next to the map, there's a calendar with a date circled: the day they were supposed to leave. That date was months ago. The caravan is still in the driveway. And the red line on the map now has a single word written across it in black marker. "Later."

They haven't given up on travelling. They're still going away. Just not like that. Not the whole lap. Not this year.

And they're far from alone. Right across Australia, thousands of grey nomads are quietly skipping the Big Lap. The trip that's been a rite of passage for retired Australians for generations. The one they talked about at work for thirty years. The one that was supposed to be the reward.

I've spent a long time in the caravan trade, and I talk to people every week about the trips they're planning. And the plans have changed. So in this video, I'm going to give you the seven real reasons people are putting the Big Lap on hold. Some are about money. Some are about the road. Some are about family. And the number one reason is something no government, state or federal, can fix, no matter how much they spend. I'll get to it at the very end, and I think it'll make a lot of you nod your heads.

Let's start the countdown.

Reason number seven. The price of getting around.

The Big Lap is usually said to cover something like fifteen thousand kilometres, and many people end up driving a lot further with the detours. Every one of those kilometres is towing kilometres. And towing burns fuel.

In 2026, fuel prices have been unpredictable, to put it politely. The caravan insurer CIL said in the middle of this year that cost-of-living pressures and rising fuel prices were directly influencing grey nomads' travel plans. Not in theory. In practice. People were planning shorter trips, closer to home.

And the further you go from the capital cities, the more you pay. Anyone who's filled up at a roadhouse in the Pilbara or on the Savannah Way knows the feeling. The price on the board makes you look twice. Then you look at the distance to the next fuel stop, and you fill the tank anyway, because what choice do you have?

So when people sit down at the kitchen table and add up fuel for a full lap, on top of everything else, a lot of them look at the total and say, let's do half this year. Let's just do Queensland. Let's just do South Australia. And the Big Lap moves to "later".

But fuel is only one line on the budget. Reason number six is about where you sleep.

Reason number six. Paying for every night.

Once upon a time, a big part of the grey nomad dream was cheap camping. Free camps by rivers. Showgrounds for a few dollars. Station stays where you paid in a jar by the gate.

Those places still exist. But if you've been on the road recently, you'll know that the popular spots aren't what they used to be. They fill up early. Some have closed. Some councils have tightened rules on free camping, citing complaints from local businesses, rubbish, or overcrowding. And in peak season, the caravan parks in the places everybody wants to be, Exmouth, Broome, Darwin, Airlie Beach, are booked out months in advance, often at prices that make retired travellers wince.

On a weekend away, a couple of expensive nights is fine. On a lap that can take six months, a year, or longer, nightly fees become one of the biggest costs of the whole trip. And if you can't find cheap or free spots, the budget doesn't work.

So many grey nomads are doing what makes sense. They pick a region, they find a park they like, they negotiate a weekly or monthly rate, and they stay put. They get to know the place. And they skip the lap.

Now, money is one thing. But for a lot of people, reason number five matters even more. And it's about who they'd be leaving behind.

Reason number five. The family needs them.

Here's something that's changed quietly over the last few years. The grandparents have become the backbone of a lot of Australian families.

Childcare is expensive. Many young couples both work. So grandparents step in. School pick-ups. Sick days. Holidays. A lot of retired Australians are doing two, three, four days a week of grandparenting. And they love it. But you can't do school pick-up from Kununurra.

Then there's the other end of the family. Many people in their sixties and seventies still have parents in their late eighties and nineties. Some of them are in aged care. Some are living at home and need help. When a phone call can come at any time saying Mum's had a fall, being two thousand kilometres away on a dirt road with no reception isn't an option a lot of people are comfortable with.

So they do shorter trips. Three weeks here. A month there. Always within a day or two's drive of home. Close enough to come back if they're needed.

And when I talk to these people, they don't sound disappointed. They sound like they've made a choice they're at peace with. But it does mean the full lap gets pushed back. And back. And back.

Reason number four is one we've talked about on this channel before, but it keeps coming up, because it's real.

Reason number four. The rig isn't up to it.

The Big Lap isn't a gentle drive. It includes some of the toughest roads in the country. Long corrugated stretches. Dust that gets into everything. Heat that pushes your engine, your tyres and your fridge to the limit.

A lot of caravans on the road today were built for the bitumen. They're comfortable, they're well-appointed, but they were never designed for weeks of corrugations or a beating on the Gibb River Road. People in the repair trade see the results every year. Cracked chassis. Broken springs. Cupboards that have shaken loose. Water tanks punctured by rocks. Bills in the thousands, sometimes in a town where the nearest qualified repairer is hundreds of kilometres away.

And it's not just the van. Many tow vehicles are working close to their legal and practical limits. Overweight rigs are a common sight at mobile weighing days. Put an overloaded rig through months of remote roads and you're asking for trouble.

So people do the honest assessment. Is my van up to it? Is my car? Do I want to spend money upgrading both before I go? And for many, the answer is: not right now. Let's do the bitumen trips. Let's do the east coast. The lap can wait.

But here's where it gets interesting. Because even people with the perfect rig are holding back. And reason number three explains why.

Reason number three. The road itself has got harder.

The roads around Australia carry more caravans than ever. Australians took a record seventeen point three million caravan and camping trips in 2025, according to Tourism Research Australia. That's more people competing for the same parks, the same free camps, the same fuel stops and the same stretch of highway.

And many of those roads were never built for this kind of traffic. Big stretches of national highway are still two-lane roads with narrow shoulders. Road trains run on many of them. Floods and fires close sections each year. Roadworks seem to be everywhere.

In early 2025, floods cut the Bruce Highway in North Queensland. Other parts of the country deal with their own closures: the Kimberley in the wet, the Savannah Way after big rain, sections of the Stuart Highway in the north during severe weather. If you're on a lap and the road ahead shuts, you're stuck until it opens. Sometimes that's a day. Sometimes it's much longer.

Add the constant presence of road trains, the risk of animals on the road at dawn and dusk, and the long distances between towns, and the lap starts to look less like a relaxing retirement trip and more like an expedition.

Many people who were planning a lap have decided they'd rather spend more time somewhere beautiful and less time on the road getting there.

Before I get to reason number two, I want you to think about something. If you've been dreaming about the Big Lap for years, what would make you put it off? Hold that thought. Because reason number two is something a lot of people only realise once they're actually out there.

Reason number two. The lap doesn't look like the brochure anymore.

For years, the Big Lap was sold as freedom. Empty roads. Quiet beaches. Wake up wherever you like. Stay as long as you want.

And there are still moments like that. Out on the road, you can still find a campsite with no one else around, a sunset over the Indian Ocean that stops you in your tracks, a night sky in the outback so full of stars it doesn't look real.

But the classic route has become crowded. The famous spots along the lap have been photographed, posted and shared thousands of times. The beaches of the Coral Coast. The gorges of Karijini. Cable Beach at sunset. People turn up expecting a quiet experience and find a car park full of vans and a queue at the lookout.

At the same time, the people on the road have changed. It's no longer mostly retirees. There are young families doing a lap with the kids. Remote workers with Starlink dishes on the roof. Couples in their thirties who've rented out the house and taken a year off. That's great for them, and it's great for regional tourism. But it changes the feel. The grey nomad who wanted quiet afternoons in a shady park now has a neighbour with a drone, three kids on scooters and a work video call at full volume.

So some experienced travellers are deliberately going the other way. Instead of the famous lap, they're heading inland. To quiet towns in western New South Wales. To the Flinders Ranges outside school holidays. To small coastal towns that don't make it onto the lists. And they're finding what the lap used to be. Just not on the lap.

Which brings us to reason number one. The reason no government can fix.

Reason number one. Health, and the distance from help.

Here's the honest truth that every grey nomad eventually has to face. The older we get, the more our health matters, and the further we are from a good hospital, the more that worries us.

Australia is enormous. That's what makes the Big Lap so special. But it's also what makes it risky as you get older. Along big stretches of the route, the nearest major hospital can be many hours away. In remote parts of the country, health care often depends on small clinics, nurse-led services and the Royal Flying Doctor Service, which does an extraordinary job across vast distances. But no matter how good those services are, they can't change the geography. If you have a serious heart event in the middle of the Nullarbor, or on a remote stretch between Broome and Port Hedland, help is a long way off.

And it's not just emergencies. Many people in their seventies are managing ongoing conditions. Regular blood tests. Medication that needs repeat scripts. Specialist appointments that can't easily be moved. Eye checks. Skin checks. Physiotherapy after a joint replacement. All of that is easy at home. On a lap, it means planning your route around towns with pharmacies and GPs, and hoping you can get an appointment when you get there.

Governments have spent a great deal of money on rural and remote health over the years, and there are hard-working people trying to close the gap. But no amount of funding can put a cardiologist in every roadhouse, or shrink the distance between Kununurra and the nearest city. That's the reason number one problem: distance itself. And it's not going away.

So what are people doing? They're planning trips that stay within reach of larger regional centres. They're spending longer in places like Townsville, Toowoomba, Mildura, Albany, Bunbury, Bendigo. Towns with proper hospitals, good GPs and reliable pharmacies. They're keeping the remote stuff to shorter, well-planned trips, sometimes with other travellers for company. And they're putting the full lap on hold until they feel confident about their health, or deciding that they'll see the remote parts of the country in pieces, rather than all at once.

There's no shame in that. It's not giving up. It's being smart.

Let me paint you a picture of what that worry looks like in real life.

It's late afternoon on the Great Northern Highway, somewhere between Port Hedland and Broome. A long, flat road. Spinifex either side. Red dirt. Not a building in sight. A couple in their early seventies have pulled into a roadside rest area for the night. He's been a bit short of breath today. Probably the heat. Probably nothing.

She checks her phone. One bar of signal, then none. She walks to the other end of the rest area, holding the phone up. Still nothing. She knows the nearest town is a few hours' drive in one direction and a few hours in the other. She knows he's got heart medication in the van. She knows they've talked about this before, and agreed it would be fine.

And it probably will be fine. Most nights are. But she doesn't sleep much. And when they finally reach Broome, the first thing she does is ring their daughter and say, "I think we need to go home and get this checked properly."

I've heard versions of that story more times than I can count. It's not dramatic. Nobody gets airlifted. But it's the moment the Big Lap changes from a dream into a risk. And once people have had that moment, many of them don't want to have it again.

That's why you're seeing more grey nomads carry satellite messengers and personal locator beacons. More of them using satellite internet on the road. More of them planning their nights around towns with a hospital rather than the prettiest free camp. Technology helps. It can't bring the hospital closer, but it can bring help faster.

And it's why so many people are choosing a different kind of trip altogether. Let me give you a few examples of what grey nomads are doing instead of the full lap.

There's the river run. Following the Murray from the high country down to the coast in South Australia. Echuca. Swan Hill. Mildura. Renmark. Paddle steamers, red gums, wineries and caravan parks right on the water, with a decent regional hospital never too far away.

There's the slow Queensland winter. Rather than racing up the coast, people pick one or two places, Hervey Bay, Bowen, Townsville, and stay for six or eight weeks. Fishing, walking, coffee with the same neighbours every morning.

There's the Tasmanian loop. Over on the Spirit of Tasmania, around the island in a month or two. Short driving distances. Huge variety. Mountains, beaches, old towns, and nothing ever too far from help.

There's the South West of Western Australia, for those who've already made it across. Margaret River, Denmark, Albany, Esperance. Beautiful country, decent services, and much milder weather than the north.

And there's the inland loop through New South Wales and Queensland. Dubbo, Bourke, Lightning Ridge, Longreach, Winton. Outback skies and outback history, with bitumen most of the way.

None of these is the Big Lap. But every one of them is a real adventure. And for a lot of people, they deliver exactly what the lap promised: time, space and freedom, without the fear of being too far from home if something goes wrong.

Now, I want to tell you about a conversation I had recently. A man in his early seventies came in to talk about a smaller van. He and his wife had done most of the lap about ten years ago. Not the full thing. They'd turned back in Darwin because she'd needed surgery. They'd always meant to go back and finish it. And he said to me, "You know what, we've decided we're going to do the last bit, the bit we missed, Darwin to home, as its own trip. Just that. And then we're going to call it done."

I thought that was one of the wisest things I'd heard in a long time. You don't have to do it all at once. You don't have to do it the way everyone else does. You don't have to tick every box on someone else's list.

So here's my advice, if you're one of the many people with a Big Lap on the calendar and some doubts in your mind.

First, break it into pieces. A lap done in three or four separate trips over a few years is still a lap. You'll probably enjoy it more.

Second, plan your health. Talk to your GP before you go. Get your scripts organised. Carry a written list of your medications and conditions. Know where the hospitals are along your route. Think about whether you need a satellite communication device for the remote stretches.

Third, get your rig checked properly. Weighed. Serviced. Tyres in good condition. Make sure it's suited to the roads you're actually planning to drive, not the ones in the brochure.

Fourth, think about timing. Travelling outside peak season and school holidays can make the whole experience quieter, cheaper and more relaxing.

And fifth, don't feel guilty about doing something different. A month in Tasmania. A slow trip along the Murray. A winter in one great spot in Queensland. Those are real adventures too.

The Big Lap isn't dead. People are still doing it, and people will keep doing it for as long as there are caravans and roads. But the way grey nomads approach it is changing. They're wiser about the money, the roads, the rigs and their health. And they're finding ways to see the country that suit the stage of life they're in now.
