It's seven o'clock on a Tuesday morning at a weighbridge on the outskirts of Rockhampton. A couple in their late sixties have just rolled a twenty-three-foot tandem-axle caravan onto the scales, hitched behind a dual-cab ute they bought new three years ago. They've done this trip before. Brisbane to Cairns, then across to the Territory, then home before the wet. But this morning the man at the window prints out a slip of paper, slides it across the counter, and says one sentence. "Mate, you're over." Not over by a few kilos. Over on the ute. Over on the rear axle. And the caravan they paid well over a hundred thousand dollars for is the reason.

Six weeks later, that caravan is sitting on a dealer's forecourt with a "for sale" sign in the window. And they're back on the road in a campervan half its length.

They're not the only ones. Right across the country, in caravan parks from Broome to Batemans Bay, something is shifting. Thousands of grey nomads are quietly ditching their big caravans. No announcement. No fuss. They're just selling the rig, buying something smaller, and getting back on the highway.

I've spent years inside the caravan trade. I've seen what comes in on trade-in, and I've seen what goes out the door. And today I'm going to give you the seven real reasons this is happening. Some of them are about money. Some of them are about safety. And the number one reason, the one I'll get to at the end, is something the caravan manufacturers in this country simply cannot fix, no matter how good their build quality gets. Stay with me, because if you own a big van right now, that last reason might change how you think about your next trip.

Let's start the countdown.

Reason number seven. The fuel bill finally caught up with the dream.

When you're towing a big caravan, your fuel economy doesn't just dip. It falls off a cliff. A modern diesel ute that might sit around nine or ten litres per hundred kilometres empty on the highway can easily double that with two and a half tonnes or more hanging off the towbar, especially into a headwind across open country. Anybody who's towed across the Hay Plain in a southerly knows exactly what I'm talking about. You watch the needle drop and you start doing sums in your head.

Now, in 2026, fuel prices have been all over the place. The insurer CIL, which covers a lot of caravans in this country, came out mid-year and said it plainly: cost-of-living pressures and rising fuel prices are changing the way grey nomads plan their travel. Shorter trips. Closer to home. Fewer of those long two-thousand-kilometre hauls.

And here's what that looks like on the ground. You pull into a roadhouse somewhere west of Ceduna, you fill a long-range tank, and the number on the pump is a number you used to spend on a week's groceries. Do that every day or two for three months and the Big Lap starts to cost more than the caravan's annual depreciation.

So people do the sums. And when they put a smaller campervan, or a compact pop-top, or a lightweight hybrid next to their big rig, the fuel difference alone is enough to make them stop and think.

But fuel is only the start. Because the next reason isn't about what you spend. It's about what you can legally carry.

Reason number six. The weighbridge doesn't lie.

There's a word every caravanner learns sooner or later, and it's not a nice one. Overweight.

Every caravan has an ATM, the aggregate trailer mass, the most it's allowed to weigh fully loaded. Every tow vehicle has a GVM, its gross vehicle mass, and a GCM, the gross combination mass, the most the car and the caravan can weigh together. And here's the problem. Brochures talk about the tare weight, what the van weighs empty off the production line. But nobody travels empty.

Fill the water tanks. Add a full gas bottle or two. Throw in the annexe, the chairs, the generator, the second battery, the food, the clothes, the fishing gear, the coffee machine. Every one of those things goes on the scale.

Mobile weighing services around the country have been saying the same thing for years. A large share of the rigs they weigh are over at least one limit. Not by a little. Sometimes by hundreds of kilos. And what catches people out most is the ute. They buy a vehicle rated to tow three and a half tonnes, hitch up a three-tonne van, and think they've got plenty of margin. Then they find out that the ball weight pressing down on the towbar comes straight off the ute's payload. Load two adults and a canopy full of gear into the ute, and suddenly you're over GVM before you've even left the driveway.

That couple at the Rockhampton weighbridge? That's exactly what happened to them. Their van was within its own limit. Their ute wasn't.

And once you know you're over, you've got three choices. Leave things behind. Spend thousands on a GVM upgrade. Or get a smaller van. More and more people are choosing option three.

But here's the thing about being overweight that nobody talks about until something goes wrong. And that's reason number five.

Reason number five. The insurance fine print.

Pull out your caravan insurance policy tonight and read it properly. Not the glossy summary. The fine print. Look for the words "unroadworthy", "overloaded", or "not in accordance with manufacturer's specifications".

I'm not going to tell you what your particular insurer will do, because every policy is different and you need to check yours. But I can tell you what keeps people in the trade up at night. If there's a crash, and an assessor puts your rig on a scale afterwards and finds it was over its legal limits, that can become a very uncomfortable conversation. And at that point you're not talking about a fuel bill. You're talking about the whole value of the van, the tow vehicle, and potentially someone else's car as well.

People in their sixties and seventies have worked their entire lives to build up what they've got. Many of them have spent a big part of their super on the rig. And when they realise that a big, heavy, overloaded van could put all of that at risk with a single claim, a lot of them decide it's not worth it.

A smaller rig is easier to keep legal. Simple as that.

Now, if reasons seven, six and five are about money and paperwork, reason four is about something much more personal. It's about what happens behind the wheel.

Reason number four. Towing big gets harder every year.

Nobody likes to say this out loud, so I'll say it for you. Towing a long, heavy caravan takes strength, concentration and quick reactions. And those things don't get better at seventy-two.

Think about what towing actually involves. Reversing a twenty-two-footer into a tight drive-through site at dusk while your partner waves their arms in the mirror. Holding the rig steady when a road train comes past at a hundred kilometres an hour on a narrow stretch of the Barrier Highway and the bow wave hits you sideways. Feeling the van start to sway on a downhill run and knowing you've got about two seconds to respond correctly. Winding down the jockey wheel. Lifting the hitch. Crawling under to check the electrics.

Caravan sway is the thing experienced towers fear most. And the bigger and heavier the van relative to the car, and the further back the weight sits, the more seriously you need to take it. Road safety bodies and caravan clubs have been running towing courses for years precisely because so many people take on a big rig without ever having been taught how to handle one.

And then there's fatigue. Long hours behind the wheel with a big van behind you are tiring in a way that driving a car alone simply isn't. You're watching the mirrors constantly. You're managing every overtaking vehicle. You're braking earlier and planning every fuel stop.

So what are people doing? They're swapping to campervans and motorhomes they can drive like a big car. Or to compact vans under five metres that feel like nothing on the back. They park in a regular car space at Woolworths in Kununurra. They don't need a second person to guide them into a site. They pull up at a lookout without wondering if they'll be able to turn around.

When I talk to people trading in their big vans, this is the reason they're often the most honest about. "I just don't enjoy the driving anymore." And when you don't enjoy the driving, the whole trip starts to feel like work.

But even people who love towing are running into reason number three. And it's happening at the gate of the caravan park.

Reason number three. Big vans don't fit anymore.

Here's something that has quietly changed over the last few years. Caravan parks are fuller than they've ever been. Tourism Research Australia recorded a record seventeen point three million caravan and camping trips by Australians in 2025. That's not a niche hobby anymore. That's a national movement.

And those trips aren't just grey nomads. You've got families who discovered camping during the pandemic and never stopped. You've got remote workers living out of vans. You've got young couples doing six months up the west coast. They all want the same sites you do, in the same places, at the same time of year.

So what happens? Popular parks along the coast in peak season are booked months ahead. And when there is a site free, it's often a small one. The big drive-through sites that suit a twenty-three-foot van with a ute on the front are the first to go.

Then there's free camping and low-cost camping. The places grey nomads have always loved. Riverside spots along the Murray. Showgrounds in country towns. Station stays out west. Many of them were never designed for the huge rigs we see today. Tight entry tracks. Low branches. Soft ground after rain. National park campgrounds sometimes list maximum vehicle lengths. A twenty-four-foot van and a ute can push you past six metres of van plus five metres of car, and suddenly half the places you wanted to stay are off the list.

People who've been on the road for years tell me the same story. They'll pull up to a spot they've loved for a decade and find they can't get in. Or they spend their afternoons ringing ahead, trying to find a site long enough. The freedom that made them buy the caravan in the first place has shrunk.

And when you can't fit, you start to wonder why you're towing so much in the first place.

Before I get to reason number two, I want you to think about something. If fuel, weight, insurance, driving and site access are all pushing people towards smaller rigs, why didn't this happen years ago? Why now? The answer is reason number two, and it's about money in a way that might surprise you.

Reason number two. The resale window is closing.

During the boom years, especially from 2020 through to around 2022, the caravan market in Australia went a bit mad. Demand went through the roof. Waiting lists for new vans stretched out for many months. Second-hand vans were selling for close to what people had paid for them new. Some sold for more.

That's when a lot of today's big vans were bought. Big, heavy, loaded with every option. Off-road suspension, huge solar arrays, lithium batteries, ensuites, washing machines. Fair enough. When everyone's buying, everyone buys big.

But markets don't stay hot forever. In the last couple of years, people in the trade have watched the used market cool down. More vans coming in on trade. More listings sitting longer on the big classifieds sites. Some manufacturers going through tough times. We've already talked on this channel about the caravan builders that have closed their doors.

So the smart money, the people who've been around the market a long time, have started to think: if I'm going to sell this big van, I'd rather sell it now while it's still worth something, than in five years when it's older, heavier than what everyone wants, and harder to move.

That's the quiet part. A lot of people aren't selling because they're in trouble. They're selling because they've seen which way the wind is blowing and they want to get out while the price is still reasonable. And then they buy something smaller, lighter and often cheaper, and they bank the difference. For a retired couple, that difference can mean another year or two on the road.

Which brings us to reason number one. The one I promised you at the start. The reason the caravan manufacturers can't fix.

Reason number one. The tow vehicle limit.

Here's the truth that the whole caravan industry is up against. You can build a caravan as well as you like. You can use better materials. You can make it stronger, more comfortable, more luxurious. But you cannot change what the car in front of it is legally allowed to carry and tow.

Most of the popular tow vehicles in Australia, the big dual-cab utes and the large four-wheel-drive wagons, are rated to tow around three and a half tonnes. That number has barely moved in years. And for most of them, the payload, what the vehicle itself can carry, isn't huge once you add people, a canopy, a bull bar, a long-range tank and a fridge in the tray.

Meanwhile, caravans got bigger. Buyers wanted more features, and every feature adds weight. Bigger water tanks. More batteries. Thicker chassis. Ensuites. Full-size fridges. Off-road suspension. Every year, the vans crept closer to the limit of the cars pulling them. Many towing experts suggest you shouldn't load your van past around eighty per cent of your car's towing capacity if you want a safe margin. A lot of big vans out there today blow straight through that.

And this is a problem the van makers cannot solve from inside the factory. The weight of the car, its brakes, its rear axle, its legal ratings, all of that is set by the vehicle manufacturer and by the law. A caravan company can shave a few kilos here and there, but they can't make a three-tonne van feel like a one-and-a-half-tonne van behind a ute.

On top of that, the vehicle market itself is changing. There's a big push towards hybrid and electric vehicles. And some of the new models simply aren't built to tow the kind of weight a big caravan needs. Nobody knows exactly what the tow vehicle of 2035 will look like. If you're seventy today, you're probably not keen to bet your retirement on that answer.

So that's the real reason. The caravan can only ever be as big as the car in front of it allows. And for a growing number of grey nomads, they've realised they bought a van that's bigger than their car, their licence, their budget and their energy can comfortably handle.

The answer, for thousands of them, is to go smaller. A campervan you can drive into any town. A pop-top that weighs half of what the old van did. A small off-road camper behind a regular wagon. A slide-on camper on the back of the ute. Something you can live with for months at a time without being nervous every time a truck comes past.

And here's what surprised me most. When people make the swap, the stories that come back are almost always positive. They talk about spontaneity. Stopping wherever they like. Pulling into a little town on the Darling River without planning it two weeks in advance. Not needing a weighbridge to tell them whether they're safe. Less stress. Less spending. More road.

Now, let me take you inside a typical trade-in conversation, because this is where you see the change most clearly.

A couple walks onto a dealer's yard on a Saturday morning. Usually it's somewhere on the fringe of a capital city. Campbellfield in Melbourne. Penrith in Sydney. Caboolture north of Brisbane. They've driven in with the big van on the back, because they want a valuation. They've got a folder with the service history, the receipts for the solar upgrade, the lithium batteries, the new tyres. They're proud of that van, and they should be. It's taken them to Cape York, the Kimberley, the Flinders Ranges.

And then the questions start. Not "what's the biggest thing you've got?" like it was five years ago. Now it's "what's the lightest thing that still has a proper bed?" "Can I tow it with a regular SUV?" "Has it got a toilet I don't have to empty every day?" "Can one of us manage it on our own if the other one gets sick?"

That last question comes up more than you'd think. And it's one of the most important. Because for a lot of couples, the big van was set up as a two-person job. One drives, one guides. One winds the stabiliser legs, one connects the water and power. If one partner has a knee replacement, a heart scare, or simply can't do the lifting anymore, the whole rig becomes a problem. A smaller, simpler setup means either of them can handle it alone. That's peace of mind you can't put a price on.

The other thing you notice is what they're happy to leave behind. The washing machine they used four times in three years. The second spare wheel. The generator that's been replaced by solar. The outdoor kitchen that took twenty minutes to set up. When people actually look at how they travelled, they often realise they were carrying a lot of weight for things they barely used.

And once they see that, the smaller rig doesn't feel like a sacrifice. It feels like a relief.

So where does that leave you, if you've got a big van parked in the driveway right now?

First, get it weighed. Fully loaded, the way you actually travel. Check your car and your van against every single limit: ATM, GVM, GCM, ball weight and axle loads. If you're under, great. If you're over, you need to fix it before your next trip, not after.

Second, read your insurance policy properly, and if you're not sure what it means, ring your insurer and ask them directly.

Third, be honest with yourself about the driving. If the towing is fun, keep going. If it's become something you dread, there's no shame in going smaller.

And fourth, keep an eye on the second-hand market. If you're thinking of selling, timing matters.

The grey nomad lifestyle isn't disappearing. Far from it. More Australians are on the road than ever before, and people over fifty-five make up the majority of those caravan and camping trips. It's just changing shape. The rigs are getting smaller, the trips are getting smarter, and the people who've been doing this the longest are often leading the way.
