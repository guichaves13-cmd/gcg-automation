It's mid-morning at the Nullarbor Roadhouse. The wind is coming straight off the Great Australian Bight, and it's carrying fine white dust across the car park. A couple in their late sixties are standing at a fuel bowser beside their caravan. He's watching the numbers roll. She's watching his face. The tank is nearly full, and the total on the pump is a number neither of them expected to see. Behind the roadhouse, there's a sign that's become famous in this country, the one with a camel, a wombat and a kangaroo on it, warning you about the next ninety-odd kilometres. They take a photo with it. They smile. And then they get back in the ute and start doing the maths on how far the rest of that fuel is going to get them.

Welcome to the Nullarbor. The Eyre Highway crossing from Ceduna in South Australia to Norseman in Western Australia. Roughly one thousand two hundred kilometres of road through some of the emptiest country on earth. For generations, it's been a badge of honour for grey nomads. You haven't really done Australia until you've crossed it.

But right now, grey nomads are getting crushed out here. By the distances. By the fuel. By the wind. By the rules at the border. By things they didn't know until they were already halfway across.

Let's head west.

Number seven. The fruit you didn't eat.

Before you even reach the Nullarbor proper, there's a trap waiting for you. And it's in your fridge.

Western Australia has strict quarantine rules designed to keep pests and diseases out of the state's farms. If you're driving in on the Eyre Highway, there's a quarantine checkpoint near Border Village. And they mean business. Fresh fruit, many vegetables, honey, plants, soil, and a list of other items can't go in. Inspectors check vehicles, and that includes your caravan. They'll open the fridge. They'll look in the cupboards.

South Australia has its own restrictions too, around fruit fly, and there's a quarantine station at Ceduna for people coming from the west.

Now, this shouldn't be a surprise. It's well publicised. But every year, people roll up to the checkpoint with a fridge full of fresh produce they bought in Port Augusta or Ceduna, thinking they'd be fine. And they end up throwing it in the bin. On a fixed pension, watching a week's worth of fruit and veg go into a quarantine bin hurts.

The fix is simple. Check the current rules on the Western Australian and South Australian government websites before you leave. Plan your shopping. Eat it, cook it or leave it behind before you get to the border. Then restock in Norseman or Esperance on the other side.

Now that's a small cost. Number six is a much bigger one.

Number six. The price on the pump.

Out here, there are no supermarkets with fuel discounts. There's no shopping around. Between Ceduna and Norseman, your fuel comes from a string of roadhouses, places like Nundroo, the Nullarbor Roadhouse, Border Village, Eucla, Mundrabilla, Madura, Cocklebiddy, Caiguna and Balladonia. They're small, family-run or independent businesses, a long way from anywhere, and everything they sell has to be trucked in over enormous distances.

So fuel costs more. Often a lot more than you paid in Adelaide or Perth. And it's not because anyone's ripping you off. It's because getting diesel to the middle of the Nullarbor is expensive, and these businesses have to survive all year on passing traffic.

Here's the problem for grey nomads. Towing a caravan across this country uses a lot of fuel. Long straight roads at highway speed, often into a strong headwind, can drive your consumption up sharply. And you've got no choice but to fill up at roadhouse prices, sometimes more than once a day.

The fuel price volatility of 2026 has made it worse. The insurer CIL said mid-year that rising fuel prices and the cost of living were changing grey nomads' travel plans. And there's nowhere you feel that more than here.

The smart move? Fill up completely in Ceduna or Norseman, where prices are usually a bit better. Carry a jerry can if your setup allows it and you know how to do it safely. And budget for roadhouse fuel. Don't be the person who arrives at Eucla doing panicked arithmetic at the bowser.

But fuel isn't just about money out here. And that's number five.

Number five. The distance between tanks.

Some of the gaps between roadhouses on the Eyre Highway are long. Not impossibly long, but long enough that a heavy rig, a headwind and a bit of bad planning can turn a simple drive into a very anxious afternoon.

Here's the thing people forget. The range of your vehicle on the brochure isn't your range towing a caravan. With a big van behind you, your effective range can drop dramatically. A tank that gets you a long way solo might get you much less with a van on the back, into the wind.

And roadhouses aren't fuel stations in the city. Occasionally they have problems. Deliveries get delayed. Pumps break down. Power goes out. It doesn't happen often, but when it does, the next one could be a long way off.

That's why the people who cross regularly follow a simple rule. Never pass up fuel. If you're at a roadhouse and you're under three-quarters, fill up. Even if you think you'll make the next one. Especially if you think you'll make the next one.

And while you're watching the fuel gauge, there's something else out there working against you. Number four.

Number four. The wind.

People who've never crossed the Nullarbor think of it as hot and still. People who have crossed it think of the wind.

The Nullarbor Plain is flat and treeless. The word Nullarbor itself is usually explained as coming from the Latin for "no tree". There's nothing to stop the wind coming off the Southern Ocean. And when it blows across the highway, a caravan becomes a sail.

A strong crosswind pushes the van sideways. A headwind cuts your fuel economy and makes the engine work hard for hours on end. And then there's the road train. When a big truck passes you in the opposite direction at speed, the bow wave of air hits your van and can push it towards the edge of the road. Combine that with a crosswind, and experienced towers say the van can start to move around in a way that gets your heart pumping.

The answer isn't heroics. It's speed. Slow down when the wind is up. Your fuel economy improves, your van is more stable, and you give yourself more time to react when a road train goes past. Some people stop altogether on the worst days, pull into a rest area, and wait it out.

And while you wait, you might notice something else about the Nullarbor. You're not alone out here. And that brings us to number three.

Number three. The animals.

The Nullarbor is not empty. It's full of wildlife, and much of it is on or near the road. Kangaroos. Wombats. Emus. Camels, in some areas. Wedge-tailed eagles feeding on roadkill. And livestock in places, wandering across the highway.

The riskiest times are dawn and dusk, when kangaroos are most active. At those times, the road can come alive with movement. And a kangaroo hitting the front of a ute towing a caravan at highway speed can cause serious damage. Smashed headlights, broken radiators, bent bull bars. And if you swerve to avoid it with a heavy van on the back, that can be far more dangerous than the hit itself.

You can see the evidence along the highway. The roadkill. The marks on the bitumen.

Experienced travellers have a simple rule out here. Don't drive at dawn or dusk. Get to camp early. Set up in daylight. Watch the sunset from a camp chair, not from behind the wheel. It means shorter days. But a shorter day is a lot cheaper than a radiator on the Nullarbor.

Let me take you through one day on the crossing, because this is where all of these things come together.

You wake up at a caravan park behind the roadhouse at Border Village. You've just come through the quarantine checkpoint the afternoon before, and the fridge is looking a bit bare. Outside, the wind has dropped overnight. Good sign.

Then you look at your watch and realise you're not sure what time it is. That's another Nullarbor quirk. Along part of the highway near the border, places like Eucla and the roadhouses around it use their own unofficial time zone, sitting between South Australian and Western Australian time. Travellers joke about it, but when you're trying to work out when the roadhouse opens or when the sun will set, it can genuinely confuse you.

You fill up before you leave. The fuel gauge reads full. You head west. The road drops down the Eucla Pass, and for a while the highway runs below the escarpment, with the old limestone cliffs on your right and the ocean somewhere out to your left behind the dunes. It's beautiful country. You stop at the ruins of the old Eucla telegraph station, half buried in sand, and take photos.

Back on the road, the wind starts to pick up. By late morning it's blowing hard from the south-west, right into your face. You watch the fuel gauge start to drop faster than you'd like. You slow down. Ninety becomes eighty. Eighty becomes seventy-five. A road train comes towards you, and when it passes, the van gives a little shove that makes you grip the wheel.

At Madura, you stop and top up the tank, even though you've still got more than half. You've learned that lesson. You have a sandwich, look out from the Madura Pass across the plain, and get back in the car.

By Cocklebiddy, it's early afternoon, and you're tired. You've only done a few hundred kilometres, but it feels like more. You think about pushing on to Caiguna. Then you think about the time. Then you think about the kangaroos. And you pull in for the night.

That's a good day on the Nullarbor. Nothing broke. Nobody got hurt. But it took every bit of planning you'd done, and you still spent more on fuel than you'd hoped.

Now imagine that same day if you hadn't filled up at Madura. Or if the wind had been stronger. Or if you'd pushed on at dusk. Every one of those small decisions is the difference between a story you laugh about in the camp kitchen and a story you'd rather forget.

And there's one more thing that's quietly changed on this road. The traffic. With Australians taking a record seventeen point three million caravan and camping trips in 2025, according to Tourism Research Australia, the crossing isn't as lonely as it used to be in peak season. Rest areas that once had one or two vans can now have a dozen. The popular ones fill up by mid-afternoon. And the roadhouses, with limited powered sites, can be booked out. So even on the Nullarbor, of all places, you now need to think about where you'll stop each night before you get there.

Before I get to number two, I want you to think about something. Imagine you're at a rest area, halfway across, a few hundred kilometres from the nearest real town, and something goes wrong. A blown tyre on the van. A bearing that's started to smoke. A warning light on the dash. What happens next? That's number two.

Number two. When something breaks.

On the Nullarbor, there's no mechanic on the corner. Some roadhouses can help with basic repairs or tyres. But for anything serious, you're often looking at a tow, sometimes over hundreds of kilometres, and a bill that can be eye-watering. Then there's the wait for parts, which might have to come from Perth or Adelaide.

People in the caravan repair trade will tell you the most common problems on long crossings are the ones that start small. Tyre pressures that weren't checked. Wheel bearings that weren't serviced. A suspension component that was already tired. A long, hot crossing at highway speed with a heavy load finds every weakness.

Tyres are the big one. A blown tyre on a caravan at speed can cause the van to sway or worse. And if you've already used your one spare, you're in trouble. Many experienced travellers carry two spares for the van on remote crossings.

The fix, again, is preparation. Service the van before you go, including the bearings and brakes. Check tyre pressures every morning, and feel the hubs at every stop to see if one's running hotter than the others. Carry the tools and parts you know how to use. Join a roadside assistance program and check exactly what it covers out here, because some cover a lot less in remote areas than people assume.

And then there's number one. The one that experienced travellers say they underestimated the most. The one you can't buy your way out of.

Number one. The isolation.

The Nullarbor is beautiful. The Bunda Cliffs drop straight into the Southern Ocean. In winter, you can see southern right whales off the Head of Bight. The night sky is extraordinary. The silence is something you'll never forget.

But that same isolation is what crushes people when things go wrong. Mobile phone coverage across the crossing is patchy, often limited to near the roadhouses. Long stretches have no signal at all. If you have a medical emergency between roadhouses, you can't just ring for help and wait ten minutes.

Look closely at the road and you'll notice something that tells you a lot. In several places, the Eyre Highway is marked as an emergency airstrip for the Royal Flying Doctor Service. White markings on the bitumen and signs at the roadside. That's how remote this road is. The highway itself doubles as a runway for when help has to come from the sky.

The Flying Doctor does remarkable work. But even they can't be everywhere at once. And for older travellers with health conditions, that distance from help is the thing that really matters.

It's not just physical health, either. Plenty of travellers say the crossing takes a mental toll. Days of the same flat horizon. Long hours of concentration with the wind pushing the van around. The famous Ninety Mile Straight, between Balladonia and Caiguna, is one of the longest straight stretches of road in Australia. Around one hundred and forty-six kilometres without a bend. It sounds like a fun fact. When you're driving it towing a caravan in a crosswind, it's an exercise in staying awake.

And here's the part money can't fix. You can buy a better caravan. You can buy more fuel capacity. You can buy satellite communication. And you should. But you can't make the Nullarbor smaller. You can't put a hospital at Cocklebiddy. You can't change the fact that for much of this crossing, you are a very long way from anywhere.

That's why the people who do it best treat it with respect. Not fear. Respect.

So here's my Nullarbor checklist, from everything people in the trade have heard over the years.

Get your rig weighed, and make sure you're under every limit. An overloaded rig in a crosswind is a bad combination.

Service the van and the car before you go. Bearings, brakes, tyres, suspension. Carry spares you know how to fit.

Fill up at every opportunity, and budget for roadhouse fuel prices.

Check the current quarantine rules for both states, and don't carry produce you'll have to throw away.

Carry a satellite communication device or a personal locator beacon. And tell someone your plan, and when you expect to reach the other side.

Don't drive at dawn or dusk. Slow down in the wind. Take breaks. Split the crossing into several short days rather than one or two long ones.

And take your time to enjoy it. Stop at the Head of Bight. Walk out to the cliff lookouts. Play a hole or two on the Nullarbor Links, the golf course spread out along the highway, with holes at towns and roadhouses all the way across. Sit under the stars at a quiet rest area and listen to the silence.
