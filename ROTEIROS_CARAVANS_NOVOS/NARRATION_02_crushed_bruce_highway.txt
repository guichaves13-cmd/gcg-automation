It's just after two in the afternoon on a two-lane stretch of the Bruce Highway, somewhere between Gin Gin and Miriam Vale. The bitumen is patched in three different shades of grey. There's no shoulder to speak of, just a drop-off into gravel and long grass. In front of you, a caravan doing eighty-five. Behind you, a B-double loaded with cattle, close enough that its grille fills your entire rear-view mirror. And behind him, a line of cars that stretches back around the bend. There's no overtaking lane for kilometres. Every time the van in front drifts onto that broken edge, it twitches. And every time it twitches, you hold your breath.

This is the Bruce Highway. Roughly one thousand six hundred and seventy kilometres from Brisbane to Cairns. The road that's often described as Australia's most-travelled grey nomad route. The road that every winter carries a slow-moving river of caravans north, chasing the warm weather to Hervey Bay, the Whitsundays, Townsville, Mission Beach and the Daintree.

And right now, grey nomads are getting crushed on it.

Not crushed by one big thing. Crushed by a lot of small things that pile up, kilometre after kilometre. The road surface. The trucks. The roadworks. The floods. The fuel. The fatigue. And one thing at the end of this video that no amount of government money seems to fix fast enough.

I've been inside the caravan trade a long time. I've talked to the people who tow this road every single year, and I've seen the damage that comes back to the workshop afterwards. So let's drive it together. Brisbane to Cairns. Seven stretches of trouble. And stay with me until the end, because the last one is the one most people never see coming, and it's the reason a lot of experienced nomads are quietly changing the way they head north.

Let's go.

Trouble spot number seven. The run out of Brisbane.

You'd think the start would be the easy bit. It isn't.

The first couple of hundred kilometres north of Brisbane are some of the busiest road in Queensland. Commuter traffic through Caboolture and Morayfield. Holidaymakers heading for the Sunshine Coast. Freight trucks running the coast. And mixed in among all that, caravans. Lots of them, especially on a Saturday morning in May or June, when the southern winter starts to bite and everyone seems to leave at once.

The good news is that a lot of money has gone into this section over the years. The Cooroy to Curra upgrade, which bypasses Gympie, opened up a big stretch of divided highway. If you haven't towed it since that work was done, you'll notice the difference.

But a divided highway full of traffic is its own kind of stress. Merging lanes. Trucks in the left lane, impatient drivers in the right. Long gradients up past the Glass House Mountains where a heavy rig starts to slow, and everyone behind you starts to stack up. If you're towing a big van at ninety, you become the obstacle, and drivers let you know about it.

Experienced travellers have a simple trick for this stretch. Leave early. Really early. Be past Gympie before the day-trippers wake up. It sounds obvious, but the number of people who leave Brisbane at ten in the morning on a long weekend and then wonder why they're exhausted by lunch is remarkable.

And once you get past Gympie, the road changes. Because north of here, the divided highway starts to run out. And that's where trouble spot number six begins.

Trouble spot number six. The narrow middle.

Between Maryborough and Rockhampton, the Bruce Highway goes through some of its most frustrating country. Childers. Gin Gin. Miriam Vale. Long stretches of two-lane road. Overtaking lanes that come up every so often, and never quite often enough.

This is where you meet the big trucks properly. The Bruce Highway is Queensland's main freight spine. Everything that goes up the coast by road goes on this highway. Fuel tankers. Livestock. Supermarket freight. Machinery. B-doubles and, in some sections, even longer combinations. Their drivers are professionals, most of them very good at what they do. But they're running to tight schedules, and a caravan sitting at eighty-five on a two-lane road is a problem for them.

Here's what happens. A truck comes up behind you. There's no overtaking lane. You've got a narrow shoulder that's broken at the edges. The polite thing to do is pull over and let them past. But where? If you pull onto a soft or crumbling shoulder with a three-tonne van, you can damage a tyre, crack a rim, or worse, drop a wheel off the edge and lose control.

So people don't pull over. And the line behind them grows. And somewhere back in that line is a driver who's lost patience, who decides to overtake three vehicles on a short straight. That's how the really bad crashes on this road happen.

Caravan clubs and road safety campaigns keep repeating the same advice. Use your UHF radio. Channel forty is the one most truckies listen to on the highway. If a truck is behind you, call him up, tell him where the next safe spot is, and let him past when it's safe. It sounds small. It makes an enormous difference.

But even if you handle the trucks perfectly, you can't control what's under your wheels. And that's trouble spot number five.

Trouble spot number five. The road surface itself.

Ask anyone who tows the Bruce regularly and they'll tell you about the surface. Patches on patches. Edge breaks. Potholes that appear after rain. Rough sections where the whole van starts to bounce and shudder, and you can hear the cupboards rattling behind you.

The RACQ, the Queensland motoring club, has for years highlighted sections of the Bruce in its surveys of the state's worst roads. Members keep naming it. And it's easy to see why. This is a long highway running through tropical country. Heat, heavy rain and heavy trucks all take their toll on bitumen. Repairs are constant, and the road is so long that by the time one section is fixed, another one is falling apart.

For a car, rough road is uncomfortable. For a caravan, it's damaging. Every jolt goes straight through the chassis, the suspension, the hitch, the cabinetry and the fittings inside. Anyone in the repair side of the trade will tell you they see vans come back from a winter up north with cracked welds, loose cupboards, broken hinges, and fridge doors that won't seal properly. Sometimes the owners didn't even notice it happening.

So the advice is simple, and most people ignore it. Slow down on rough sections. Check your tyre pressures every morning. Have a quick walk around the van every time you stop, looking for anything loose, anything leaking, anything that's moved. It takes two minutes. It can save you thousands.

Now, there's one part of this highway where the trouble isn't just rough road. It's the road disappearing completely. That's trouble spot number four.

Trouble spot number four. When the water comes over.

North Queensland is wet country. And the Bruce Highway crosses a lot of rivers, creeks and low-lying flood plains. When the big rain comes, sections of it go under.

In early 2025, severe flooding in North Queensland cut the highway in several places. The bridge at Ollera Creek, north of Townsville, was badly damaged. Ingham was cut off. Communities between Townsville and Cairns were isolated for days. It was a stark reminder that the main road up the east coast of Australia can simply close.

Now, most grey nomads head north in the dry season, roughly from May through to September or October, precisely to avoid this. And that's smart. But weather doesn't always read the calendar. Unseasonal rain can still hit, and the shoulders of the wet season, early and late in the year, can catch people out. If you're one of those travellers who likes to stay up north until November or leave early in the year, you need to be watching the forecasts and the road reports closely.

And here's the part that really crushes people. When the highway closes, you don't just sit for an hour. You can be stuck for days. Caravan parks in the towns on either side fill up instantly. Supermarket shelves empty. Fuel can run short. If you're on a fixed budget, extra nights in a park and extra days of food add up quickly. If you've got a medical appointment or a family event down south, you can miss it.

The lesson? Check the QLDTraffic website or app before you leave each morning. Carry extra water and food. Have a plan B, and know which towns have the services you need if you get caught.

But floods are dramatic. Most of the time, the thing that really wears grey nomads down on this road is something much quieter. That's trouble spot number three.

Trouble spot number three. Roadworks. Everywhere.

If you've towed the Bruce in recent years, you know the sight. Orange cones disappearing into the distance. Speed limits dropping from a hundred to sixty to forty and back again. A lollipop person holding a stop sign in the full sun. A single lane of loose gravel with your van bouncing behind you.

There's a good reason for all of it. The state and federal governments have committed billions of dollars to upgrading the Bruce Highway over the long term. Wider lanes. More overtaking lanes. Better shoulders. Flood-resilient bridges. Safer intersections. When it's finished, many sections will be far better than they are today.

But "when it's finished" is a long way off. And in the meantime, you're driving through a construction zone that stretches across a whole state. Every set of roadworks adds time to your day. Every stop-and-go section adds fuel. Every stretch of loose gravel adds risk to your windscreen, your paintwork and the front of your van. Plenty of people arrive at their destination with a chipped windscreen and a stone-damaged front panel they didn't have when they left.

Here's a tip from people who do this road every year: fit a stone guard or a mesh protector to the front of your van, and keep your distance from the vehicle in front through gravel sections. It's the easiest bit of damage to prevent.

And while you're sitting in that queue of roadworks, watching the fuel gauge, you might start thinking about trouble spot number two.

Trouble spot number two. The fuel and the fatigue.

One thousand six hundred and seventy kilometres. That's a long way in a car. Towing a caravan, it's a serious undertaking. Most sensible travellers break it into at least five or six driving days, often many more, with rest stops along the way.

And every one of those kilometres costs money. Towing a heavy van can roughly double your fuel use compared to driving the same vehicle without it. Fuel prices in regional Queensland can be noticeably higher than in Brisbane, and in 2026 prices have been anything but stable. Insurers and industry groups have noticed grey nomads cutting trips shorter and staying closer to home because of fuel costs. On a highway this long, you feel every cent.

Then there's fatigue. Queensland's road safety campaigns have long targeted fatigue on the Bruce, because the long, straight, monotonous stretches through cane country can send even experienced drivers drowsy. There are driver reviver stops at various points on the highway, often run by volunteers, where you can pull in for a free cuppa and a biscuit. Use them. They're there for a reason.

And here's the hard truth for older drivers. Reaction times slow down as we age. Concentration fades faster in the afternoon heat. Towing takes more out of you than it did at fifty. The single most effective safety tool most grey nomads have is a shorter driving day. Two hundred and fifty, maybe three hundred kilometres. Stop by mid-afternoon. Set up in daylight. Sit down with a cold drink and watch the sunset in Bowen, instead of pushing on to Townsville in the dark with roos on the road.

Before we get there, let me walk you through one real driving day, because it puts all of this together.

You wake up in a caravan park in Rockhampton. The Tropic of Capricorn marker is just down the road. You've got Mackay in your sights, a bit over three hundred kilometres north. On paper, that's an easy day.

You hitch up at seven. Do your walk-around. Tyres look good. Lights work. You pull out onto the highway and within twenty minutes you're out of town and into open grazing country, with the Berserker Range fading in the mirror.

North of Rockhampton, the towns get small and far apart. Marlborough. Then a long stretch where there isn't much at all. This is one of those parts of the Bruce where people get caught out. They pass a roadhouse thinking they'll fill up at the next one, and the next one is a long way off. If there's one rule on this stretch, it's this: when you see fuel and you're under half a tank, fill up.

By mid-morning the road starts to wind through hillier country. Long climbs. Long descents. Your ute is working hard on the way up, and your brakes are working hard on the way down. A truck comes up behind you on a climb. You can hear his engine labouring as much as yours. You grab the UHF, tell him you'll pull into the next rest area, and you both breathe a bit easier.

You stop at a rest area for a cuppa. There are already four other caravans in there. Two from Victoria, one from South Australia, one from Tassie. Every one of them is heading the same way you are, and every one of them is going to be looking for a site in Mackay this afternoon.

Back on the road, you hit roadworks. Fifteen minutes stopped in the sun. Then a stretch of fresh gravel at forty. A stone flicks up off a car coming the other way and you hear it crack against the front of the van. You wince. Nothing you can do.

By the time you reach Sarina, it's early afternoon and you're tired. Properly tired. The kind of tired where your eyes feel heavy and your shoulders ache from gripping the wheel. You ring the park in Mackay you were hoping to stay at. Full. You ring another. Full. The third one has a site, but it's small, and you'll have to back in.

That's one day. Three hundred kilometres. No disasters. Nothing went wrong. And you're still worn out.

Now multiply that by five or six days, and add the weather, the traffic and the cost of fuel, and you start to understand why so many grey nomads arrive in Cairns feeling like they need a holiday from their holiday.

Which brings us to trouble spot number one. The one that money doesn't fix quickly. The one that might change the way you travel.

Trouble spot number one. Everyone is going north at the same time.

Here's the thing about the Bruce Highway that almost nobody talks about. The real problem isn't any single pothole, truck or roadwork zone. It's the timing.

Every year, as the weather cools down south, grey nomads begin their great northern migration. Victorians, South Australians, people from Tassie and New South Wales, all pointing their rigs towards the sun. And because so many of them follow the same calendar, they end up on the same road at the same time. May, June and July on the Bruce Highway can feel like one long, slow caravan convoy, mixed in with the trucks that never stop.

And it's not just grey nomads anymore. Australians took a record seventeen point three million caravan and camping trips in 2025, according to Tourism Research Australia. Families, remote workers, young travellers. They all want the Whitsundays in winter too. So the caravan parks in Airlie Beach, Mission Beach, Port Douglas and the popular free camps along the way fill up. People drive longer days because they can't get a site. They push on into the evening because there's nowhere to stop. And tired drivers towing heavy vans on a narrow, busy, patched-up highway is exactly the combination you don't want.

This is the problem no government upgrade can fix overnight. You can widen a road, but you can't stop half the country deciding to drive it in the same eight weeks. Every new overtaking lane helps. Every new bridge helps. But as long as the road is carrying more caravans, more trucks and more traffic every year, the upgrades are always running a step behind.

So what are smart grey nomads doing about it?

Some are leaving earlier. Heading north in April, before the rush, and coming home in August, before everyone else turns around.

Some are taking a different road entirely. Going up the inland routes, through places like Roma, Emerald, Charters Towers. Quieter roads, different scenery, fewer caravans, though you trade that for longer distances between towns.

Some are booking their key parks months in advance and planning every driving day, so they're never forced to push on late.

Some are breaking the trip into much smaller pieces, staying a week in Hervey Bay, a week in Yeppoon, a week in Bowen, letting the rush pass them by.

And some, honestly, are deciding that the Bruce Highway in peak season just isn't worth it anymore, and finding somewhere warm that's a little bit closer to home.

None of those choices is wrong. What's wrong is going up there unprepared, driving too far each day, overloaded, tired, and in a hurry.

So here's my checklist if you're heading up the Bruce. Get your rig weighed and keep it under every limit. Check your tyres, including the van's, every morning. Carry a UHF radio and use channel forty. Check QLDTraffic before every driving day. Keep your days short, and stop at the driver reviver sites. Protect the front of your van from stones. Book ahead in peak season. And above all, don't let a line of traffic behind you pressure you into a decision you wouldn't otherwise make. Pull over when it's safe. Let them past. You're retired. You've got time. They don't.

The Bruce Highway is still one of the great drives in this country. Sugar cane glowing in the evening light. The first sight of the Coral Sea past Proserpine. Mangoes from a roadside stall in Bowen. The rainforest closing in around Tully and Innisfail. It's worth doing. It's just worth doing smart.
