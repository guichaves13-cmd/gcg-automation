If you're a pensioner in Britain, there is a wave of changes coming at you between now and the end of twenty twenty-seven, and most people have no idea it's heading their way. Not because they're not paying attention. But because these changes are scattered across different departments, buried in budget documents, and explained, when they're explained at all, in language that would put a judge to sleep.

Number six. The winter fuel payment you might have to pay back.

You'll remember the uproar when the winter fuel payment was taken away from most pensioners. Then came the U-turn. The payment was brought back for many more people, but with a twist. If your taxable income is above a threshold, which was set at thirty-five thousand pounds a year, the payment is effectively clawed back through the tax system.

Now here's where it catches people out. Plenty of pensioners don't think of themselves as well-off. But add together a full State Pension, a decent workplace pension from forty years at the council or the bank, and maybe a bit of rental income or savings interest, and some people find themselves over that line without realising. And because the clawback happens through tax, often through your tax code or self assessment, it can arrive as a nasty little surprise months later.

You can opt out of receiving the payment in the first place if you know you'll be over the threshold, which saves the hassle of paying it back. But a lot of people simply don't know the option exists. I heard from a retired couple in Kent, where he'd had a career in engineering, and they were genuinely baffled when their tax code changed. They thought something had gone wrong. Nothing had gone wrong. It was the winter fuel clawback doing exactly what it was designed to do. They just hadn't been told in plain English. That's number six.

Number five. The State Pension creeping into tax.

This one is quietly enormous. The personal allowance, the amount you can earn before paying income tax, has been frozen at twelve thousand, five hundred and seventy pounds, and the freeze has been extended for years to come. Meanwhile, the State Pension keeps going up each April under the triple lock, which is genuinely good news on its own.

But put those two facts side by side. The full new State Pension is now not far off that frozen allowance, and on current trends, within the next couple of years, the full new State Pension on its own could reach or overtake it. That means millions of pensioners who have never paid tax in retirement could find themselves pulled into the tax system, and millions more who already pay a bit will pay more.

The government has said it doesn't want pensioners whose only income is the State Pension to have to fill in tax returns for tiny amounts, and there's been talk of a simplified approach. But the details matter, and if you have any other income at all, even a small private pension, you are almost certainly already paying more tax than you would have if the allowance had kept pace with prices. This is what people call a stealth tax. No headline rate goes up. You just slowly pay more. That's number five.

Number four. Driving licences, eyesight, and the age seventy rule.

Once you hit seventy, your driving licence doesn't renew automatically. You have to renew it every three years, and you have to declare that you meet the eyesight standards and that you don't have any medical conditions that affect your driving. It's free to renew online, but plenty of people don't realise their licence has quietly expired.

Why does this matter more now? Because there's been a growing debate in Britain about older drivers and eyesight. Coroners and road safety groups have repeatedly called for compulsory eye tests for older drivers, and the government's road safety strategy has looked at tightening things up. Whatever the final shape of the rules, the direction of travel is clear: more checks, not fewer.

And here's what really catches people out. If you're driving with an expired licence, or you've failed to tell the DVLA about a condition you were required to report, your insurance may not cover you properly in an accident. Some people only find out at the worst possible moment. I spoke to a lady in Norfolk whose husband had let his licence lapse after his seventy-third birthday without realising. He had a minor bump in a supermarket car park, nobody hurt, and the insurer started asking questions. It got sorted, but she said, "We'd have been in real trouble if it had been serious." For a lot of older people in rural Britain, the car is their lifeline. Don't let a bit of paperwork take it away. That's number four.

Number three. Council tax bills that keep on climbing.

Council tax isn't a new rule, of course, but the way it's moving is catching pensioners out. Councils across England have been raising bills at or close to the maximum allowed without a local referendum, year after year, and some councils in financial trouble have been given permission to go even higher than that. Combine that with talk, in various think tanks and reviews, about revaluing properties or reforming council tax bands for the first time in decades, and you have a lot of nervous pensioners in larger family homes.

Think about who this hits. The retired couple who've lived in the same four-bedroom house in the South East since nineteen eighty-five. Asset rich, cash poor, as the saying goes. Their house might be worth a lot on paper, but their income is a pension. Every big council tax rise comes straight out of the money they live on.

Here's what too many people miss. If you live alone, you can get a twenty-five percent single person discount. If you're on a low income, you may qualify for council tax reduction, sometimes called council tax support. And if you get Pension Credit, you may get a full reduction. Which brings me to the uncomfortable truth that hundreds of thousands of pensioners who are entitled to Pension Credit still don't claim it, according to the government's own estimates. Pension Credit is the gateway to a whole bundle of other help, including the winter fuel payment for those on lower incomes, help with council tax, and a free TV licence for over-seventy-fives. If you think you might be anywhere near the line, please check. It takes a phone call. And if the phone isn't your thing, ask a family member, a neighbour, or a local Age UK or Citizens Advice volunteer to help you with the online checker. People are often amazed. I've heard of pensioners who assumed they'd get nothing, and turned out to be entitled to a few thousand pounds a year once Pension Credit and the help that comes with it were added up. Pride stops a lot of people claiming. But this isn't charity. It's a benefit you paid towards your whole working life. That's number three.

Number two. Savings tax and the interest trap.

For years, when interest rates were close to zero, most pensioners didn't need to worry about tax on their savings. The personal savings allowance meant you could earn up to a thousand pounds in interest tax-free as a basic rate taxpayer. Nobody with ordinary savings got near that.

Then interest rates went up. Suddenly, a pensioner with, say, twenty or twenty-five thousand pounds in an easy access account or a fixed rate bond was earning enough interest to come close to that allowance, or go over it. And here's the catch. If you're a higher rate taxpayer, your allowance is only five hundred pounds. And remember number five? As the frozen personal allowance drags more pension income into tax, more people's savings interest can become taxable too.

HMRC gets information on your interest directly from banks and building societies, and then adjusts your tax code or sends you a bill. People have been genuinely shocked to get a letter asking for tax on interest they thought was free. And the government has signalled changes to ISAs too, with cash ISA limits being tightened for younger savers, though the rules for over-sixty-fives have been treated differently. If you've got savings outside an ISA, it's worth asking whether some of it could sit inside one, within the rules that apply to you. A simple move like that can save real money over a few years. That's number two.

And now, number one. Pensions and inheritance tax from April twenty twenty-seven.

This is the big one. From April twenty twenty-seven, unused pension pots, the private and workplace defined contribution pensions that so many people have built up, will be brought into the scope of inheritance tax. For decades, those pots have usually sat outside your estate. You could leave your pension to your children, and in many cases it passed on without inheritance tax at all. That was one of the great planning tools of the last decade. People were even told to spend their other savings first and leave their pension untouched for exactly that reason.

That's changing. From April twenty twenty-seven, the value of most unused pension funds and death benefits will be counted as part of your estate for inheritance tax purposes. Now, there are still allowances. The nil-rate band, the residence nil-rate band if you leave your home to children or grandchildren, and the exemption for anything left to a spouse or civil partner all still apply. So plenty of families won't pay a penny. But for families with a decent house in the South East plus a reasonable pension pot, the numbers can stack up fast, and suddenly an estate that was comfortably under the limit is well over it.

And there's an extra sting that has worried a lot of experts. In some cases, if you die after seventy-five, the person who inherits your pension may also pay income tax when they draw that money out. So the same pot can face inheritance tax first, and then income tax on what's left. Some advisers have warned that, in the worst cases, the combined bite could take well over half of a pension pot before it reaches the next generation.

Why is this the one that hurts most? Because you won't feel it. Your children will. And they'll feel it at the worst possible moment, while they're grieving, dealing with probate, and trying to sort out your affairs. The administration has also been a big worry. The rules put a lot of responsibility on personal representatives, often a son or daughter, to work out values and deal with pension schemes and HMRC on tight deadlines.

I had an email from a gentleman in Cheshire, retired after a long career in sales, who told me he'd spent ten years carefully following the advice to leave his pension alone and live off his savings. "I did everything I was told," he wrote. "And now they've moved the goalposts." I hear that phrase a lot. Moved the goalposts. And honestly, I understand the frustration. When people plan around the rules for years, and the rules change, it feels unfair, whatever your politics.

So what can you actually do? Well, first, don't panic, and don't rush into anything. Big, sudden decisions with pensions can create their own tax problems. Second, check that your expression of wishes, sometimes called a nomination form, is up to date with every pension scheme you've got. Third, look at whether your will is still right for the new rules, especially the residence nil-rate band and how your home passes on. Fourth, think about whether it now makes more sense to draw from your pension during your lifetime, perhaps to help family now rather than later, with the gifting rules in mind. Regular gifts out of normal surplus income can be exempt from inheritance tax if you meet the conditions, and that's a tool too many people ignore. And fifth, if the sums involved are significant, sit down with a regulated financial adviser who actually knows this area. A few hundred pounds in advice could save your family many thousands.

Now, I said six, but I'd be letting you down if I didn't mention two more things in passing, because they're catching people out right now as well, and they're linked to everything we've just talked about.

The first is the State Pension age itself. It's been sixty-six for some time, but the move up to sixty-seven is now under way, phased in for people born from the spring of nineteen sixty onwards, between twenty twenty-six and twenty twenty-eight. If you're in your early sixties, please don't assume you'll get your State Pension at sixty-six like your older brother or your friend from work did. Check your exact date on the official State Pension age calculator. I've heard from people who had planned to retire on their sixty-sixth birthday, handed in their notice, and then discovered they had months still to wait. Months with no wage and no State Pension. That's a gap you can plan for if you know about it, and a shock if you don't. And there's a review of whether the age should rise further and faster in the future, so the younger you are, the more important it is to keep checking.

While you're at it, check your National Insurance record too. You usually need thirty-five qualifying years for the full new State Pension, and there are gaps on a lot of people's records, especially women who took time out to raise children, or people who worked abroad, or did self-employed work and didn't realise. In some cases you can fill those gaps with voluntary contributions, and for many people that's one of the best value deals going, because a few hundred pounds paid now can add a meaningful amount to your pension every single year for the rest of your life. But there are deadlines, and the rules on how far back you can go have tightened. So please don't put this off.

The second thing is scams. Every time a rule changes, the fraudsters are right there behind it. When the winter fuel payment changed, there were reports of text messages telling pensioners to click a link to apply for their payment. When there's talk of tax refunds, the fake HMRC emails start flying. And with pensions and inheritance tax in the news, expect to see more cold calls offering to review your pension or move it somewhere safer to beat the new rules.

So here are the golden rules. The winter fuel payment is paid automatically if you're eligible, you don't apply by text message. HMRC will never ask you to confirm your bank details by clicking a link in a text. And nobody legitimate cold calls you out of the blue to move your pension. If someone does, put the phone down. If you're unsure about anything, hang up, find the number yourself from an official letter or website, and ring back. A real organisation will never mind you doing that.

I know a lady in Lancashire, eighty-one, sharp as a tack, who got one of those winter fuel texts. She told me she nearly clicked it, because it looked so official, with the right logo and everything. What stopped her? Her granddaughter had shown her a video just like this one the week before. That's it. That's the whole reason.
