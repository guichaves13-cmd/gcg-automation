Something is happening on park home sites up and down this country right now, and almost nobody outside those little gated lanes is talking about it. For years we were told park homes were the clever answer. Sell the family house, buy a tidy single-storey home for a fraction of the price, put the difference in the bank, and enjoy a quiet retirement with friendly neighbours and a bit of garden. And for a lot of people, that is still true. But in twenty twenty-six, the park home world has hit a breaking point. Costs are climbing faster than pensions, homes are sitting unsold for months, and some owners are discovering that the thing they thought was their biggest asset is worth a lot less than they assumed when they finally try to get out.

So today I'm going to walk you through the seven pressures squeezing park home owners right now, counting down from seven to one. Some of these you'll know. Some will surprise you. And number one, I promise you, is the one that keeps owners awake at night, and it's the one hardly any buyer asks about before signing. So stay with me to the end, because if you own a park home, or you're thinking of buying one, that last one could cost you tens of thousands of pounds.

Before we start, a quick word. I'm not here to tell you park homes are bad. I know plenty of people who are blissfully happy on their park, and I'd never knock that. But happiness and money are two different things, and you deserve the full picture. Right, let's get into it.

Number seven. The pitch fee that never stops rising.

If you own a park home, you own the home, but you don't own the ground it sits on. You pay a pitch fee to the site owner, usually every month, and that fee goes up once a year. Now, here's the bit of good news. The rules changed a little while back so that annual increases in England are now linked to the Consumer Prices Index rather than the old Retail Prices Index, which tended to run higher. That was a genuine win, and the campaigners who fought for it deserve credit.

But here's the problem. Even with CPI, the increases compound. A pitch fee of around two hundred pounds a month a few years ago might well be pushing two hundred and fifty or more today on a lot of sites, and reports from owners suggest some premium parks are well beyond that. Meanwhile, the full new State Pension sits at roughly two hundred and thirty pounds a week. Do the maths on a single pensioner, and the pitch fee alone can swallow a quarter of their pension before they've bought a loaf of bread or paid for a drop of gas.

I spoke to a gentleman a while ago, let's call him Derek, who moved onto a park in Lincolnshire with his wife about twelve years back. He told me that when they bought, the pitch fee felt like pocket money. "It was less than our old council tax and water put together," he said. Now his wife has passed, he's on his own, and he told me, quite simply, "The fee didn't halve when she died. Everything else did." That one stuck with me. And that's number seven.

Number six. Energy costs that hit park homes harder than bricks and mortar.

Park homes are wonderful in summer. Light, airy, easy to look after. But a lot of the older homes, especially the ones built before the more modern insulation standards, lose heat faster than a traditional house. Thinner walls, more exposed underneath, and often a reliance on bottled gas or LPG rather than mains gas. And when you buy energy that way, you don't always get the same price protections that people on a standard mains tariff get.

Age UK has been raising the alarm for years that older people in park homes struggle to keep warm. And in twenty twenty-six, with the winter fuel payment now means-tested and a lot of pensioners having had to adjust to that, a cold park home in January is not a romantic picture. It's a worry. Some owners tell me they're living in one room through the winter, with an electric blanket and a hot water bottle, just to keep the bills down.

Now, there are schemes out there. Some park home owners have been able to get help with external wall insulation or improved heating through various grant schemes over the years, and it's always worth asking your local council what's available right now. But if you're buying an older home, please, please ask for the actual winter bills from the seller. Not an estimate. The real bills. That one question can save you a world of pain. And that's number six.

Number five. Site fees and charges that creep in quietly.

This is one that catches people out. Beyond the pitch fee, there can be water and sewerage charges passed on by the site owner, sometimes with an admin element, plus things like gas supplied through the site, electricity metered through the site, and so on. Now, there are rules about how much a site owner can charge when they resell utilities, and most site owners play it straight. But owners do sometimes find that the costs they assumed were included are actually extra, or that the way the charges are worked out isn't crystal clear.

My advice here is simple. Ask to see your written statement, which is the agreement that sets out your rights and the site owner's rights, and read it properly. Ask what's included in the pitch fee and what isn't. Ask for the last three years of utility charges. And if anything doesn't add up, don't just grumble about it on the bench by the noticeboard. Get in touch with one of the residents' associations or the free advice services that specialise in park homes. You'd be amazed how many people simply never ask.

And here's a little story. A lady I heard from in Somerset, I'll call her Margaret, noticed her water charge had jumped noticeably in one year. She almost let it go. Instead, she knocked on a few doors, found five neighbours who'd seen the same thing, and the residents' association raised it with the site. It turned out to be a billing error. Small win, but over a few years that's real money back in pensioners' pockets. Never underestimate a few determined neighbours with a kettle on. That's number five.

Number four. Homes sitting unsold for months.

This is where the breaking point really starts to show. On many parks, there's a simple problem. The people who bought twenty or twenty-five years ago are now in their eighties and nineties. Some need to move into care. Some have passed away, and their children are left trying to sell a home they never lived in. And all of that is happening at the same time on the same parks.

So what do you get? More homes for sale than buyers. Estate agents and park home brokers have been reporting that some older homes are taking much longer to sell than they used to, especially the ones that need work, and especially on sites further from towns, shops and buses. Meanwhile, brand-new homes on the same park, sold by the site owner, can look very shiny next to a thirty-year-old home with a tired kitchen and a damp patch in the bedroom.

And when a home doesn't sell, the pitch fee doesn't stop. The family keeps paying, month after month, sometimes for a year or more, while the home sits empty. I've heard from sons and daughters who've ended up paying thousands of pounds in pitch fees on a home their late mum or dad can't live in anymore, simply because there's no buyer. That's a cost nobody puts in the brochure. That's number four.

Number three. The value trap.

Here's the uncomfortable truth that a lot of people discover far too late. A traditional house usually sits on land, and over the long run land tends to hold its value or rise. A park home is different. You own the structure, not the plot. And structures, like cars, tend to age. A park home can lose value over time in a way that bricks and mortar generally doesn't, particularly as it gets older and approaches the point where buyers start asking how many years it's got left.

There was a story in the Yorkshire press some time ago about retirement homes that had plummeted in value, and the details vary from case to case, but the theme comes up again and again on owner forums. People buy expecting the same kind of investment as a house. They sell, or their family sells, and they're stunned at the number.

Now, newer, well-built, well-maintained homes on popular, well-run parks can hold their value pretty decently, and there's genuine demand, especially with average park home prices still well under half of what a typical bungalow costs these days. GB News reported an average of roughly one hundred and forty-five thousand pounds against bungalows at three hundred and thirty-five thousand or more. That price gap is exactly why so many people buy. But please go in with your eyes open. Think of a park home more like a comfortable lifestyle purchase and less like a nest egg that's guaranteed to grow. If it does grow, wonderful. If it doesn't, at least you won't be shocked. That's number three.

Number two. Finance and the shrinking pool of buyers.

This one doesn't get talked about enough. Getting a conventional mortgage on a park home is very difficult. Most mainstream lenders simply don't offer them, because the buyer doesn't own the land. There are some specialist lenders and finance arrangements out there, but they're limited and often more expensive.

What does that mean in practice? It means most park home buyers have to pay cash. And the people who have that kind of cash are, mostly, people who've just sold a house. So the entire park home market depends on one thing: older homeowners selling their houses at a decent price and moving down. When the wider housing market is slow, when houses take longer to sell, when chains collapse, that river of cash buyers slows to a trickle. And park home sellers feel it straight away.

Add to that the fact that many parks have age restrictions, often fifty or fifty-five plus, and some have rules about pets, about children visiting for long periods, about working from home. Every rule, however sensible, makes the pool of buyers that little bit smaller. I'm not saying those rules are wrong. A lot of residents love them, and they're part of why parks feel so peaceful. But when it comes time to sell, you're fishing in a smaller pond than you would with an ordinary bungalow. That's number two.

Right. Number one. The ten percent commission.

This is the one. This is the one that makes park home owners go quiet when you mention it. When you sell your park home, the site owner is legally entitled to take a commission of up to ten percent of the sale price. Not ten percent of your profit. Ten percent of the whole price.

Let me put that in real numbers. You sell your park home for one hundred and fifty thousand pounds. Up to fifteen thousand of that can go to the site owner. On top of that, you may have agent or broker fees. You may have had to knock the price down because the home's been on the market for eight months. You may have paid the pitch fee all through those eight months. Add all of that together, and you can see how somebody who thought they'd walk away with a hundred and fifty thousand ends up with a lot less.

Now, to be fair, this isn't a secret rule that site owners are sneaking in. It's in the law. Site owners argue that the commission helps fund the running and upkeep of the parks, and that removing it would put pressure on pitch fees instead. That's their side, and it's a real argument. Campaigners, on the other side, have been fighting for years to get it cut or scrapped, pointing out that homeowners in normal houses don't have to hand a slice of their sale to anyone just for the privilege of selling.

And here's why this is so live right now. In twenty twenty-six, this issue has been back in front of Parliament. There was a Westminster Hall debate in April, where MPs described park home residents as the forgotten home owners in our housing system. The government ran a call for evidence on the sales commission that closed at the end of May. And a petition from the Park Home Owners Justice Campaign has been pushing for a final decision this year. So, as I'm recording this, there's a real possibility that something could change. Or nothing could change at all, again, like so many times before.

And that uncertainty is itself part of the breaking point. Some owners tell me they're holding off selling, hoping the commission might be cut. Some buyers tell me they're holding off buying, waiting to see what happens. And when both sides are waiting, the market stalls. Homes sit. Pitch fees keep ticking. Families keep paying.

And there's one more thing I want to add here, almost a bonus, because it ties all seven of these together. It's the question of who owns your park, and what happens when that changes.

Over the last few years, more and more parks have been bought up by larger operators. Some of those operators run their parks beautifully. They invest in roads, lighting, drainage and communal areas, and residents say the place has never looked better. Others, according to residents' groups, are more focused on selling new homes on empty pitches than on looking after the people who already live there. The point is, when your park changes hands, the character of the place can change too, and you have very little say over who buys it.

There's also the question of licences. Make absolutely sure the park you're buying on has a proper residential site licence, allowing people to live there all year round. That sounds obvious, but there have been sad cases, including one reported in Yorkshire, where pensioners bought what they believed were permanent homes on sites that were only licensed for holiday use. When the council or the site enforced the rules, people faced being told they couldn't stay. Imagine selling your house, moving your whole life into a lovely little home by the coast, and then being told it was only ever meant to be a holiday let. The heartbreak in those stories is very real.

So check the licence. Check the written statement. Check whether the site is a residential park home site, not a holiday park with homes that look the part. And if the seller or the sales office gets vague when you ask, that tells you something.

One more thing on this. The rules around fit and proper persons, meaning the checks that site owners have to pass to run a residential park in England, were brought in to protect residents from the worst operators. Councils keep a register. It costs you nothing to look up whether your site owner is on it. Five minutes on the phone or online can give you real peace of mind.

I mention all this because the breaking point isn't just about money. It's about control. When you own a house, you decide when to repaint, when to sell, and who you sell to, within reason. When you own a park home, there's always another party in the picture. Most of the time that relationship works perfectly well. But you need to know it's there.

So what do you actually do with all of this?

If you already own a park home, here are a few honest pieces of advice. First, keep your home in good order. A tidy, well-maintained home with recent work done, new windows, a decent boiler, a sound base, sells faster and for more. Second, keep every receipt and every document. Buyers love paperwork. Third, join or support your residents' association, because a qualifying residents' association has real rights to be consulted on things that affect your park, and there is genuine strength in numbers. Fourth, if you've got family, talk to them now about what happens when the time comes to sell. Don't leave them guessing.

If you're thinking of buying, please do these things. Visit the park more than once, including on a cold, wet weekday in winter. Talk to residents, not just the sales office. Ask for the written statement before you commit. Ask what the pitch fee has been for the last five years. Ask for real energy bills. Ask how long homes on that park typically take to sell. And always remember the ten percent when you work out what you'll get back one day.

And if you're a family member trying to sell a parent's park home right now, you are not alone. Thousands of families are in exactly the same spot. Get advice from the specialist park home advice services, talk to the site owner early and politely, and get two or three valuations rather than just one. Be realistic on price from day one. I know it's tempting to list high and see what happens, especially when it was mum's home and it holds so many memories. But every month the home sits there costs you a pitch fee, and an overpriced home often ends up selling for less in the end, after two or three painful reductions. A fair price on day one is very often the kindest thing you can do for yourself, and for the buyer who's about to start their own chapter there.

I'll leave you with something Derek said to me at the end of our chat. I asked him whether, knowing everything he knows now, he'd buy his park home again. He thought about it for a long time. Then he said, "Yes. But I'd have kept more money back. I thought the savings from selling the house were spare. They weren't spare. They were for later. And later came sooner than I thought."

That's the lesson, really. Park home living can be a lovely, gentle, sociable way to spend your retirement. But the costs don't stand still, the home doesn't always hold its value, and getting out can be harder and more expensive than getting in. Know that going in, and you'll be fine.
