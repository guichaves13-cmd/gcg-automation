Picture this. Two thousand and nineteen. A man in Harrogate walks out of his local hi-fi shop with a box under each arm and a receipt for just under four thousand pounds. It is the very latest thing. A streaming system, wireless, app controlled, the shop assistant told him it would see him out. Last month he tried to sell it. The best offer he got, after three weeks on a well-known auction site, was six hundred and fifty pounds. That is a loss of more than eighty per cent in under seven years. And here's the bit that stings. He'd have done better buying a secondhand car.

This is British HiFi, and today we are going through twelve kinds of hi-fi kit that have lost up to eighty per cent of their value here in the UK, the gear that dealers quietly shake their heads at, the boxes that sit on the shelf of every part-exchange room in the country, and that nobody, frankly, wants any more. Some of these will make you wince if you own one. A couple of them might make you smile, because the collapse in price means there's a bargain hiding in plain sight. And number one on this list, well, I'd bet good money you've got one in a cupboard right now and you have no idea what's happened to it. So stay with me right to the end.

Now, before we start, a quick word on how I've put this together. I've spoken to dealers, I've watched the secondhand listings and the forum classifieds for months, and I've compared what these things cost new against what they're actually fetching today. The numbers are approximate, they move week to week, and condition matters enormously. But the pattern is very, very clear. Let's begin.

Number twelve. Big home cinema receivers from around ten years ago. Remember when every living room in Britain was going to have seven speakers and a subwoofer? The industry certainly thought so. People spent fifteen hundred, two thousand, sometimes more on a heavy black AV receiver with more sockets on the back than a telephone exchange. Today, dealers say they can barely give them away. Why? Because the world moved on twice. First the video standards changed, so a receiver that can't pass the latest picture formats becomes a bottleneck between your new telly and your Sky box. And then the soundbar arrived, and most families decided they'd rather have one slim bar than seven speakers and a spaghetti of cables. A receiver that cost around two thousand pounds in twenty fifteen will often fetch somewhere between two and four hundred today. That's a drop of up to eighty per cent, and in some cases more. Now here's the twist I promised. For pure two channel music, some of these old receivers, the better ones, are actually rather decent amplifiers. If you just want to drive a pair of speakers in a spare room, you can get a lot of metal for very little money. Just don't expect it to talk to your new television.

Number eleven. iPod docks. Oh, the iPod dock. I have a soft spot for these, I really do. In the late two thousands, every British kitchen seemed to have one, and the posh ones were properly posh. Some of the premium British designs cost four or five hundred pounds and genuinely sounded lovely. The problem is the plug. The old thirty pin connector was retired by Apple in twenty twelve, and then the iPod itself was discontinued. So these docks became a beautiful sculpture with nothing to sit in it. You will see them at car boot sales for twenty quid. You'll see them in charity shops for ten. I had a chap write in to me last year, a retired engineer from Shrewsbury, and he said he'd kept his for the look of it, and every Christmas his grandchildren ask him what it is. That's when you know something's lost its value. When the grandchildren need it explained.

Number ten. Standalone DAB tuners. Now, this one is a little sad, because there was a time when a proper hi-fi DAB tuner was a status symbol. The BBC was pushing digital radio hard, and the separates makers responded with handsome tuners at three, four, five hundred pounds. But two things happened. First, a lot of the early units only did the original DAB, not the newer DAB plus standard that many stations have since moved to. And second, everybody discovered that their streamer, or their smart speaker, or even their telly, could play Radio Three and Radio Four over the internet without any aerial at all. Dealers tell me that a hi-fi DAB tuner is one of the hardest things to shift on the secondhand shelf. Fifty to a hundred pounds is typical, from a starting price several times that. If you love your radio, and plenty of us do, an older FM tuner is often the better buy, and in a funny twist, some of the classic FM tuners have actually held their value better than the digital ones that were meant to replace them.

Number nine. MiniDisc decks. I can already hear some of you groaning. MiniDisc was the format of the future in about nineteen ninety eight, and for a few years it really was brilliant. You could record off the radio, you could make your own compilations, you could carry them around in a pocket. Hi-fi MiniDisc decks cost a few hundred pounds, and the top ones were more. Then the MP3 player came along, and then the iPod, and MiniDisc simply evaporated. For years these decks were worth almost nothing, twenty or thirty pounds, often less. Now, I'll be honest with you, there's a small collector revival going on, the same way cassette has had a bit of a moment, and the very best decks are creeping up again. But the average MiniDisc deck in an average condition is still worth a fraction of what it cost, and getting blank discs is a hobby in itself.

Number eight. First generation network streamers. This is a big one, and it's the one that catches out the most serious listeners. Around twenty ten to twenty fifteen, the British hi-fi industry went all in on streaming. Some superb machines came out, thousands of pounds each, built like tanks, sounding glorious. And the electronics inside them were absolutely fine. The trouble is the software. Streaming services change, apps change, phones change, and a box that relied on a particular app, or a particular chip, or a particular service, can find itself left behind. To be fair, several British makers have been very good about supporting their older streamers for years. But the market doesn't care about fairness. Buyers see a streamer from twenty twelve and they think, will this still work next year? And that fear alone knocks the price down. A machine that cost around three thousand pounds new can sell for six or seven hundred. That's the Harrogate man I told you about at the start. And it's a lesson that anyone buying the latest streaming box today should think hard about.

Or are you sitting there smugly with a turntable and an amplifier from nineteen eighty five that's actually worth more now than when you bought it? Tell me in the comments. Right. Number seven. Multi room systems. You know the sort. A little box in every room, all talking to each other, music following you round the house. When these first appeared they felt like magic, and the British makers had their own versions, some of them very expensive indeed, with central servers and touch panels on the wall. Here's the problem. A multi room system is only as good as its weakest link, and that is usually the controller, the app, or the central server. If one bit stops being supported, the whole lot can become a set of very expensive paperweights. Dealers say that when one of these comes in for part exchange, they often price it as spares. And installers say the cost of ripping out a built-in system and replacing it can run into thousands. If you're thinking about buying a full house system secondhand because it looks cheap, please, please check what happens if the app disappears. Because cheap can very quickly become worthless.

Number six. First wave active wireless speakers. Now, I want to be careful here, because some of these are terrific. Active speakers with the amplifiers built in, streaming built in, just plug them into the wall and away you go. Several highly regarded British loudspeaker companies made their names in this category over the last decade. And sound wise, many of the early ones still hold up beautifully. But they suffer from exactly the same problem as the streamers. All the clever bits are inside the speaker, so when a newer generation comes out with better connections, a newer app, support for the latest formats, the older ones look dated overnight. A pair that cost two thousand pounds might struggle to fetch six or seven hundred a few years later. That's a much steeper drop than a pair of good passive speakers, which, as we'll see, often barely move in price at all. There's a lesson there. The more computer you put inside a piece of hi-fi, the more it behaves like a computer when you come to sell it.

Number five. Flagship CD players and transports from the late two thousands. This one surprises people. A top end CD player from around two thousand and eight might have cost two, three, four thousand pounds. It's a lovely heavy thing, beautiful finish, transport that glides like silk. And today? Dealers say many of them trade for a quarter of their original price, sometimes less. The reason is simple. A lot of people ripped their CD collections, moved to streaming, and put the discs in the loft. So demand fell off a cliff. There's also the worry about the laser mechanism. If a bespoke transport fails and the part is no longer made, you've got a very handsome doorstop. Now, I'll say this. If you still love CD, and plenty of people in this country do, this is one of the best times in history to buy a superb player. You're getting four thousand pounds worth of engineering for maybe eight hundred. Just ask the seller what the laser is, and whether a replacement can still be found.

Number four. Universal disc players. The ones that would play absolutely everything. CD, DVD, Blu ray, SACD, DVD audio, the lot. For a few years these were the ultimate box for the serious enthusiast, especially for those high resolution music discs. Some of them cost well over a thousand pounds. Today, the high resolution disc formats have mostly gone niche, streaming offers high resolution at the press of a button, and nobody wants a big player just to watch the odd film when the television has an app for that. Prices have fallen by seventy or eighty per cent in many cases. There's a small group of collectors who still chase the very best ones, but for most people, these are boxes that are hard to sell, hard to justify, and very hard to give away to the grandchildren.

Number three. Expensive cables. Ah, now I'm going to upset somebody. Let me tell you a quick story. Years ago a friend of mine, I'll call him Graham, bought a set of speaker cables and interconnects that cost him, and I'm not exaggerating, more than his amplifier. He was convinced they transformed the sound. Maybe they did, to his ears, and that's his business. But when Graham came to sell them, he found out what the market thinks. Secondhand cable prices are brutal. Used cables, even from well known names, routinely sell for a small fraction of their list price. Partly because buyers can't easily tell how old they are or how they've been treated, and partly because, let's be honest, a lot of people aren't willing to pay big money for cable without hearing it in their own system first. Graham's set, which cost him the thick end of a thousand pounds, fetched about a hundred and fifty. He laughs about it now. Mostly.

Number two. High end headphone amplifiers and portable players from the boom years. Around the middle of the last decade, there was a huge burst of interest in personal audio. Portable high resolution players, fancy headphone amps, all sorts of little boxes promising studio sound on the train to Waterloo. Some of them cost more than a decent pair of speakers. And then the smartphone got good enough, wireless headphones got good enough, and the whole category deflated. Many of those portable players run old versions of Android, they can't run modern apps, and their batteries are tired. Dealers say they see them come in and simply don't know what to price them at, because the buyers just aren't there. Losses of seventy to eighty per cent are common. The exception, and there's always an exception, is a well built desktop headphone amplifier with a good reputation. Those can hold their own. But the little portable ones? Ouch.

Before I give you number one, let me tell you what's not on this list, because it's just as important. Good passive loudspeakers from the established British names have tended to hold their value remarkably well. A classic pair of standmounts or floorstanders, looked after, often sells for not far off what someone paid for them years ago, sometimes more if they've become sought after. Turntables from respected British makers, too, are remarkably resilient, as are many simple, well built integrated amplifiers. The common thread? No software. Nothing to go out of date. Just a box that does one job well for decades. Keep that in mind, because it's the single most useful thing in this whole video.

Right. Number one. And I said you'd probably have one in a cupboard. It's the all in one micro system. The compact stereo. The neat little unit with a CD drawer, a radio, maybe a USB socket, and a pair of matching little speakers, often made by a very respectable brand. Millions of these were sold in Britain between the late nineties and the late twenty tens. They went in bedrooms, kitchens, student flats, conservatories. A good one cost three or four hundred pounds, a premium one more. And today? Honestly, they're worth almost nothing. Ten pounds, twenty pounds, if you can find someone who wants one. Charity shops are turning them away because they can't sell them. Recycling centres are full of them. Of everything on this list, this is the category that's lost the most value in the most homes, and in many cases it's well beyond eighty per cent. Ninety. Ninety five.

Now, here's why it matters, and why I put it at number one. It's not because a micro system was a bad buy. Many of them did a lovely job for years. It's because almost everyone has one, and almost everyone assumes it's worth something. It isn't, in money terms. But in another sense, it might be worth a great deal. Because if you've got a decent pair of little speakers from one of those systems, and a cheap secondhand amplifier from the classifieds, you can build a second system for a grandchild or a spare room for next to nothing. The value isn't in selling it. It's in using it.

Before I wrap up, let me give you a quick checklist, because if you're going to go hunting in this collapsed market, you want to come home with a bargain and not a burden. First, for anything digital, a streamer, a wireless speaker, a multi room box, look up whether the maker still lists it as supported, and whether the app still works on a current phone. Five minutes on the maker's website can save you a few hundred pounds. Second, for anything with a disc drawer, ask the seller to show you it playing, start to finish, and ask what happens if the laser fails. Can a part still be had? Is there an independent repairer near you who knows the brand? We are lucky in this country to still have some brilliant small repair workshops, and they are worth their weight in gold. Third, ask how old the item really is, not how old the seller thinks it is. Serial numbers and old receipts tell you a lot. And fourth, and this is the one most people skip, ask yourself whether you'll actually use it. A bargain that sits in a box in the spare room is not a bargain. It's just clutter that cost you money.

And if you're on the other side, if you're the one selling, here's my honest advice. Don't wait. With digital kit especially, prices tend to go one way, and every year you hold on, another newer model comes along and pushes yours further down. If you're not using it, sell it now, to someone who will, and put the money towards something that holds its value. A good pair of speakers. A tidy amplifier. A turntable. The boring, solid, simple things. Because in hi-fi, boring is beautiful when it comes to resale.

So let's draw the threads together. What's the lesson from all twelve? I think it's this. In hi-fi, as with cars, the things that lose value fastest are the things that rely on technology that moves quickly. Software, apps, formats, connectors, chips. The things that hold their value are the things that do a simple job, beautifully, for a very long time. A good pair of speakers. A well built amplifier. A proper turntable. That's not nostalgia talking, that's just what the secondhand market tells us, week after week.

And there's a flip side, which is what makes this hobby such fun. Because when something loses eighty per cent of its value, but still works perfectly, somebody gets a bargain. That flagship CD player for a quarter of its price. That big old receiver for a spare room. That streamer from a decent maker who still supports it. If you go in with your eyes open, the collapse in value is someone else's loss and your gain.
