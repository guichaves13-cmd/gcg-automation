Walk into almost any used guitar room in Canada this November, from a Long and McQuade in Mississauga to a little consignment shop in Halifax, and you will see something that would have been unthinkable three years ago. Guitars that used to sell in a weekend are just hanging there. Week after week. Same price tag, then a lower price tag, then a little orange sticker that says "make an offer." And still nobody bites.

I spent the last few weeks talking to shop staff, scrolling Kijiji and Facebook Marketplace listings from Victoria to St. John's, and tracking what is actually moving and what is collecting dust. And I came away with a list of twelve guitars that Canadian sellers simply cannot get rid of right now. Some of them will surprise you. A couple of them are guitars people paid serious money for during the pandemic boom. And the number one guitar on this list? I promise you will not guess it, and if you own one, you need to hear why before you try to sell it this winter. So stay with me right to the end.

Quick bit of context first, because it matters. Between twenty twenty and early twenty twenty two, everybody in this country picked up a guitar. We were stuck inside, the snow was piling up, cheques were coming in, and gear prices went absolutely bananas. Used guitars were selling for more than new ones because new ones were back ordered for months. Then the music stopped. Interest rates jumped, groceries went up, rent went up, the loonie wobbled around seventy two, seventy three cents US, and suddenly all those pandemic guitars came back out of the closet at the same time. Dealers say used inventory in a lot of Canadian stores is higher than they have seen in over a decade. So you have a flood of supply and a buyer who is counting every dollar. That is the recipe for the list I am about to give you.

Number twelve. The pointy, high gain, twenty four fret shred machine from the budget lines. You know the type. Flamed maple veneer top that looks like a tiger exploded, active pickups, Floyd style trem, a neck that feels like a credit card. In twenty twenty one these were going for six, seven hundred dollars used. Today? Staff at more than one store told me they are struggling to move them at three fifty. And here is the honest reason. The kids who bought them to learn metal riffs on YouTube discovered that a locking trem is a nightmare to restring, and a lot of those guitars have been sitting in a cold basement with the trem floating at some crazy angle. Buyers walk in, see the back of the guitar sticking up like a drawbridge, and walk out. If you own one, my advice is simple. Get it set up properly before you list it. A forty dollar setup can be the difference between it sitting for three months and it selling in a week.

Number eleven. Signature models of players who are no longer in the spotlight. I am not going to name names, because I do not want to dunk on anyone, but you know exactly the type of guitar I mean. A custom graphic, a signature on the headstock, a specific pickup combo that only made sense for that one player's sound. When that artist was touring and all over the internet, those guitars were hot. Now? A shop owner in Winnipeg told me, and I am paraphrasing, that signature guitars are like hockey jerseys. Great when the guy is scoring, very hard to sell when he has been traded. The lesson here is that the more specific a guitar is, the smaller the pool of buyers, and in a slow market the small pool dries up first.

Number ten. The fancy smart guitars and the built in modeling guitars. A few years ago, there was a wave of guitars with apps, Bluetooth, built in effects, lights in the fretboard, the whole thing. They looked like the future. The problem is technology moves fast, and a guitar is supposed to last thirty years. When the app stops getting updates or the battery compartment starts acting up, you are left with a mid quality guitar that has a hole cut in it for electronics nobody wants. Used prices on these have, according to listings I tracked, dropped by sixty to seventy percent from original retail. And honestly, I am not surprised. A guitar is wood and wire. The minute you make it a gadget, you give it an expiry date.

Number nine. Seven and eight string guitars. Now, before the djent crowd comes at me in the comments, I love an extended range guitar. But the numbers are the numbers. These things sat on used walls even in the good times, and right now they are just piling up. The market for them in Canada is tiny compared to the States, and the people who really want one usually want a very specific scale length and pickup. So a generic seven string from a budget line sits there. One staff member in Calgary told me they have had the same eight string on the wall for, and I quote, "longer than I have had my car." If you want an extended range guitar, this is honestly a fantastic time to be a buyer. If you are trying to sell one, be patient, and price it like you mean it.

Number eight. Mid priced semi hollow bodies from the big Asian factories. Here is where it gets a bit painful. These are good guitars. The build quality on a lot of the Indonesian and Chinese semi hollows coming out in the last five years is honestly impressive. But there are just so many of them. Everybody bought one to play blues and jazz standards during lockdown, and now everybody is selling one. Dealers say they can only move these if they are under about five hundred Canadian, and plenty of sellers are still asking seven or eight. And there is a Canadian twist here. A lot of people store these in a closet next to an exterior wall, and over one or two prairie winters, the binding starts to shrink and lift, the finish gets little checks, and the neck moves. Buyers in this country are getting smart about that. They look closely at the binding now. If it is pulling away, the offer drops by a hundred bucks on the spot.

Number seven. Boutique pedalboard guitars, by which I mean the heavily modded, partscaster style builds. Somebody buys a body from one place, a neck from another, a set of hand wound pickups from a guy on a forum, and puts it all together with a lot of love. To the builder it is worth fifteen hundred dollars. To the market it is worth, on a good day, the price of the neck. This is the part nobody wants to say out loud. A partscaster has no brand resale value. A shop cannot easily appraise it, and a buyer cannot easily trust it. I have seen absolutely beautiful builds in a Toronto consignment room that went from twelve hundred to six fifty over the summer and are still sitting there. If you love building guitars, keep building, it is one of the best parts of the hobby. Just do not build them as an investment.

Number six. Expensive acoustic electrics with fancy onboard preamps from the late two thousand tens. Specifically the ones with the little tuner and EQ slider panel cut into the side of the body. Those preamps age badly, and when one stops working, the replacement is often discontinued. So a buyer sees a two thousand dollar acoustic with a dead preamp, and they mentally knock off four hundred dollars before they even play it. And again, Canada makes this worse. Our winters are brutal on acoustics. Indoor humidity in a heated house in January can drop down to fifteen, twenty percent. That cracks tops, raises frets, and lifts bridges. Anyone who has bought a used acoustic in this country more than once knows to check for that. So the acoustics that sit the longest are the ones that clearly lived without a humidifier. Do yourself a favour this winter. A twenty dollar soundhole humidifier will save you hundreds when you sell.

Number five. Premium Les Paul style guitars from the pandemic years at full sticker. And I want to be careful here, because the guitars themselves are not bad, and plenty of people love them. The problem is the price people paid. When you bought something for, let us say, thirty five hundred Canadian in twenty twenty one, and the same model new is now on sale for similar money or less, you are not going to get your money back. But a lot of sellers are anchored to what they paid. So they list it at three thousand, nobody calls, they drop it to twenty eight hundred, nobody calls. Meanwhile, a buyer can walk into a store and get a brand new one with a warranty and a case. Shop staff told me this is the single most common conversation they have at the trade in counter. "But I paid this much for it." And the answer, as gently as they can say it, is that the market does not care what you paid. It only cares what the next person will pay.

Number four. Tube combo amps. Okay, I know, technically not a guitar, but hear me out, because this is one of the clearest signs of the whole market shift. Fifty and hundred watt tube combos are rotting on used shelves all over this country. Every shop I asked said the same thing. People are moving to modelers and small heads and plugging straight into the PA. Nobody wants to carry a sixty pound combo up a snowy staircase to a gig in Sudbury in February. And the ones who still want tube tone are buying small five or fifteen watt amps. So the big combos sit. Prices on some classic models have dropped by forty, even fifty percent from their peak. If you are a buyer who still loves the big amp sound, honestly, this might be the best time in twenty years to grab one. Just bring a friend to help you carry it.

Number three. Beginner bundle packs. The guitar, the little practice amp, the gig bag, the strap, the clip on tuner, all in one box. These were everywhere during lockdown. Parents bought them for kids, adults bought them for themselves, and a lot of them got played for about three weeks. Now they are coming back in boxes, and dealers simply do not want them. Some stores will not even take them on trade anymore. On Marketplace I saw dozens of complete packs listed for under a hundred and fifty dollars across the Greater Toronto Area alone, and many of them were listed for over a month. Here is the sad part. A lot of these guitars are perfectly playable with a decent setup. If you know a kid who wants to learn, this is a goldmine. Grab one for a hundred bucks, spend fifty on a setup, and you have a genuinely good starter.

Number two. And this one hurts a lot of people. Limited edition and relic finish guitars. During the boom, every brand released colour drops and limited runs, and people bought them like sneakers. The idea was that a limited edition would hold its value or even go up. In a hot market, some of them did. But when the market cooled, it turned out that "limited" often meant a few thousand units worldwide, and that a burgundy mist sparkle finish is a very personal taste. Relic finishes are even trickier, because to some buyers a worn down finish is art, and to others it just looks like a beat up guitar that someone is charging extra for. Dealers say relic and limited colour guitars now take noticeably longer to sell than standard finishes of the same model. A sunburst sells. A seafoam relic with a matching headstock? It waits for the one buyer who loves it, and in a slow market, that buyer might be in another province.

And now, number one. The guitar that is rotting on more Canadian walls than anything else right now. And it is not a weird gadget, it is not a seven string, it is not a pricey signature model. It is the mid range, Canadian made or Canadian designed, laminate top acoustic that every second household in this country seems to own. Yes, I am talking about the everyday, solid workhorse acoustics many of us grew up with. Guitars from the big Quebec factories that built a massive reputation for value.

Now hold on, this is not a knock on those guitars. Quite the opposite. I would argue some of the best value acoustics in the world have come out of Quebec, and I own one myself. That is exactly the problem. They are so good, so durable, and so widely sold, that literally hundreds of thousands of them are out there. Every cottage has one. Every basement has one. Every uncle has one with a capo still clamped on the headstock. When the market slowed down and people started cleaning out closets, guess which guitar showed up on Kijiji in massive numbers? That one.

I counted listings in a single week across three big cities, Toronto, Montreal and Vancouver, and these Quebec built acoustics outnumbered every other single model by a wide margin. And because there are so many of them, buyers have total power. They can pick the one with the cleanest top, the lowest action, the best case, and they can offer two hundred dollars below asking and still find someone who will say yes. Dealers told me they are getting offered these on trade almost every day, and many simply cannot take more of them. The wall is full.

There is also a subtle thing going on. Because these guitars are built so tough, many have survived decades of Canadian winters without a crack. But they have often survived without much care either. Old strings, high action, a neck that needs a truss rod adjustment, a saddle that has been shimmed. So a lot of the ones listed look tired. And a tired guitar in a crowded market just sits.

So what does that mean for you? If you are a buyer, honestly, this is incredible news. You can probably get a very good Canadian made acoustic for between two hundred and four hundred dollars right now, which is less than many entry level imports cost new. Spend another sixty to eighty on a proper setup and fresh strings, and you have a guitar that will outlast your car. And if you are a seller, here is what I would do. Clean it. Change the strings. If the action is high, get it adjusted. Take photos in good daylight, not under a basement bulb. Mention that it has been stored with humidity control if it has. And price it realistically, not at what it was worth in twenty twenty one. The ones that are presented properly still sell. It is the dusty ones with blurry photos that sit there until spring.

Let me zoom out for a second, because I think this whole list tells a bigger story. The Canadian guitar market is not collapsing. People are still playing. Lessons are still happening, and open mics are still packed on a Thursday night. What is happening is a correction. For two years, everybody bought everything, and prices went up on things that maybe did not deserve it. Now the market is sorting the gear into two piles. The pile people actually want, which is versatile, well set up, fairly priced guitars. And the pile people bought on impulse, which is now sitting on walls with orange stickers on it.

And if you are a buyer this winter, here is my honest take. This is one of the best moments in the last decade to buy used gear in Canada. Go into the store, be polite, and make a reasonable offer. Staff are often more flexible than the price tag suggests, especially on things that have been on the wall for a few months. Ask how long it has been there. That question alone tells you a lot. And always, always check the binding, the frets, and the neck relief, especially on anything that has spent a winter in a cold house.

And if you are a seller, the rules are simple. Presentation matters more than ever. Price matters more than ever. Patience matters more than ever. A guitar that is clean, set up, and priced to the current market will still find a home. One that is priced off a twenty twenty one receipt will not.

Let me also give you a quick buyer checklist for winter, because this is when a lot of people shop. When you pick up a used guitar that has just come in from a cold car or a cold store room, do not open the case right away if you are bringing it home. Let it sit closed for an hour or so to warm up slowly, otherwise you risk finish checking. Sight down the neck from the headstock. Press the strings at the first and last fret and look at the gap in the middle. Run your finger along the fret ends, because in a dry Canadian winter the fretboard shrinks and the fret ends start sticking out like little needles. And plug it in and wiggle every knob and the output jack. Crackly pots are cheap to fix, but they are a great reason to ask for twenty or thirty dollars off.

One last little story before I wrap up. A guy I know in Kingston had a beautiful limited run electric he paid a small fortune for. Listed it for nearly what he paid. Sat for five months. He finally dropped it by seven hundred bucks in October, and it sold in two days, to a sixteen year old who had been saving up from his job at a grocery store and drove in with his dad. He told me afterward that watching that kid plug it in made him feel better than the money ever would have. And I think there is something in that. The guitars on these walls are not dead. They are just waiting for the right person at the right price.
