Something is happening in Canadian guitar stores right now, and most people outside the industry have no idea how bad it has gotten. A guitar that cost you nine hundred dollars in twenty nineteen can easily cost you fourteen hundred today. Strings that were eight bucks a pack are pushing fourteen. A tube amp that was a stretch purchase five years ago is now basically a car payment. And the people feeling it the most are not the collectors with ten guitars on the wall. It is the kid saving up for a first electric, the parent paying for lessons, and the weekend player who just wants to replace a cracked acoustic before the next jam.

Canadians, in big numbers, cannot afford guitars anymore. And today I am going to break down exactly why, the seven forces squeezing your wallet, from the loonie to tariffs to shipping to something about our winters that almost nobody talks about. And stick around to the very end, because the last part is not doom and gloom. I am going to show you how real Canadian players are still getting great gear without going broke, and the number one move is so simple you will wonder why nobody told you before.

Let me start with a little story. Last month, I was in a big music store in Ottawa, the kind with the long wall of electrics and the acoustic room with the humidifier humming. There was a dad there with his daughter, maybe twelve years old. She had picked out a basic Mexican made electric. Nothing crazy. The dad looks at the tag, does the math with tax in his head, and you could just see his face drop. They ended up leaving with a used import and a smaller amp. And she was happy, which is great, but he said something to the staff on the way out that stuck with me. He said, "I bought basically this same guitar for myself in high school with a summer job." And I thought, yeah. That is the whole story right there. The gap between what a guitar costs and what a regular Canadian earns has gotten wider, and it is getting uglier.

So let us get into why. Force number seven. The loonie. This is the one everyone sort of knows, but few people understand how much it matters. Almost every guitar sold in Canada is priced, at some point in its life, in US dollars. Even guitars built in Asia or Mexico usually get bought by distributors in US dollars. So when our dollar sits around seventy, seventy three cents US, every guitar effectively costs roughly thirty five to forty percent more before anyone adds a single cent of margin. Back around twenty twelve and twenty thirteen, the loonie was at or near par. Players who remember cross border shopping in Buffalo or Bellingham remember how good that was. Today, a guitar listed at a thousand US can land in a Canadian store north of fourteen hundred Canadian, and that is before tax. In Ontario with thirteen percent HST, or the Maritimes at fifteen, you are suddenly looking at sixteen hundred plus for something that sounds cheap on an American YouTube review.

Force number six. Tariffs and trade uncertainty. I am not going to get political here, I promise. But you cannot talk about gear prices in twenty twenty six without talking about the back and forth on trade between Canada and the US over the last couple of years. Distributors have told reporters and retailers that the uncertainty alone makes them price conservatively. When you do not know whether a shipment will have an extra duty on it next quarter, you build a buffer into the price. And some of that buffer stays even when things calm down. Add the fact that a lot of parts, from pickups to tuners to electronics, cross borders multiple times before they end up in a finished guitar, and you can see how small charges stack up. The end result for you is that the price tag creeps up, and it rarely creeps back down.

Force number five. Shipping and the geography of Canada. This one is uniquely Canadian, and it drives me a little nuts. We are a huge country with a relatively small population spread along a thin line near the border. Getting a guitar from a warehouse in Ontario to a store in Whitehorse, or Gander, or Prince Rupert costs real money. Freight rates spiked after twenty twenty and never fully came back down. Fuel surcharges come and go. And because guitars are bulky, fragile, and need to be shipped in big boxes, they cost more to move per dollar of value than a lot of other products. Players in remote communities feel this the most. A friend of mine in northern BC told me that, between shipping and a long drive to a store, a new guitar for him ends up costing a couple hundred dollars more than for someone in Toronto, just because of where he lives.

Force number four. The pandemic boom hangover. Remember twenty twenty and twenty twenty one? Everyone stuck inside, everyone suddenly learning guitar. Demand went through the roof. Brands raised prices, and here is the thing, prices are sticky. Once a guitar goes from eight ninety nine to eleven ninety nine, it very rarely goes back to eight ninety nine. Instead, brands run sales, bundle deals, holiday specials. But the base price stays. So what you got after the boom was a new floor for prices, and that floor never came back down even though demand cooled off. The other side of this is that the used market got flooded, which is honestly good news for buyers, and I will come back to that at the end.

Force number three. Everything else got more expensive at the same time. This sounds obvious, but it is the thing that turns a price problem into an affordability crisis. It is not just that guitars got pricier. It is that rent, groceries, gas, home heating, car insurance, all of it went up in the same few years. When you are spending way more on groceries than you did five years ago and your rent jumped by several hundred a month, a fourteen hundred dollar guitar does not feel like a treat. It feels impossible. Music retailers I have read about have said that the middle of the market is where it hurts the most. People either buy the very cheapest option or, if they have money, they buy the high end. The thousand to two thousand dollar range, where a lot of great working guitars sit, has gotten squeezed from both sides.

Force number two. Hidden costs that nobody mentions in the store. And this is where Canadian winter comes in. When you buy a guitar in this country, especially an acoustic, the guitar is not the final cost. You need a humidifier, and if you have a few guitars you might want a room humidifier. You need a proper hard case if you are going to carry it through minus twenty five in January, because a gig bag will not save a solid top from a temperature shock. You need a seasonal setup, because the neck will move between a humid July in Toronto and a bone dry February. Then you have strings, which, if you play a lot, can be a hundred and fifty to two hundred dollars a year. Add lessons if you are a beginner, which in a lot of cities are now forty to sixty dollars for a half hour. Suddenly the real cost of getting into guitar in Canada is not the price on the tag. It is the tag plus several hundred dollars in the first year. And a lot of people find that out after they have already spent everything on the guitar.

And now, force number one. The one I think nobody really talks about. The death of the cheap, good, domestic option. For decades, Canadians had a secret weapon. We had guitars designed and built right here, especially in Quebec, that gave you incredible value for the money. Solid tops, great necks, simple and tough designs that could survive our climate. For a lot of us, that was our first real acoustic. And at one point you could walk into a store and buy a solid top Canadian built acoustic for a price that was genuinely affordable for a student. Today, those same kinds of guitars, and I am talking about the category, not attacking any one brand, have climbed in price along with everything else. Labour costs went up, wood costs went up, and the price for a new solid top Canadian made acoustic has crept well past where a lot of first time buyers can go. That means the bottom of the market is now mostly imports. Which can be fine, a lot of imports are very good now, but it means the easy, obvious, proudly Canadian first guitar is harder to reach than it used to be. And for a lot of players, that stings.

Before I get to the fixes, let me put some rough numbers on all of this, because I think seeing it laid out side by side really drives it home. And keep in mind, these are approximate ranges based on listings and what players and store staff have shared, not official figures.

Take a typical beginner setup in twenty nineteen. A decent entry level electric, maybe two fifty to three hundred Canadian. A small practice amp, around a hundred and fifty. Cable, strap, tuner, picks, maybe fifty. Call it five hundred dollars all in, plus tax. Now take roughly the same setup today. The guitar is three fifty to four hundred. The amp is closer to two hundred. The accessories are seventy or eighty. You are at six fifty to seven hundred before tax, and with HST in Ontario you are pushing eight hundred. That is a jump of more than fifty percent for a parent trying to get a kid started.

Now move up a level. The classic step up guitar, the one a lot of us bought after a year or two of playing, used to sit around eight or nine hundred dollars. Today the equivalent is often twelve to fifteen hundred. And the pro level stuff, the American made and Japanese made guitars? Those that were sixteen to eighteen hundred a few years ago now commonly sit north of twenty five hundred, sometimes three thousand. Meanwhile, the median paycheque did not go up fifty percent. Not even close.

And here is something that really surprised me when I started digging. Accessories went up faster, in percentage terms, than the guitars themselves. A decent overdrive pedal that was a hundred and twenty is now a hundred and seventy or more. A good hard case can be two hundred. Even a basic gig bag that was thirty is now fifty. Individually, none of these seem huge. Together, they add up to a real barrier.

Let me tell you about a guy I met at a jam night in Halifax. Late forties, played in bands through his twenties, stopped when the kids came along. He decided this year was the year he would get back into it. He walked into a store expecting to spend maybe a thousand bucks on a guitar and an amp, the way he did back in the day. He told me he walked out with nothing, sat in his truck, and just laughed. Not because he was angry, but because he could not believe how far things had moved while he was not paying attention. He ended up buying a used guitar from a retired music teacher down the road, and an old solid state amp from a garage sale. Total cost, under four hundred. And you know what? He sounds great. But his story is a perfect example of the sticker shock a lot of returning players in this country are going through right now.

There is also a regional angle that I think deserves a minute. In big cities like Toronto, Vancouver, and Montreal, you have competition. Multiple stores, a busy used market, lots of players selling gear. That keeps prices somewhat honest. But in smaller towns, there might be one music store, or none at all. Buyers there are stuck either paying whatever the local store charges, driving three hours, or ordering online and paying for shipping and hoping the guitar arrives without a cracked headstock in the middle of January. In some parts of the country, the cost of getting a guitar is not just the guitar. It is the gas, the day off work, the motel. That is a real thing, and I think it gets ignored by a lot of the big gear channels that are based south of the border.

And finally, there is the emotional side. When gear gets this expensive, people stop experimenting. They stop trying that weird fuzz pedal or that baritone guitar, because a mistake is too costly. And honestly, that is part of what makes guitar fun, trying things, being surprised. So the affordability crunch does not just hurt wallets. It can make the whole hobby a little more cautious, a little more boring. That, to me, is one of the ugliest parts of all of this.

So is that the whole story? Is guitar becoming a rich person's hobby in this country? Honestly, no. And here is where I want to flip this around, because every single one of those seven forces has a workaround, and real Canadian players are using them right now.

First, buy used, but buy used smart. The pandemic boom that pushed prices up also filled every closet in Canada with guitars that are now coming out. The used market, from Kijiji to Marketplace to the used rooms in big chain stores, is flooded. Prices on a lot of used electrics are down twenty, thirty, sometimes forty percent from where they were a few years ago. The trick is to check three things every time. Look down the neck for twists. Look at the binding and the top for cracks or lifting, which is the classic sign a guitar spent a winter in a dry house. And check the frets for wear. If those three are fine, you probably have a good guitar.

Second, buy in the off season and buy from the floor. In Canada, a lot of people sell gear in late winter and early spring, after the holidays and when tax time comes around. That is when prices soften. And in stores, ask about floor models, open box, and B stock. Staff will often knock real money off a guitar that has been hanging on the wall for a while, especially if you ask nicely and you are ready to buy today.

Third, rent to own. Many Canadian music stores offer rental programs, especially for students. A lot of people do not know that these rental payments can often go toward buying the instrument. For a parent who is not sure if their kid will stick with it, this is a fantastic option. If the kid quits after three months, you are out a few rental payments instead of a thousand dollars.

Fourth, the hidden cost fix. Do your own basic maintenance. Learn to change strings properly, learn to clean your fretboard, and learn to keep your humidity in check. A twenty dollar soundhole humidifier and a cheap hygrometer will save you a cracked top that costs three hundred dollars to repair. Buy strings in bulk packs, which can easily cut your per set price in half. And if you are in a big city, look for community music programs and group lessons, which can cost a fraction of private lessons.

And here is a bonus tip that a lot of Canadians overlook. Check your local library and community centres. Some public libraries in this country now lend out instruments, including guitars and ukuleles, the same way they lend books. A few school boards and community music programs run instrument banks for families who cannot afford to buy. If you are not sure whether guitar is for you, or for your kid, borrowing one for a few weeks is the ultimate zero risk test. And if you have an old guitar sitting in a closet that you know you will never play again, think about donating it to one of those programs. That guitar could be the reason someone in your town starts playing music for the rest of their life. Honestly, in a market this tight, that kind of thing matters more than ever.

And fifth, and this is my number one move, the one I promised you at the start. Stop shopping for the guitar you want, and start shopping for the setup you need. Here is what I mean. A three hundred dollar used guitar with a professional setup, fresh strings, and properly cut nut will feel and play better than a nine hundred dollar new guitar straight out of the box with high action. I have seen it a hundred times. Players spend all their money on the instrument and nothing on making it play well, so they end up frustrated and thinking they need a more expensive guitar. They do not. They need a sixty to eighty dollar setup from a good tech. Find a local tech you trust, which, by the way, is one of the most valuable relationships a guitar player in this country can have, and take every guitar you buy straight to them. That single move turns a budget guitar into a great guitar. And in an economy where every dollar counts, it is the smartest money you will ever spend on this hobby.

Let me end with that dad and his daughter from the Ottawa store. I actually bumped into them again a few weeks later, in the same store, picking up strings. The dad told me they had taken that used import to a tech, it cost them seventy bucks, and now she will not put it down. She plays every day after school. He said, "honestly, I think she likes it more than the one we couldn't afford." And that is the point. Guitars got more expensive in Canada. That is real, and it is frustrating, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. But the joy of playing did not get more expensive. You just have to be a little smarter about how you get there.
