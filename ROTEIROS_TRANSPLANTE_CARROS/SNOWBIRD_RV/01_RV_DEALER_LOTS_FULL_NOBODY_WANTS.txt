Number ten. Big luxury Class A diesel pushers.

The problem is, the people who buy these are a pretty small group, and a lot of them already bought during the boom. Now with interest rates where they are, financing a half-million-dollar motorhome over twenty years is a tough conversation. Dealers say these are sitting longer than almost anything else on the lot, and some are being discounted heavily, we're talking six figures off sticker in some cases, according to buyers on the RV forums.

And here's what really hurts. A diesel pusher can lose a huge chunk of its value in the first few years. If you buy one new, you're the person paying for that drop. If you buy one three or four years old, you're the person who benefits from it. Just keep that in mind.

Number nine. Mid-size gas Class A motorhomes from the boom years.

These are the thirty-two to thirty-six foot gas rigs that sold like hotcakes in twenty-one and twenty-two. They're not bad rigs, not at all. But they're showing up in big numbers as trade-ins and consignments, because a lot of folks who bought one during the pandemic realized they don't actually want to drive a bus down a mountain pass.

Number eight. Super C motorhomes.

These are the big brutes built on a heavy-duty truck chassis, often a Freightliner. They can tow a car, they can tow a boat, they're built like a tank. They're also expensive, often well over three hundred thousand new, and they sit in this awkward middle ground. Too pricey for the folks shopping regular Class Cs, too truck-like for the folks who wanted a luxury Class A.

Dealers tell me these are sitting. And when they do sell, it's usually after a serious price cut. If you're a full-timer who tows heavy, a lightly used Super C right now could be one of the better buys out there. But buying new off the lot at full price? I'd think hard about that.

Number seven. Standard Class C motorhomes.

Now the rental market cooled off, and those fleet units are flooding the used market with high mileage and, let's be polite, some wear and tear. That drags everybody's price down. Number five. Bunkhouse travel trailers.

These are the family trailers. Two sets of bunks in the back, sometimes three, made for mom and dad and three or four kids. During the boom, manufacturers built these by the thousands, because families were buying them as fast as they rolled off the line.

Now families are pulling back hard. Summer camping trips got expensive. Campground prices went up, gas went up, groceries went up. And a lot of these bunkhouse trailers are being traded back in after just a few seasons. Dealers say the lots are full of them. New ones, used ones, and everything in between.

For a retired couple, a bunkhouse might seem like a strange buy. But think about it. Bunks make fantastic storage. I know a couple who took the bunks out of theirs, put in a desk and shelving, and now it's his workshop and her sewing room. They paid way below what that trailer cost new two years ago. Sometimes the thing nobody wants is exactly what you need.

Number four. Big luxury fifth wheels.

These are the forty-foot, four-hundred-square-foot palaces that full-time snowbirds love. Fireplace, theater seating, kitchen island, king bed. They're beautiful. They're also heavy, they need a serious one-ton dually truck to pull them, and that truck alone can run you seventy or eighty thousand dollars.

So you've got buyers doing the math. A hundred-twenty-thousand-dollar fifth wheel, plus an eighty-thousand-dollar truck, plus insurance, plus fuel at eight or nine miles to the gallon. Suddenly that's a two-hundred-thousand-dollar lifestyle. And a lot of retirees are saying, you know what, maybe not.

Black Book has reported fifth wheel values continuing to soften this year, and dealers say the high-end units are the slowest of the bunch. If you already have the truck, though, you are in a very strong spot to negotiate right now. Walk in knowing that the dealer is probably paying interest on that rig every single month it sits there.

Number three. Ultra-light travel trailers with fancy features.

This one's a little sneaky. Ultra-lights were marketed as the answer for people who didn't want a giant truck. Tow it with your SUV, the ads said. And a lot of them were built fast and light during the boom, with a pile of features crammed in to make the brochure look good. Electric awnings, outdoor kitchens, solar packages.

Reports from owners and inspectors suggest some of these boom-era units haven't aged as gracefully as buyers hoped. Lighter construction can mean more water intrusion issues, more sagging, more little things breaking. I'm not naming names here, because it really varies by unit and by how it was cared for. But dealers say buyers have gotten more cautious, and the used ones from twenty-one and twenty-two are sitting longer and selling for less.

My advice? If you're looking at one of these, get an independent RV inspection. It costs a few hundred dollars and it can save you from buying a rolling sponge. I learned that one the hard way years ago, and my wallet still remembers.

The day the new model year arrives, last year's unit becomes "old" in the eyes of every buyer and every lender. It's the exact same trailer. Same floor plan, same fridge, same everything. But it's suddenly worth less on paper, and the dealer has been paying floor plan interest on it for twelve, eighteen, sometimes twenty-four months.

Let me give you a few more quick tips before we wrap up, because I don't want you leaving here empty-handed.

First, never negotiate the monthly payment. Negotiate the out-the-door price. Dealers love to talk about the payment because they can stretch a loan to twenty years and make almost any price look small. Twenty years. Think about how old you'll be when that's paid off. Second, watch out for add-ons. Paint protection, fabric protection, extended warranties, nitrogen in the tires. Some of those have value, plenty don't, and they all add up fast. Ask for each one to be taken off, and see what happens to the price.

Third, if you're trading in, get a separate number for your trade. Don't let them blend it all together. And honestly, with what's happening to used values, you may do better selling privately, especially if you've kept your rig clean and you've got the maintenance records.

Fourth, think about timing. Late fall and early winter, once the northern dealers start winterizing, can be a great time to buy. Nobody up north is shopping in a snowstorm, and the dealers know it.

And fifth, and this is the big one, don't buy more rig than you need. I've met so many retirees who bought the biggest thing they could afford, and then spent every trip worried about backing it in, finding a site long enough, or paying for fuel.
