Number seven. The older, well-kept small fiberglass trailer.

You've seen these. Little egg-shaped trailers, thirteen to nineteen feet, molded fiberglass shells. They've been built in small factories for decades, and the reason people love them is simple. There aren't many seams for water to get into. And water, as any RVer will tell you, is the number one killer of trailers.

Now, here's the funny part. These little trailers actually hold their value very well. So they're not always dirt cheap. But the older ones, from the two thousands or even the nineties, can often be found for a lot less than a new stick-and-tin trailer, and they'll outlast it. I met a couple in Florida who've been pulling the same seventeen-foot fiberglass trailer for nineteen years. Nineteen years! Still no leaks. He told me, "We've had three tow vehicles and one trailer."

Why do people ignore them? They're small. If you want a walk-around bed and a big sofa, this isn't for you. But for a couple who spends most of their time outside, and let's be honest, that's why we go south in the first place, these little trailers are absolute gems.

Number six. The mid-two-thousands Class C on a Ford chassis.

This is the old reliable. Twenty-four to thirty feet, cab-over bunk, Ford E-series chassis, usually with the big V-ten gas engine. Not the fastest thing on the road, not the best on fuel. But here's what matters: almost every mechanic in America knows how to work on that chassis. Parts are everywhere. You break down in a small town in New Mexico, somebody at the local garage can probably help you.

And prices on these older Class Cs have come way down, especially now that the rental fleets are dumping newer units and pushing everything else lower. Depending on condition, you might find a nice one from the mid-two-thousands for somewhere in the high teens to the twenties, sometimes less.

What to watch for? The cab-over bunk. That's where water likes to sneak in on older Class Cs. Push on the walls up there. Look at the ceiling corners. If you see staining or soft spots, walk away, or at least knock a lot off the price. But a dry one with a good maintenance history? That's a rig that can carry you south for many more winters.

Number five. The basic single-slide travel trailer from the twenty-tens.

This one is about as unglamorous as it gets. A twenty-two to twenty-six foot travel trailer with one slide, a queen bed, a dinette, a small bathroom. No outdoor kitchen. No fireplace. No theater seats. And that's exactly why I like it.

One slide means one slide mechanism, one set of seals, one thing to worry about. These trailers can be pulled by a lot of half-ton pickups and some larger SUVs, so you may not need a big new truck. And the used market is flooded with them right now, because so many were bought during the boom and traded in.

Dealers and private sellers are hungry to move them. I've seen campground bulletin boards in Arizona with three or four of these listed at once, all from folks who decided to upgrade or hang it up. If you're patient and you've got an inspector you trust, you can often get one for well under what the owner paid.

Here's a little tip. Look for one that's been stored under a roof. Ask where it lived in the off-season. A trailer stored under a carport or in a barn for ten years is a completely different animal from one that sat in a backyard under the sun and snow.

Okay. Number four. The older gas Class A, about thirty feet, with no slides or just one.

Now I know what you're thinking. Wait, didn't you say Class As are piling up on lots? I did. But that's mostly the big, new, expensive ones. The older, shorter gas Class As are a different story, and they've become some of the cheapest motorhomes per square foot you can buy.

What to watch out for: tires and roofs. Check the date codes on the tires, those little four-digit numbers on the sidewall. And get up on the roof, or have an inspector do it. Roof sealant needs regular care, and an ignored roof is an expensive roof.

Number three. The truck camper.

This is one almost nobody in the snowbird world talks about. A truck camper slides right into the bed of a pickup. No towing, no hitching, no backing a trailer into a tight site. You just drive in, and you're home.

People ignore them because they're small and they need the right truck. But think about it for a second. If you already own a three-quarter or one-ton pickup, a used truck camper might be the cheapest way into the RV life there is. Older hard-side campers can be found for a few thousand dollars up to the low teens, depending on age and features.

And here's the bonus. When you get to your winter spot, you can take the camper off on its jacks and use your truck like a truck. Run to the store, go to the beach, go see the grandkids. No towing a little car behind you.

I spent a week once with a retired couple in a truck camper on a beach in South Texas. They had more fun than anybody else on that beach, and they'd spent less on their rig than most people had spent on their awnings. I'm only half joking.

Number two. The mid-size fifth wheel from the early twenty-tens.

Now this might surprise you, because I just told folks in another video that big luxury fifth wheels are sitting on lots. That's true. But the mid-size, older fifth wheels, around twenty-eight to thirty-two feet, built around ten to fifteen years ago? Those have become a quiet bargain.

Here's why. Fifth wheels tow more steadily than a lot of travel trailers, because the hitch sits over the rear axle of the truck. Many of these mid-size ones can be towed with a three-quarter-ton truck, no dually needed. They've got a real bedroom up front, decent storage, and often a residential feel without all the fancy gadgets.

And because the market is so focused on new and big right now, these older mid-size units are priced to move. Depending on condition and floor plan, a lot of them are selling for a small fraction of their original sticker.

The catch? Same as always. Water damage and slide mechanisms. Look under the front cap, check the floor near the slides, and work every slide in and out a couple of times. If the seller won't let you do that, that tells you something.

Number one. The low-mileage older motorhome that sat too long.

You'll see these at dealers and in classified ads all over the country. A motorhome, maybe fifteen or twenty years old, with ridiculously low miles. Twenty thousand, thirty thousand miles. The owners bought it, used it a few times, then life happened. Health issues, a spouse passed, the kids talked them out of traveling. And the rig just sat in the driveway.

Most buyers see that and get scared, and you know what, some of that fear is fair. Sitting is hard on a motorhome. Tires dry rot. Seals dry out. Generators gum up. Batteries die. Mice move in. So the market treats these rigs like they're worth very little, and the sellers, often the family settling an estate, just want them gone.

But here's why it's number one. The expensive part of a motorhome, the engine, the transmission, the chassis, may barely be broken in. The things that go bad from sitting, tires, batteries, fluids, seals, are usually the cheaper and more fixable stuff. If you budget a few thousand dollars to bring it back up to snuff, you can end up with a motorhome that drives like a much younger rig for a fraction of the price.

I'll tell you my story, since I promised. Years ago I bought a motorhome exactly like this. Owner's widow was selling it. Twenty-two thousand miles on a twelve-year-old coach. I paid about a third of what comparable coaches with normal miles were going for. I spent around four thousand on tires, batteries, a generator service, a full fluid change, and new roof sealant. And that coach carried us back and forth to Arizona for six winters without a single major problem. The worst deal I ever made, by the way, was the one before that, when I bought a shiny rig with a pretty interior and didn't check the roof. But that's a story for another day.

Here's your checklist for one of these sit-too-long rigs. First, check the tire date codes, and plan on new tires no matter what. Second, ask to start the generator and run it under load, air conditioner on, for at least half an hour. Third, look for signs of rodents, droppings in the cabinets, chewed wires in the basement compartments. Fourth, have a mechanic look at the brakes and the fluids, especially brake fluid and coolant. And fifth, always, always, get a professional RV inspection. A few hundred dollars up front can save you thousands.

And be kind with the family if it's an estate sale. These folks are often going through something hard. A fair offer, delivered with some respect, goes a long way. More than once I've seen a family take a lower offer from somebody who promised to take good care of grandpa's motorhome.

Now, I want to take a minute to talk about where you actually find these deals, because that's half the battle.

The first place most people look is the big dealership. And that's fine, dealers have inventory, they offer financing, and some of them do a good job inspecting their used units. But the cheapest rigs on this list often aren't sitting on the big lots at all. Dealers don't love taking in twenty-year-old motorhomes or old truck campers on trade, and when they do, they often send them straight to wholesale auction.

So where do you look? Start with the campground itself. Seriously. Walk the bulletin board at the clubhouse. Ask the camp host. In snowbird parks across Florida, Arizona and Texas, there's a steady stream of folks who've decided this will be their last season. Maybe their health changed, maybe they're moving closer to the kids. They'd much rather sell to somebody they've shared a potluck with than deal with strangers online.

Second, look at the online classifieds, but look smart. Search a little wider than your own town. Prices for the exact same rig can vary by thousands of dollars from one state to the next. Rigs in the northern states often get cheaper in late fall, once owners realize they're about to pay another winter of storage. A short drive, or even a one-way flight and a drive back south, can save you real money.

Third, consider consignment lots. These are smaller dealers who sell rigs on behalf of private owners. The prices aren't always the lowest, but you often get a little more protection than a straight private sale, and the owners are usually motivated.

Fourth, and this one gets overlooked, talk to the RV service shops and mobile techs in your area. Those folks know everybody. They know who's been bringing their rig in for regular maintenance, and they often hear first when somebody's thinking of selling. A well-maintained rig with a known service history is worth its weight in gold.

And whatever you do, be careful with deals that seem too good online. There are scammers out there who post pictures of rigs they don't own, ask for a deposit, and disappear. If the price is way below market and the seller can't meet you in person, can't let you see the rig, or wants you to wire money, walk away. Never pay for an RV you haven't seen with your own eyes or had inspected by somebody you trust.

Let me also say a word about financing, because it matters more with older rigs. A lot of lenders won't finance an RV over a certain age, often somewhere around ten to fifteen years old, and the ones that do may charge higher rates. That's actually one of the reasons these older rigs are so cheap. Fewer buyers can get a loan for them. So if you can buy with cash, or mostly cash, you're competing with a much smaller group of people, and that gives you leverage.

One more thing. Insurance. Before you buy, call your insurance agent and get a quote on the exact year and model. Some older rigs are cheap to insure, some are surprisingly not. It's a five-minute phone call that can keep you from an unpleasant surprise.

And while you're at it, check the campground rules where you plan to spend the winter. Some resorts, especially the fancier ones, have a rule about how old a rig can be before they'll let it in. It's often called the ten-year rule. Many parks will make an exception if the rig is clean and well kept, and they'll ask you to send a photo. But it's a lot better to find that out before you buy than when you're pulling up to the gate in January with nowhere else to go.

Before I let you go, let me give you a few general rules for buying cheap without buying trouble.

Rule one. Water is the enemy. More than engines, more than appliances, more than anything else. Water damage is what turns a bargain into a money pit. Smell the inside when you first walk in. A musty smell is a warning. Press on the walls, the floor, the corners. Soft means trouble.

Rule two. Simple is your friend. Fewer slides, fewer motors, fewer screens. Every gadget is one more thing that can break three hundred miles from the nearest service center. The old-timers at the campground will tell you the same thing.

Rule three. Buy the records, not just the rig. A seller with a folder full of receipts is a seller who took care of things. A seller who says, "Oh, it's always been fine," with nothing to show for it, makes me nervous.

Rule four. Budget for the first year. Whatever you pay, set aside some money for tires, batteries, seals, and the little things you'll find in the first few trips. If you don't need it, great, that's your margarita fund.

And rule five. Buy for the life you actually live, not the life in the brochure. If you spend most of your winter outside, by the pool, at the pickleball courts, on the patio with friends, you don't need a rolling mansion. You need a dry, safe, comfortable place to sleep and cook. Everything else is gravy.
