Walk into almost any guitar shop in Australia right now, from a little family store in Geelong to one of the big chains out near a Brisbane industrial estate, and look up at the wall. Not at the shiny new arrivals near the counter. Look up high, at the back, where the dust settles. Because up there you'll find guitars that have been hanging in the same spot for eight months, ten months, sometimes well over a year. Price tags that have been crossed out twice. "Sale" stickers that have faded in the sun. And here's the thing that surprised me when I started asking around: a lot of these aren't bad guitars. Some of them are genuinely great instruments. They're just guitars that Aussies have quietly decided they don't want anymore.

Quick bit of context first, because it matters. The Australian guitar market has had a strange few years. During the lockdowns, everyone and their cousin bought a guitar. Shops couldn't keep stock on the walls. Prices went up, and then the Aussie dollar wobbled against the US dollar, and then freight costs went through the roof, and then prices went up again. Now we're in 2026, the lockdown beginners have either kept going or given up, the second hand market is absolutely flooded with barely played instruments, and the cost of living has every household looking at what they can live without. A five thousand dollar guitar is a very easy thing to live without when your rent's just gone up another hundred a week.

So shops are caught in the middle. They ordered stock when demand was crazy, they paid premium freight, and now they're trying to sell it into a market where buyers have more choice and less cash than they've had in a decade. That's the backdrop. Let's get into the list.

Number nine. Premium seven and eight string extended range guitars.

Five years ago, extended range guitars were the hot thing. Every metal player wanted a seven string, the djent kids wanted eight strings with fanned frets, and brands pumped out higher end models aimed squarely at that crowd. Some of those models landed in Australia at prices north of three thousand dollars. And look, if you play that style, they're incredible tools. But here's what dealers are telling me: the market for a three and a half thousand dollar eight string in Australia was always small, and it's gotten smaller. The players who wanted one already bought one. The beginners who were curious went for a cheap import. And second hand, these things are brutal. I've seen high end extended range guitars listed on Gumtree and Facebook Marketplace for forty to fifty percent under their original shop price, and they still sit there for weeks.

One shop owner in Perth told me he had a fanned fret eight string on his wall for so long that the regulars started giving it a name. They called it Kevin. Kevin hung there for fourteen months. Eventually he sold it at a price he described as "barely covering what I paid for it". That's the reality for a lot of these guitars right now. Great instruments, tiny audience, and an audience that already owns one.

Number eight. Hybrid acoustic electric "smart" guitars with built in effects.

You've probably seen these. Acoustic guitars with reverb, delay and chorus built right into the body, so you can get that big lush sound without plugging in. When they first came out, people were genuinely excited, and I get it, it's a cool party trick. But they landed in Australia at a significant premium over a normal acoustic of the same quality, often somewhere between a few hundred and well over a thousand dollars extra. And the novelty wore off fast.

The problem dealers keep mentioning is that people play them in the shop, go "wow, that's fun", and then buy the normal version, because they realise they'd be paying hundreds extra for a feature they'd use for a week. Then there's the battery and electronics side. In our climate, especially up north, anything with a lot of onboard electronics in a wooden box that's breathing in tropical humidity makes some buyers nervous. Fair or not, that worry is real at the counter. Second hand, these often go for roughly half of what they cost new, and plenty of sellers are reporting they can't move them even then.

Number seven. Signature model guitars from artists who've drifted out of the spotlight.

This one's a bit sad, honestly. Signature guitars are great when the artist is everywhere. Kids see their hero on stage, they want the exact same guitar, they save up, they buy it. But when that artist goes quiet, or the band breaks up, or their sound just isn't what younger players are chasing anymore, those signature models become very hard to shift. And unlike a standard model, you can't easily convince a buyer to just ignore the name on the headstock.

Dealers in Melbourne and Sydney both told me the same story. They've got signature models, sometimes really well made ones, that they literally can't move unless they knock off a huge chunk. One bloke said he'd rather sell a plain standard model at full price than a signature model at a discount, because the signature model drags. And second hand, unless the artist is a genuine legend, you're often looking at prices thirty to fifty percent below new. The lesson here is simple. If you love the guitar, buy it. But don't buy a signature model thinking it'll hold value, because the market just doesn't work that way unless the name on it is truly iconic.

Right. Number six. Mid priced Japanese and Korean made "upgrade" models.

This one surprises people. For years, the classic advice was simple: skip the cheap Chinese and Indonesian stuff, and save up for the Japanese or Korean made version. That middle tier, sitting roughly between fifteen hundred and three thousand dollars in Australia, was the sweet spot. Better woods, better fretwork, better quality control. And a lot of those guitars really are excellent. But in 2026, they're getting squeezed hard from both directions.

From below, the cheap Asian made guitars have gotten frighteningly good. A guitar that costs five or six hundred dollars today is often better than what you'd have paid twelve hundred for ten years ago. From above, the second hand market is full of American and Australian made guitars at prices that overlap with brand new mid tier ones. So a buyer walks in with two grand, looks at the new Japanese model on the wall, then pulls out their phone and finds a used American one for the same money. Guess which one they're going home with.

A guitar tech I spoke to in Adelaide put it bluntly. He said, "The middle of the market is where guitars go to die right now." That's harsh, but the numbers back him up. Dealers say these models are sitting longer than they used to, and they're being discounted more often to clear space. If you're buying, that's actually good news for you, and I'll come back to it at the end.

Number five. Boutique style pedalboard friendly "modeller ready" guitars with fancy switching.

So this is a niche that exploded a few years back. Guitars with coil splits, series parallel switching, piezo pickups, push pull pots, a toggle for this, a mini switch for that. The pitch was one guitar that does everything. And on paper, it makes total sense, especially for a working musician playing covers at the local RSL who needs Strat sounds, Les Paul sounds and acoustic sounds all in one night.

But here's what actually happened. Players found that one guitar trying to do everything usually ends up doing everything at about seventy percent. And the more complicated the wiring, the more things there are to go wrong. In humid parts of the country, scratchy pots and dodgy switches are already a common complaint. Add five extra switches and you've got five extra problems. Techs tell me they see these guitars come in for electronics work far more often than a simple two knob, one switch guitar.

So buyers have gone back to simple. And those clever do everything guitars? They're hanging there, priced at two to four thousand dollars, and nobody's biting. Second hand, they often struggle to get more than half their original price. The market has spoken, and the market wants a guitar with a volume knob, a tone knob, and a pickup selector. That's it.

Number four. High end Australian made acoustics at full retail.

Now, I need to be careful here, because I love Australian guitars. I think brands like Maton and Cole Clark make some of the best acoustics in the world, and small builders like Gallin Guitars and plenty of other local luthiers are producing instruments that would stand up next to anything coming out of the US or Europe. This isn't a quality problem. It's a price and timing problem.

Top end Australian made acoustics, the ones sitting at four, five, six thousand dollars and up, are taking longer to sell at full retail than they used to. Shop staff in a couple of states told me the same thing. People come in, they play the higher end models, they absolutely love them, and then they go home and think about it. And in 2026, with mortgages where they are, a lot of them think about it and decide to wait.

Meanwhile, the second hand market for Australian acoustics is surprisingly healthy, which sounds like a contradiction, but it isn't. A well looked after Maton or Cole Clark often holds its value better than a lot of imported acoustics in the same price bracket. So what happens is buyers skip the brand new top shelf model and grab a two or three year old one on Marketplace for a decent chunk less. The guitar still sells. Just not off the shop wall at full price. And for the shop, that's the same problem.

One shop owner in regional Queensland told me something I keep thinking about. He said, "I'm not competing with other shops anymore. I'm competing with every bloke in the country who bought a Maton during lockdown and now needs the cash." That's the market right now.

Number three. Expensive relic and aged finish guitars.

You know the ones. Brand new guitars that look like they've been dragged behind a ute down a gravel road for forty years. Worn paint, fake checking in the lacquer, rusty looking hardware, all done in a factory or custom shop on purpose. At the high end, these can run anywhere from six thousand to well over twelve thousand dollars here in Australia. And for a while there, people were lining up for them.

But the relic trend has cooled off a lot. Younger players especially seem much less interested in paying a premium for fake wear. A few comments I've seen in Aussie guitar groups sum it up nicely. One guy wrote, "Why would I pay extra for someone else to bash my guitar? I can do that myself at gigs for free." And honestly, he's got a point.

The bigger issue is resale. A heavily relic'd guitar is a very personal taste. If you love the look, great. But when you go to sell it, you're only selling to the slice of buyers who also love that exact look. Dealers say that slice has shrunk. I've seen expensive relic guitars listed second hand in Australia at thirty to forty five percent under their original price, and some of them sit there for months. That's a lot of money lost on a finish that was designed to look old, and now, ironically, actually feels a bit dated.

Number two. Imported premium electrics priced off the US dollar.

This one's all about the exchange rate, and it's hurting shops more than almost anything else on this list. A lot of premium electric guitars are priced in America in US dollars. When that price gets converted to Aussie dollars, then you add international freight, then GST, then the distributor's margin, then the shop's margin, you end up with a guitar that might be two thousand five hundred US dollars over there and somewhere around five to six thousand Aussie dollars here. Sometimes more.

Australian buyers aren't stupid. We can all look up the American price on our phones in about four seconds. And when someone sees that the guitar in front of them costs, in real terms, a lot more than it does for a bloke in Nashville, it leaves a bad taste. Some people buy it anyway, because they want it now and they want the warranty and the local support. But a lot of people walk.

And then there's the used market, which is where these guitars really get hit. Second hand, a lot of imported premium electrics in Australia sell for prices that track closer to the US price than the Australian retail price. So if you paid six grand new, you might find you're only getting three and a half to four grand back a year or two later, even if it's mint. Dealers know this, buyers know this, and the result is premium imported electrics hanging on walls for months.

A worker at a big Sydney store told me they've got certain premium models that move maybe once or twice a year. Not once a month. Once or twice a year. They keep them on display mostly because they make the wall look impressive. That tells you everything.

And now, number one. The guitar that's absolutely rotting on Australian shop walls in 2026.

Brand new, top tier vintage reissue electrics in the eight to fifteen thousand dollar range.

I'm talking about the dream guitars. The faithful recreations of the late fifties and early sixties classics, the ones with all the period correct details, the ones you see in magazine spreads and on the walls of every guitar shop that wants to look serious. These are, without question, some of the best production guitars on the planet. And right now, in Australia, they're among the hardest guitars to sell at full price.

Here's why. At eight, ten, twelve, fifteen thousand dollars, you're not competing with other guitars anymore. You're competing with a family holiday to Bali. With a used car. With a deposit on a house, or more realistically these days, a chunk of a deposit on a house. The pool of Australians who can drop that kind of money on a guitar was always small, and in 2026 it's smaller than it's been in years.

On top of that, the high end used market has been flooded. A lot of people bought these guitars during the boom, when interest rates were low and everyone was stuck at home with savings and not much to spend them on. Now rates are higher, budgets are tight, and those guitars are coming out of cupboards and onto Marketplace. I've seen top tier reissues listed second hand in Australia at a third off their original shop price, sometimes even more, in near mint condition with case candy and paperwork. So imagine you're a shop with a brand new one on the wall at full retail. A customer comes in, plays it, falls in love, and then finds an almost identical one on their phone for four or five grand less. What's the shop supposed to do? Some are discounting heavily. Some are just letting them hang, hoping the right buyer walks in. And a few shop owners told me they've simply stopped ordering them unless a customer specifically asks and puts down a deposit.

One shop owner in Melbourne, who's been in the business more than thirty years, said something that stuck with me. He said, "The guitars haven't changed. The guitars are better than ever. It's the people who've changed. They've got less money and more choice, and they're not afraid to wait."

And that's really the theme of this whole list, isn't it? None of these nine guitars are bad. Most of them are genuinely excellent instruments. They're stuck because of price, timing, trends, and a second hand market that's absolutely bursting at the seams.

So what does this mean for you?

If you're buying, this is honestly one of the best times in years to be a guitar buyer in Australia. Those mid tier Japanese and Korean models I talked about at number six? Shops want them gone. Walk in with cash, be polite, and ask what their best price is. You'd be surprised. Those premium imported electrics and vintage reissues? Look second hand first.
