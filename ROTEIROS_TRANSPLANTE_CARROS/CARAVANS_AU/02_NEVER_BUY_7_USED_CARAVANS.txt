And for a lot of people, it is. Let me set the scene first. During the covid boom, Australians bought caravans like there was no tomorrow. Builders couldn't keep up, waiting lists blew out to well over a year, and frankly, some vans were rushed out the door faster than they should have been. New builders popped up, imports surged, and quality control across the whole industry was under enormous pressure. Now, a few years later, a lot of those vans are hitting the second hand market all at once. Industry people have described a flooded used market, especially in Queensland, and buyers have plenty to choose from. That's great. Number seven. Any van with soft floors or hidden water damage. This is the classic, and it's still the biggest killer of used caravans in this country. Water gets in through a tired window seal, a cracked roof hatch, a badly sealed light fitting, or around the edges of the front boot. It runs down inside the walls where you can't see it, and it sits there, slowly rotting the timber frame or the floor. By the time you notice a soft spot near the door or a stain in the corner of a cupboard, the damage can be serious. Repair costs can run into the thousands, and in the worst cases the van just isn't worth fixing. So here's what I do every single time. Walk every inch of the floor in socks, pressing down near the door, the shower, the sink and the corners. Open every cupboard and smell. Musty means trouble. Run your fingers along the bottom of the walls looking for bubbling. And if you're serious about the van, pay for a moisture test. A couple of hundred dollars now can save you ten grand later. If the seller won't let you test it, walk away.

Number six. Boom year vans from builders that are no longer trading. Again, I'm not naming names, and I'm not having a go at anyone, because businesses close for all sorts of reasons. But there were a number of smaller manufacturers who started up or expanded during the boom, and some of them simply aren't around anymore. When you buy one of their vans second hand, you're on your own. There's no factory warranty, no one to ring about a design fault, and getting the right replacement parts, a specific door, a window, a body panel, can turn into a real headache. And when it comes time for you to sell, the next buyer will ask the same question you should be asking now. Who built this, and are they still around? So before you buy, search the builder's name properly. Check whether they're still trading, look for owners' groups on social media, and read what actual owners say about repairs and parts. If the builder is gone and the owners' group is full of horror stories, keep scrolling.

Number five. Vans that are overweight for any sensible tow vehicle. This one is quietly catching thousands of people out. A lot of people bought big heavy vans during the boom, then found out at a weighbridge that their rig was over its limits once it was loaded. Some have had accessories added after the fact, a second battery, a bigger water tank, a generator box, a bike rack on the back bar, and every one of those adds weight, often right at the back where it upsets the balance. When you buy that van used, you inherit the whole problem. So check the compliance plate for the tare, the ball weight and the aggregate trailer mass. Then ask, has anything been added since it left the factory? Has it been weighed recently? If the seller can show you a weighbridge ticket, great. If not, and the van looks loaded up with heavy extras, assume the numbers are worse than the plate says. Being overweight isn't just about the law. It's about how the rig handles in a crosswind on the Bruce or when a road train blasts past you on the Stuart. It's about brakes, tyres, and in the worst case, your insurance. Don't buy someone else's overweight van.

Number four. Off road vans that have actually been used off road, hard, and not looked after. Now, this might sound strange, because I've said before that a lot of off road vans never leave the bitumen. But the ones that have done the Gibb, the Tanami and the Cape, again and again, without proper maintenance, can be a nightmare. Corrugations shake everything loose. Cabinetry rattles apart, hinges crack, wiring rubs through, suspension bushes wear out, chassis welds can develop cracks, and bulldust gets into absolutely everything. Some owners look after their vans like a newborn baby. Others just keep driving. So if you're looking at a used off road van, get under it with a torch. Check the chassis, the suspension mounts, the hitch and the drawbar for cracks or fresh welds. Open every drawer and cupboard and look at the joins. Look for red dust in places it shouldn't be, behind the fridge, inside the electrics. And ask for a service history. A well maintained off road van can be a fantastic buy. A thrashed one is an expensive restoration project.

Number three. Vans with dodgy aftermarket electrical work. This is a big one heading into twenty twenty seven, because so many owners have upgraded their power systems over the last few years. Lithium batteries, bigger solar setups, DC to DC chargers, inverters, the works. Done properly by a qualified auto electrician, these upgrades are brilliant. Done badly by someone watching videos in the shed, they can be dangerous. Undersized cables, missing fuses, poor connections, batteries mounted in the wrong spot, and in the worst cases, a real fire risk. And because the wiring is hidden behind panels, most buyers never see it. So ask who did the electrical work, and ask for paperwork. If the answer is, oh, my brother in law did it, he's pretty handy, that's your cue to get it checked by a professional before you buy. Look at the battery compartment. Is it neat, labelled and properly fused, or is it a bird's nest of wires and electrical tape? You can tell a lot from that one little compartment.

Number two. Vans with an unclear history or no paperwork at all. This one sounds boring, but it matters more than people think. Before you buy any used van, check the registration and run a check on the national register for money owing and stolen status, so you don't end up paying off someone else's loan, and with so many boom year vans financed over long terms, that's a real risk right now. Make sure the VIN on the compliance plate matches the paperwork. Ask for service records, especially brakes, bearings and the gas system. Ask whether the gas has had a compliance check, because in a lot of states you'll want that certificate in hand. And be wary of a seller who's in a big hurry, wants cash only and can't quite explain where the van has been. A genuine seller with nothing to hide will usually have a folder of receipts and be happy to show you. Someone who says, mate, it's all good, trust me, is someone you shouldn't trust.

And number one. The one I see people fall for over and over again. The big, glamorous, fully loaded luxury van from the boom years that's been sitting unused for two or three years. It looks perfect. Low kilometres, barely a mark on it, still has the plastic on the mattress in some cases. The ad says, as new, hardly used. And grey nomads see that and think, what a find. But here's the problem. Caravans hate sitting still. Tyres go hard and crack from age even if they've got plenty of tread. Wheel bearings and brakes can seize or corrode. Seals dry out and start to let water in. Batteries die after being left flat. Fridges, water pumps and hot water systems can fail after long periods without use. Rodents can move in and chew through wiring. And the gas system needs checking before it's safe. So that as new van might actually need new tyres, new bearings, brake work, new batteries, resealing and a fridge repair before it's safe for the lap. That can add up to many thousands of dollars on top of the purchase price. And because these were expensive vans to begin with, the parts and repairs aren't cheap either. I'm not saying never buy a low kilometre van. I'm saying don't let low kilometres fool you. A van that's done twenty thousand kilometres over three years and been serviced regularly is often a much better buy than one that's done two thousand and sat under a tree.

So let's talk about what you should do instead, because I don't want you leaving this video scared of buying used. Honestly, the used market right now is full of great vans, and if you're careful, you can save a fortune compared with buying new. Here's my checklist.

First, always get a professional inspection on any van you're serious about. There are mobile caravan inspectors in most parts of the country now, and they'll check the chassis, the electrics, the gas, the water, the seals and the running gear. It might cost a few hundred dollars, but it's the best money you'll spend. Use their report to negotiate. If they find two thousand dollars of work, that's two thousand dollars off the price.

Second, check the tyres' date codes. The number on the sidewall tells you the week and year the tyre was made. If the tyres are more than five or six years old, budget for new ones, no matter how much tread is left.

Third, take it to a weighbridge if you can, or at least ask the seller for a recent weighbridge ticket. Know exactly what you're buying and whether your tow vehicle can handle it safely and legally.

Fourth, do the paperwork properly. Registration, the money owing and stolen check, gas compliance, service records, and the manufacturer's details. Five minutes on your phone can save you from a disaster.

Fifth, look past the cosmetics. A shiny van with a beautiful interior can still be rotten underneath, and a van with a few scratches and dents might be mechanically perfect. Focus on the floor, the walls, the chassis and the running gear first, and the cushions last.

Sixth, take your time. In the boom, you had to decide on the spot, because someone else would buy it. Not anymore. There are plenty of vans out there, and sellers are much more willing to negotiate. If something feels off, trust your gut and keep looking.

And seventh, think about where you're actually going. If you're mostly going to stay in caravan parks along the coast, you don't need a heavy off road van. If you're dreaming of the Kimberley and the Cape, you need something properly built for it and properly maintained. Buy the van that suits the trip you'll really do, not the trip you see in someone else's photos.

Before we wrap up, let me give you the questions I always ask a private seller, because the way someone answers tells you almost as much as the inspection does. Grab a pen, or just screenshot this bit.

Why are you selling? There's no wrong answer, but listen carefully. Health reasons, a change of plans, upgrading to something bigger, all completely normal. But if the story keeps changing, or they get a bit cagey, that's worth noting.

Where has it been? Has it done the Gibb, the Savannah Way, the Cape, or has it mostly lived on powered sites along the coast? Neither answer is bad, but it tells you what to look for. A coastal van means you check for salt and corrosion. An outback van means you check for dust, corrugation damage and cracks.

How has it been stored? Under a roof, under a cover, or out in the open at the back of a paddock? Years in the full Queensland sun do terrible things to seals, decals, plastics and canvas. A van stored under a proper shed is worth more, full stop.

When were the bearings last serviced, and when were the tyres last replaced? Most owners who look after their van will know this straight away, and they'll often have the receipt. If they look at you blankly, assume nothing has been done, and price it in.

Has anything been modified or added? Solar, batteries, an extra water tank, a bigger awning, a generator box. Every one of those is fine if it's been done properly, but every one of them adds weight and can add risk.

Has it ever leaked, or been in any accident or insurance claim? People don't always volunteer this, but a surprising number will tell you the truth if you ask directly and politely. And a leak that was found early and fixed properly by a professional isn't necessarily a deal breaker. It's the hidden ones you need to worry about.

And finally, can I take it for a tow, and can I bring an inspector? A genuine seller will say yes to both. If the answer is no to either, I'd be very cautious. Towing it, even just around the block, tells you how it sits, whether the brakes pull to one side, and whether anything rattles or bangs. And an inspector sees things you and I simply won't.

I'll also say this about online listings. Photos can be very flattering. Wide angle lenses make vans look bigger, and a good clean and some fresh cushions can hide a lot. Never buy a van sight unseen, no matter how good the price is, and be extra careful with anything that looks too cheap compared with similar vans.
