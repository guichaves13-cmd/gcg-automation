Rows and rows of brand new vans, white walls shining in the sun, little price flags flapping in the wind. And here's the thing nobody in the industry wants to say out loud. A lot of those vans have been sitting there for months. Some of them for the better part of a year. The salesman will tell you it's a buyer's market and he's not wrong, but he won't tell you which vans are the ones he's desperate to get rid of. So that's what we're going to do today. Now before we start, a bit of context, because this didn't come out of nowhere. Back in the covid years, the caravan market went absolutely mad. Borders were shut, nobody could fly to Bali, and suddenly every second family in the suburbs decided they were going to do the lap. Waiting lists for some builders stretched out twelve, eighteen, even twenty four months. People were paying deposits on vans that hadn't even been built yet, and second hand vans were selling for more than they cost new. I remember a bloke at a caravan park in Kalbarri telling me he sold his three year old van for eight grand more than he paid for it. He thought he was a genius. And for about five minutes, he was.

Then it all turned around. Interest rates went up, the cost of living bit hard, fuel prices kept climbing, and all those vans bought in the boom started coming back onto the market at the same time. Industry figures have shown towable production easing year on year, imports from China turning up in bigger numbers at every show, and one report had new RV sales down by around fourteen and a half percent in January compared with a year earlier. Caravan Industry Victoria has talked openly about local sales dropping right off, with higher prices and a flooded second hand market. Industry leaders call it a correction rather than a crisis, and that's fair enough. But a correction still means vans sitting on yards. So let's go through them, from number ten down to number one.

Number ten. The huge twenty four foot tandem family bunk van. You know the one. Triple bunks across the back, an ensuite, a full kitchen, enough room to sleep six. In twenty twenty one these things were gold. Every young family wanted one, and they were towing them behind a brand new dual cab ute on finance. Fast forward to now, and those same families have done one or two trips, realised the kids are getting older, and the van spends most of the year parked down the side of the house under a cover. Dealers say they're getting a steady stream of these coming back in as trade ins, and there just aren't enough young families with the spare cash to take them. When a new one sticks around seventy, eighty, ninety thousand dollars, and a two year old one is sitting next to it for a good chunk less, the new one doesn't move. If you're a family buyer, this is actually good news. You can pick up a lightly used bunk van for a lot less than it cost a couple of years ago. Just make sure your tow vehicle can genuinely handle it, which brings me to the next one.

Number nine. Anything over about three and a half tonnes loaded. This is a quiet one, but it's real. For years the big trend was bigger, heavier, more luxurious. Then people started actually weighing their rigs. Weighbridge days at caravan shows have scared plenty of owners, because a lot of rigs turn out to be over their limits once you add water, gas, food, the generator and all the gear. And the new generation of tow vehicles, including some of the hybrids and the electric utes people are curious about, simply don't have the towing grunt or the range to drag a three and a half tonne house down the highway. So who buys the really heavy vans now? A smaller and smaller group of people with a big diesel four wheel drive and the confidence to tow it. Dealers say these big heavy vans take noticeably longer to sell, and the discounts on the yard are getting bigger. If you're tempted, weigh everything, and I mean everything, before you sign anything.

Number eight. The so called extreme off road van that never leaves the bitumen. This one makes me laugh a little, because I've been guilty of wanting one myself. Big chunky tyres, independent suspension, checker plate everywhere, raised chassis, a name that sounds like a military vehicle. They look incredible parked in the driveway. But here's the reality. Most of them spend their whole lives going from a suburban driveway to a powered site at a big caravan park with a jumping pillow. And people have worked that out. Why pay a big premium for off road gear you'll never use, and then cop the extra weight and the worse fuel economy on every single trip? Dealers say buyers are now walking straight past the hardcore off road models and asking for semi off road or plain touring vans instead. So the extreme models sit there, and the price tags quietly drop. Now, if you genuinely do the Gibb River Road or the Cape, a proper off road van is still worth every cent. But if you're honest with yourself and you mostly stick to sealed roads, don't pay for what you won't use.

Number seven. Vans with gimmicky tech that's already out of date. A few years ago, every builder was racing to add the newest gadget. App controlled everything, fancy touch screens, smart monitoring systems, all sorts. The problem is, tech ages fast, and caravans don't get software updates the way your phone does. Buyers are now looking at vans from just a few years back with a control panel that's no longer supported, or an app that hasn't been updated, and thinking, what happens if that breaks in the middle of the Kimberley? Dealers report that plain, simple, reliable systems are what grey nomads actually want. A good battery setup, decent solar, a gauge you can read without your glasses, and a switch that works. The flashy stuff? It's turning into a reason not to buy.

Number six. Imported budget vans from brands nobody has heard of. Now I want to be careful here, because I'm not saying every imported van is bad. Some of them are well made and great value. But there's been a flood of new names in the last couple of years, and buyers are getting nervous. The questions are always the same. Who fixes it if something goes wrong in Broome? Will the company still be around in five years? Can I get a replacement part for that door or that hinge? And when it comes time to sell, will anyone actually want it? Dealers say some of these lesser known imports are sitting longer, even with really attractive price tags, because the resale question hangs over them. There's also a big shift going the other way, with a lot of buyers saying they'd rather pay a bit more for something built locally, with a warranty network they understand. My advice, if you're looking at a cheaper import, ask hard questions about service agents, parts and warranty, and get the answers in writing.

Number five. Pop tops from the boom years with tired canvas and seals. Pop tops were huge with retirees, because they're lighter, they fit in the garage, and they're easier to tow. But a lot of pop tops bought cheaply during the boom are now coming back onto the market with sun damaged canvas, tired seals and the odd bit of mould. Buyers are cautious, and rightly so, because fixing a canvas roof or tracking down a leak isn't cheap. So you've got two problems at once. Used pop tops in average condition are hard to shift, and new pop tops are competing against hard top vans and compact campers that do the same job. If you're looking at one, take a torch, check every corner of the canvas and the roof seals, and push your nose right up into the cupboards for any musty smell. That smell tells you more than the salesman will.

Number four. Vans bought on long finance that are now worth less than the loan. This isn't really one model, it's a whole category of vans, but it's one of the biggest reasons the yards are full. During the boom, plenty of people bought vans on seven, ten, even fifteen year loans. And of course prices have come down, so lots of owners now owe more than the van is worth. They can't sell privately without paying the difference, so they trade it in, and the dealer is stuck holding stock that won't move at the price they need. Dealers say they've seen more of these trade ins in the last eighteen months than ever before. If you're buying, this is where the real bargains are, because those dealers want them gone. If you're the one with the loan, don't panic. Talk to your lender, do the maths, and sometimes the smartest thing is just to keep the van and use it.

Number three. Full size luxury fifth wheelers. Oh, these are beautiful. Slide outs, big lounge, a proper bedroom, a washing machine, the lot. They're the closest thing to a house on wheels you can tow. But the market for them in Australia has always been small, and it's getting smaller. You need a big ute or a converted truck to pull one, you need somewhere to store it, and a lot of caravan parks simply can't fit them on a standard site. Dealers say these are among the slowest moving vans in the country, and the ones that do sell are often going for a fair bit under what the original owner was hoping for. If you've always dreamed of one, the next twelve months might be your moment. But be honest about where you'll actually take it, because if half the parks you want to stay at can't fit you in, that dream gets frustrating very quickly.

Number two. Brand new twenty twenty five plated stock. Here's one a lot of people don't think about. As soon as the calendar ticks over, the previous year's vans become old stock, even if nobody has slept in them. Buyers don't want a van that's already a year old on paper, because it hurts resale down the track. So dealers end up with brand new vans from last year's build that are effectively getting older every day they sit there. And with the slower market in twenty twenty six, there's more of this old stock around than usual. Dealers say they're willing to talk, really willing to talk, on these vans. Sometimes it's a cash discount, sometimes it's a big list of free extras, things like solar panels, an annex, a weight distribution hitch or a few years of extended warranty. If you don't care about the build plate, this is one of the easiest ways to save serious money. Walk in, ask straight out, which vans on the yard are last year's stock, and watch the salesman's face change.

And number one. The one that surprised me most. The mid range, mid size tandem touring van from a well known builder. Not the cheap import. Not the luxury fifth wheeler. The bread and butter van, the nineteen to twenty one footer with a queen bed, a small ensuite and a decent kitchen, priced somewhere around the high fifties to the high eighties. For years this was the safest bet in the whole industry. It was what every grey nomad couple wanted, and it practically sold itself. So why is it sitting on the yards now? Because it's caught right in the middle of everything. On one side, there are thousands of two to four year old versions of exactly the same van coming back onto the second hand market, often for twenty or thirty thousand dollars less. On the other side, there are cheaper imports and new compact campers grabbing the budget buyers. And at the top, the people with real money are going for the premium brands or a motorhome. So the honest middle of the market, the van that used to be the sure thing, is now the one fighting hardest for every sale. Dealers say they're seeing more people come in, walk around these vans, nod, and then go home and buy a used one off Marketplace instead.

And that, more than anything else, tells you where the market is. It's not that Aussies have fallen out of love with caravanning. Not at all. The caravan parks are still busy, the lap is still on the bucket list, and grey nomads are still out there in their thousands. It's that people have become far smarter buyers. They're weighing their rigs, asking about warranties, checking resale and doing their homework before they hand over the cash.

So what should you actually do with all this? If you're buying, this is the best time in years. Take your time, look at used and new side by side, ask about old stock, and don't be afraid to walk away. If you're selling, be realistic, price it fairly, present it beautifully, and understand that the boom prices are gone. And if you're happy with the van you've got, here's my honest opinion. Keep it, maintain it, and go and use it, because the best van in the world is the one that's actually on the road, not the one sitting in the driveway or rotting on a dealer yard.

Now, because a few of you will be heading out to a dealer this weekend, let me give you my practical game plan for walking onto a full yard in twenty twenty six. These are the things I'd do myself, and they've come from years of chatting to owners at camp kitchens and campfires right around the country.

First, do your homework before you set foot on the yard. Spend a few nights on the big listing sites and on Marketplace looking at the exact model you're interested in, both new and used. Write down what two, three and four year old versions are actually listed for. When you walk in knowing that a near identical used van is sitting on someone's front lawn for thirty grand less, the conversation with the salesman changes completely. He knows you're not a soft target.

Second, go on a weekday, ideally late in the month. Weekends are busy, and the salesman has three other couples waiting. On a quiet Tuesday afternoon, towards the end of the month when there are targets to hit, you get his full attention, and usually a far more flexible attitude. It's not a guarantee, but dealers I've spoken to over the years quietly admit it makes a difference.

Third, ask for the total drive away price, in writing, with every extra listed. Don't get distracted by the monthly repayment. A finance figure can make almost anything look affordable, and that's exactly how a lot of people ended up owing more than their van was worth in the first place. Get the real number, take it home, sleep on it.

Fourth, check the compliance plate and the weights yourself. Look at the tare, the aggregate trailer mass and the ball weight, then add what you'll realistically carry. Water tanks, gas bottles, food, clothes, the generator, the bikes on the back, the fishing gear. Then compare that with what your tow vehicle can legally and safely handle. If the numbers are tight on the yard, they'll be a nightmare on the Hume.

Fifth, never be afraid to walk away. In the boom years, if you walked away, someone else bought the van ten minutes later. That's not the market anymore. Right now, walking away politely, leaving your number, and saying you'll think about it is often the thing that gets you the best deal. I've lost count of how many people have told me the phone rang two days later with a better offer.

And sixth, for the sellers out there, the same rules apply in reverse. Clean it like you're selling a house. Fix the little things, the sticky drawer, the cracked hatch, the flat battery. Get a weighbridge ticket and a service history ready to show. And price it against what's genuinely selling, not what your neighbour got in twenty twenty one, because those days are gone.
